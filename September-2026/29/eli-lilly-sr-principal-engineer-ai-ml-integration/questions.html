Forcing questions for the recruiter or the hiring manager

A forcing question asks for a specific answer, so that the answer settles something. These come from the research; they are not interview preparation, which waits until he asks for it. Q1 to Q4 suit the recruiter; Q5 to Q9 suit the hiring manager (Q stands for question). Ask them as curiosity, never as a challenge.

If the call runs short, get Q1, Q2 and Q5: which team, what level, and what he would own first.

Q1 Which team is this?

“Which team is this Sr. Principal seat in: Lilly USA commercial technology, the Customer Insights & Analytics AI engineering team, or a central Tech@Lilly architecture group? And who would I report to, and where are they based?”

Good answer: a named team, a named manager and the city they sit in. Weak answer: “Tech@Lilly”, with nothing more specific.

The JD names no team, and Lilly’s Bengaluru office hosts commercial, clinical, regulatory and cybersecurity technology teams (research section 4). The daily work, the systems and the stakeholders differ a great deal between them.

Q2 What level is it?

“How is this seat levelled on Lilly’s engineering ladder? Is it R4, like the Senior Principal Machine Learning Engineer role you have open, or banded across levels?”

Good answer: a stated level, and how it compares with the Technical Lead Architect and Advisor roles. Weak answer: “The level is decided after the interviews.”

Lilly’s other Senior Principal posting says R4 and asks for 11–15 years; this one asks for 8+ and states no level (research section 5). The title is a Principal-family title, which fits his target; the level decides whether the seat matches it.

Q3 Is it on-site every day?

“LinkedIn lists the role as on-site at Embassy Tech Village. How many days a week in the office, and is there any on-call or shift duty for this seat?”

Good answer: a stated number of days, and a clear yes or no on on-call. Weak answer: “It depends on the project.”

The posting is silent on both, while two Hyderabad engineering postings at this level (Senior Principal and Principal Agentic Engineer) and one operations posting ask for some weekend and shift work (research section 10). Six- or seven-day weeks are fine for him; 24/7 on-call is worth knowing up front.

Q4 What does the process look like?

“What are the interview rounds for this seat, and who is on the panel?”

Good answer: a list of rounds with rough dates, including an architecture or system-design round. Weak answer: no clear steps.

The posting closes on 28 October 2026 and had 106 applicants within about five days (research section 6). Knowing the steps lets him plan, and tells him when the interview preparation should start.

Q5 What would he own first?

“In the first six months, which systems would this role integrate? For example, the field assistant, the move to Vault CRM, marketing systems, or the agentic AI platform?”

Good answer: one or two named programmes with a clear owner and a date. Weak answer: “All of AI integration.”

Lilly has several live commercial AI and CRM programmes (research section 8); the JD does not say which one this seat serves. A named programme also tells him which part of his record to lead with.

Q6 How hands-on is the seat?

“How would you split this role’s week between designing and building, and reviews, standards and vendor governance? Does it write reference implementations or proofs of concept?”

Good answer: a rough split, with examples of things the role builds itself. Weak answer: “It’s mostly strategy.”

The JD is written around strategy, standards, reviews and vendors. A sibling architect posting insists “This is not a slide-deck architecture role”; this one does not say (research section 10).

Q7 Which cloud and AI platforms?

“The job description mentions Azure, Azure OpenAI, AWS and Databricks. Which of these does this team actually run on today, and which large language models do you use in production?”

Good answer: a named stack. Weak answer: “We are open to anything.”

Lilly’s Bengaluru AI engineering postings name AWS and Databricks; no public Lilly source mentions Azure or Azure OpenAI (research section 7). The answer shows what to learn first.

Q8 How is AI governed?

“How does an AI feature get approved for production here: who reviews privacy, compliance and responsible-AI questions, and how are models evaluated before and after release?”

Good answer: a named review board or process, and an evaluation practice. Weak answer: “Each team decides.”

The JD asks the seat to “Establish end-to-end testing, monitoring, observability, and AI evaluation practices” and to promote Responsible AI; whether that means building the process from scratch or joining an existing one changes the job (research section 9).

Q9 Who are the stakeholders in the US?

“Which US teams would I work with most, and how much of the day overlaps with Indianapolis time?”

Good answer: named partner teams and a stated overlap window. Weak answer: “Everyone.”

The likely customers are Lilly USA’s field, marketing and market-access teams (inference, research section 5). Indianapolis is 9.5 hours behind India for much of the year, so late-evening calls may be part of the work.
