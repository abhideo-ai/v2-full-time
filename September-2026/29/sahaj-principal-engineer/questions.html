Sahaj Software: open questions

What is still open, and who can settle it

The research could not settle these from public sources. None of them blocks sending the application; they matter for deciding how much the seat suits him once Sahaj replies. The forcing questions in the next fold turn them into questions he can ask.

  1. Which client project would he join first? The posting lists a “menu” of four kinds of work that “changes every quarter” and says “We can’t promise which problem you’ll pick up” (research section 3). Who can settle it: the recruiter in outline, the Hyderabad office lead or the first interviewer in detail.
  2. What does “On-site” mean for Hyderabad? LinkedIn says on-site; employees report two or three office days a week, and Sahaj’s own book describes rolling back a three-day office rule (research section 4). Who can settle it: the recruiter.
  3. How often would he be at a client’s site, and would that mean travel? Most of Sahaj’s delivery is from its own offices, but some projects have put people on-site with clients, including abroad. No source gives a cadence. Who can settle it: the recruiter, then the office lead.
  4. Is there on-call or out-of-hours support? No source mentions it; it probably depends on the client’s contract. Who can settle it: the first technical interviewer.
  5. How would a principal-level engineer be recognised with no grades? Everyone is a “Solution Consultant”, there are no front-line managers, and reviewers name career growth as the main weakness (research section 4; flags). Who can settle it: the recruiter for the process, a senior Solution Consultant for how it works in practice.
  6. What title would the offer letter carry? The posting calls “Principal Engineer” “the translation for the outside world”. Who can settle it: the recruiter.
  7. Is this seat the same pool as Sahaj’s “Senior and Lead Engineer” posting? Both ask for 8+ years. Who can settle it: the recruiter.
  8. How big is the Hyderabad office today, and who leads it? The last public account (February 2025) described a seed team of six in a co-working space (research section 2). Who can settle it: the recruiter.
  9. What does the hiring process look like? The engineer who set up Sahaj’s Hyderabad office has written that Sahaj prefers a conversation to an interview; the steps for this seat are not public. Who can settle it: the recruiter.
Forcing questions for the recruiter or the hiring manager

A forcing question asks for a specific answer, so that the answer settles something. These come from the research; they are not interview preparation, which waits until he asks for it. Q1 to Q5 suit the recruiter (Sahaj calls its recruiters “Org Builders”); Q6 to Q10 suit a senior Solution Consultant or the Hyderabad office lead (Q stands for question). Ask them as curiosity, never as a challenge.

If the call runs short, get Q2, Q1, Q3 and Q6: what “on-site” means, which project, how the seat is placed, and how depth is recognised.

Q1 Which project first?

“The posting lists four kinds of work. For someone joining in Hyderabad now, which client engagement would I most likely start on, and how long is it expected to run?”

Good answer: a named kind of engagement (data platform, streaming, a rebuild) with a rough length. Weak answer: “we’ll see when you join”, with no current demand in view.

Settles open item 1. In a consultancy the project is the job, and a vague answer can also mean there is no project waiting, which leads to time between engagements (research section 2).

Q2 What does “on-site” mean?

“LinkedIn marks the role on-site. How many days a week are people in the Hyderabad office, and is that set by Sahaj or by the client?”

Good answer: a clear number and who decides it. Weak answer: “it depends”, with no example.

Settles open item 2. The posting and employee reviews disagree, and the answer shapes his week (research section 4).

Q3 How is the seat placed?

“Everyone at Sahaj is a Solution Consultant. What makes this a ‘Principal Engineer’ opening rather than the Senior and Lead Engineer one, and what title would the offer letter show?”

Good answer: a description of the scope expected (for example leading design on a client engagement) and a clear answer on the letter. Weak answer: no difference beyond the job-board title.

Settles open items 6 and 7. Both postings ask for 8+ years, and with no grades the outside title is the only marker he carries forward (research section 4).

Q4 Client sites and travel

“How often do consultants in Hyderabad work from a client’s office, in India or abroad, and for how long at a time?”

Good answer: a pattern (for example a kick-off week on site, then remote). Weak answer: no pattern, or long stays arranged at short notice.

Settles open item 3. Travel cadence is worth knowing before he commits, and no public source gives it.

Q5 The Hyderabad office

“How many Solution Consultants are in Hyderabad today, and who is the office lead I would work with?”

Good answer: a number and a name. Weak answer: the office is mostly a desk, with the team elsewhere.

Settles open item 8. The office lead helps assign people to projects, so this is the person who shapes his first months (research section 2).

Q6 How depth is recognised

“With no grades and no managers, how does Sahaj recognise someone who is trusted with the hardest design decisions? Could you give an example of how that has worked for a senior person?”

Good answer: a concrete example of scope or responsibility growing. Weak answer: only “it’s up to you”.

Settles open item 5. Career growth is the most common complaint in employee reviews (research section 6).

Q7 On-call

“On the engagements I’d likely join, does the team carry production support or on-call, and how is it shared?”

Good answer: a clear rota, or none. Weak answer: “whoever is around”.

Settles open item 4. Nothing public mentions it.

Q8 The streaming work

“The posting mentions a vehicle-telemetry platform on Kafka. Is that a live engagement, and is it staffed from Hyderabad?”

Good answer: yes or no, and which office. Weak answer: the example is historical.

The published vehicle work does not show the “billions of messages a day” or the Kafka detail; the answer shows whether the menu is current (research section 3).

Q9 Languages in practice

“Across current engagements, which two or three languages do most teams actually write in?”

Good answer: a short, specific list. Weak answer: the full list of ten, repeated.

The posting lists ten languages as “context, not a checklist”; the published case studies lean on Java and Spring Boot. The answer tells him where the ramp-up would be.

Q10 Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted delivery

“Sahaj offers intelligent Engineering to clients. Would a principal-level consultant be expected to lead that kind of work on engagements?”

Good answer: a clear yes or no, with an example. Weak answer: unclear.

The posting does not mention AI, but Sahaj’s current offering and recent posts put AI-assisted delivery at the front (research section 2).
