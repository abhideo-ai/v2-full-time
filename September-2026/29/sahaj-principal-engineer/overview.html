Glossary: every acronym and unfamiliar term, expanded

This seat is at a consultancy whose work is data-heavy distributed systems: large analytical databases, streams of messages, and platforms that serve many clients at once. Each entry says what the thing is and why it matters for this seat. The full list of acronyms, on every page of this workspace, is at the end.

Columnar analytics databases (the ClickHouse example)

ClickHouse
An open-source database built for analytics: it answers questions over billions of rows quickly by storing data column by column. Why it matters: the posting’s first example is redesigning a ClickHouse schema, and a Sahaj engineer has written about exactly that work.
OLAP and OLTP
OLAP (online analytical processing) means reading and summarising large amounts of data, as in reports and dashboards. OLTP (online transaction processing) means many small reads and writes, as in placing an order. Why it matters: ClickHouse is an OLAP database, and its design rules differ from the OLTP databases most application code uses.
Columnar storage
Keeping each column’s values together on disk, so a query that needs three columns reads only those three, and similar values compress well. Why it matters: it is why ClickHouse is fast, and why a table with thousands of columns becomes slow, which is the lesson of Sahaj’s post.
Schema, and the grain of a table
A schema is the shape of the data: its tables and columns. The grain is what one row stands for (one billboard, or one billboard in one hour). Why it matters: Sahaj’s post concludes that the schema’s grain should match the grain of the query.
Pre-exploding rows (denormalising)
Storing data already broken out into the rows a query needs, instead of computing that at query time; it uses more storage to get faster, steadier queries. In Sahaj’s post each billboard became 168 rows, one per hour of the week. Why it matters: it is the “168x” in the posting.
Sort key (ORDER BY) and partitioning
In ClickHouse the sort key decides the order rows are stored in, so queries that filter on it skip most of the data; partitioning splits a table into large chunks, often by date. Why it matters: these two choices decide most of a ClickHouse table’s speed.
Materialized view
A stored, automatically updated result of a query, used to pre-compute summaries. Why it matters: a common alternative to pre-exploding rows, likely to come up in any ClickHouse design discussion.
Low cardinality
A column with few distinct values (such as day of the week). ClickHouse has a special type for it that compresses well. Why it matters: Sahaj’s post uses it to explain why the extra rows cost less storage than expected.
P50 and P95 latency
The response time that half, or 95 per cent, of requests beat. Why it matters: “under two seconds” is only meaningful once you say for which share of queries.

Advertising words in the ClickHouse example

OOH and DOOH
OOH (out-of-home) advertising is advertising seen outside the home: billboards, bus shelters, screens in malls. DOOH is its digital form, on screens. Why it matters: several of Sahaj’s published clients (Talon, Clear Channel) are in OOH, and the ClickHouse post is about DOOH screens.
Frame
In OOH, one physical advertising screen or board. Not a video frame. Why it matters: it is the main unit in the ClickHouse post (about 1.7 million frames).
Impression, audience index, daypart
An impression is one estimated viewing of an advert. An audience index compares how concentrated a target audience is at one frame against the market average (100 = average). A daypart is a slice of the day an advertiser buys, such as weekday mornings. Why it matters: these define the query that had to return within one to two seconds.
DMA
Designated Market Area: a geographic advertising market in the United States, such as the New York metro area. Why it matters: it is a filter and sort column in the ClickHouse post’s tables.
Programmatic bidding
Buying advertising space automatically, auction by auction, in milliseconds. Why it matters: Sahaj engineers have written about scaling a DOOH bidding service to about 15,000 requests a second in Java.

Streaming with Kafka (the vehicle-telemetry example)

Apache Kafka
A system for moving streams of messages between programs reliably and at high volume; producers write messages, consumers read them, and Kafka keeps them for a set time. Why it matters: the posting’s second example is keeping Kafka consumers healthy on a platform that moves billions of messages a day.
Topic and partition
A topic is a named stream of messages. It is split into partitions, which are the unit of parallel reading; message order is kept only within a partition. Why it matters: the number of partitions caps how many consumers can share the work.
Consumer group
A set of consumers that share a topic’s partitions between them, so each message is handled by one member. Why it matters: “Kafka consumers falling over” usually means a consumer group losing members, rebalancing or falling behind.
Offset and consumer lag
An offset is a message’s position in a partition; a consumer records the offset it has reached. Consumer lag is how far behind the newest message a consumer is. Why it matters: lag is the main health signal for a streaming platform.
Rebalance
Kafka reassigning partitions among a consumer group when a member joins, leaves or stops responding. Frequent rebalances pause reading. Why it matters: a classic cause of consumers “falling over” under load.
Backpressure
Slowing the intake of work when the downstream side cannot keep up, instead of crashing. Why it matters: the design answer to bursts of messages.
Delivery guarantees: at-least-once, exactly-once, idempotent
At-least-once means no message is lost but some may arrive twice; exactly-once means each takes effect once. An idempotent consumer gives the same result if a message is processed twice. Why it matters: at scale, duplicates happen, and the design must absorb them.
Dead-letter queue (DLQ)
A separate place where messages that cannot be processed are put aside, so they do not block the stream. Why it matters: a standard way to keep one bad message from stopping a consumer.
RabbitMQ
Another message broker, used for passing messages between services. Why it matters: Sahaj’s Comviva wallet platform used RabbitMQ, not Kafka.

Telemetry and connected vehicles

Telemetry
Measurements sent automatically from a device to a central system: a vehicle’s location, speed, engine health. Why it matters: a telemetry platform receives a constant, heavy stream of small messages, which is why it sits on Kafka-style streaming.
Connected vehicle platform (CVP)
A system that collects and uses data from vehicles on the road: tracking, trip history, health alerts. Why it matters: Sahaj’s published case study describes one serving 0.5 million vehicles and generating over 2 terabytes (TB) of data a day.
Telematics control unit (TCU)
The box in a vehicle that collects its data and sends it over the mobile network. Why it matters: Sahaj’s case study says its platform gathers data from carmakers’ and after-market units.
MQTT
Message Queuing Telemetry Transport: a light messaging protocol often used by devices such as vehicles to send data to a server, which may then pass it to Kafka. Why it matters: common at the edge of telemetry platforms.
Time series
Data recorded as a sequence of values over time. Why it matters: telemetry is time-series data, and its storage and queries differ from ordinary tables.
MES
Manufacturing execution system: software that runs and tracks production on a factory floor. Why it matters: Sahaj’s July 2026 case study built one for an Asian carmaker on Kafka; it is the published Kafka example closest to the vehicle work.
Lakh
An Indian number word for 100,000. Why it matters: Sahaj’s book says its connected-vehicle platform runs “on lakhs of vehicles”.

Wallets, platforms and legacy systems

Mobile wallet (mobile money)
An app, often run through a mobile network operator, that lets people store, send and pay money from a phone, often without a bank account. Why it matters: the posting’s third example; Sahaj built a platform for Comviva’s wallet used by telecom providers in Africa, the Middle East, South America and Southeast Asia.
Multi-tenant
One running system serving several customers (tenants), each seeing only its own data and settings. Why it matters: Sahaj’s wallet platform and one of its Kafka posts are both about multi-tenant design.
Strangler pattern (“strangulation approach”)
Replacing an old system piece by piece, routing one function at a time to the new system, while the old one keeps running. Why it matters: the posting says the work is “often sitting on top of something that already exists and isn’t perfect”; this is the usual way to change such systems safely.
Monolith and microservices
A monolith is one large program; microservices are many small services that talk over the network. Why it matters: Sahaj’s Comviva work moved a monolith with “more than 35 independent versions” to microservices.
Data product
A dataset, with its pipeline and quality checks, run as a product that other teams depend on. Why it matters: the posting’s fourth example is “the data product behind peer-review submissions”.
Peer review
The step in scientific publishing where independent experts assess a submitted paper before it is accepted. Why it matters: the data behind submissions and reviews is the fourth example’s domain.
Customer engagement platform (CEP); Braze
Software that sends targeted messages to customers; Braze is one. Why it matters: Sahaj’s published Springer Nature work moved author engagement to Braze.

Test-driven development, pairing and refactoring

Test-driven development (TDD)
Writing a failing automated test first, then the smallest code that makes it pass, then cleaning up: the “red, green, refactor” cycle. Why it matters: the posting names “TDD, refactoring, and continuous improvement” as tools it cares about.
Test Driven Generation (TDG)
Sahaj engineers’ 2023 name for writing tests first and letting an AI model generate the code that passes them. Why it matters: it shows TDD is part of how Sahaj thinks about AI-assisted coding. Not to be confused with TDD.
Pair programming (pairing)
Two engineers working on the same code at once: one types (the driver), the other reviews and thinks ahead (the navigator), switching often. Why it matters: the posting says “You mentor through pairing and feedback, not hierarchy”.
Refactoring
Changing the inside of code to make it clearer or easier to change, without changing what it does. Why it matters: named in the posting, and the subject of a Sahaj engineer’s post.
Continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD)
Merging and testing every change automatically, and keeping the code always ready to release. Why it matters: assumed in the TDD-and-refactoring way of working; Sahaj’s senior engineer posting mentions it.
Polyglot team
A team that works in several programming languages, picking the one that suits each problem. Why it matters: the posting lists ten languages and says “Work across languages when needed”.
SOLID, YAGNI, KISS, DRY
Design rules of thumb named in Sahaj’s TDG post. SOLID: single responsibility, open-closed, Liskov substitution, interface segregation, dependency inversion. YAGNI: you aren’t gonna need it. KISS: keep it simple. DRY: don’t repeat yourself. Why it matters: the shared vocabulary of the code-quality culture the posting describes.

The consulting model, and Sahaj’s own words

Consultancy (technology services company)
A company that builds or advises on software for other companies, who pay for its people’s time or for results. Why it matters: at Sahaj his work would be for whichever client he is placed with, not for one product of Sahaj’s own.
Engagement
One piece of client work, from start to finish; at Sahaj typically six to eighteen months. Why it matters: the project, and so the technology, can change every engagement.
Discovery
A short opening phase of an engagement for understanding the client’s problem before building. Why it matters: Sahaj’s case studies describe short discoveries and workshops at the start of work; a principal-level consultant would often lead them.
Offshore and on-site delivery
Offshore delivery is work done from the consultancy’s own offices in another country; on-site is work done at the client’s premises. Why it matters: Sahaj says it sells mainly offshore delivery; some projects still put people at the client. Not the same as LinkedIn’s “On-site” label, which is about the employer’s office.
Time between engagements (the bench)
Periods when a consultant is not on a client project. Why it matters: a Sahaj engineer writes that people use it to sharpen skills; how long it lasts depends on demand.
Solution Consultant
The single designation every Sahaj engineer holds, from graduate to the most senior. Why it matters: “Principal Engineer” is only the outside name for this seat.
Sahajeevi
Sahaj’s word for a member of the company.
Org Builder
Sahaj’s name for its recruiting and people staff. Why it matters: the job poster on LinkedIn is an Org Builder, so this is his first contact.
Office lead
A Solution Consultant who, besides project work, looks after one Sahaj office, including helping with people’s assignment to projects. Why it matters: the nearest thing to a manager in a manager-free company.
Parishad
A Hindi word for council; Sahaj’s leadership group beside the founders, including its managing director for India.
Gram Sabha
A Hindi term for a village assembly; Sahaj’s name for its company-wide town hall.
DevDay
Sahaj’s community meetup series for engineers, held in its offices, including a monthly Hyderabad chapter.
intelligent Engineering
Sahaj’s name (with a lower-case “i”) for its offering that builds AI tools into every stage of a client’s software delivery. Why it matters: the posting does not mention AI, but this is where Sahaj is putting its current effort.
Individual contributor (IC)
A senior engineer who leads through technical work rather than by managing people. Why it matters: this seat is one, which is what he is looking for.
Easy Apply
LinkedIn’s one-click application, which sends a LinkedIn profile and an uploaded résumé.
