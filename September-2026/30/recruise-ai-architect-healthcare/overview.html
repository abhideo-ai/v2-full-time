Glossary: every acronym and unfamiliar term, expanded

Three fields meet in this seat: recruiting through a search firm, platforms for large language models, and healthcare with its rules. The recruiter, the client and the search

Recruise
The trading name of Recruise India Consulting Private Limited, a recruiting firm with its head office in Bengaluru. It posted this search. It is not the employer.
Recruiter, agency and search firm
A company that finds candidates for other companies. “Agency” and “search firm” mean the same thing here. Why it matters: the recruiter stands between him and the employer, and decides what the employer sees.
Client
The company that is hiring and that engaged the recruiter. In this workspace “the client” always means the unnamed employer. Why it matters: the post does not name it, so nothing about it can be checked yet.
Mandate, and open mandate
Recruise’s word for one search assignment from one client. An “open mandate” is a role it is currently filling. Its website lists 318 of them; one, number 5832, is titled “AI Architect”.
Executive search, and retained search
Executive search is recruiting for senior roles, usually by approaching people directly. In a retained search the client engages a firm in advance to run the search, usually one firm alone. Recruise describes its work as retained search.
Confidential search
A search where the client’s name is held back from candidates at first. Recruise says most of its searches are run this way.
Submission
The moment a recruiter sends a candidate’s résumé to the client. Why it matters: after a submission the candidate is recorded in the client’s hiring system as coming through that recruiter.
Consent, per submission
The candidate’s agreement, given each time, before his résumé goes to a particular client for a particular role. Recruise’s website promises it; asking for it in writing makes it a record.
Exclusivity, and double submission
Exclusivity means one firm alone works a search. A double submission is the same candidate reaching the same employer by two routes. Why it matters: an employer then has to decide whose candidate he is, and some drop the candidate.
Talent acquisition, and practice head
Talent acquisition is another name for recruiting. A practice head leads one line of the firm’s work; the recruiter here leads the one for product and technology roles.
Global capability centre (GCC), and recruitment process outsourcing (RPO)
A GCC is a foreign company’s own office in India that does work for the whole group. RPO means a recruiting firm runs a company’s hiring on its behalf. Both are services Recruise sells.
Individual contributor (IC)
A role that is measured on the person’s own technical work, with no team to manage. The post says “Individual Contributor / Architect”.
Big Tech
An informal phrase for the largest technology companies. It has no fixed list. The post uses it for the background it prefers in candidates; it says nothing about who the client is.
Feed post, and job listing
A feed post is an ordinary LinkedIn post that appears in people’s feeds. A job listing is a structured page with a status and an apply button. This search is a feed post, so there is no status to check.
1st-degree connection, and mutual connections
On LinkedIn, a 1st-degree connection is someone he is directly connected to. Mutual connections are people connected to both of them. He and the recruiter have 11.
CV (curriculum vitae)
The résumé. The post asks for an “updated CV”.

AI and machine-learning platforms

Artificial intelligence (AI), and machine learning (ML)
AI is software that does tasks which normally need human judgement. ML is the main way it is built today: a program learns patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. “AI/ML” is the two written together.
Model, training and inference
A model is the trained program. Training is the process of teaching it from data. Inference, in this sense, is running the trained model to get an answer. (See Collisions: this workspace also uses “inference” as a label for our own reading of evidence.)
Platform, and AI/ML platform
A platform is a shared set of services that many applications build on. An AI/ML platform gives teams common ways to reach models, data, evaluation and monitoring. Why it matters: the seat is to design and build one.
End-to-end
Covering the whole path, from choosing a model to running it for users and improving it. The post uses the phrase twice.
Production-grade
Good enough to run for real users: reliable, secure, monitored and recoverable. The opposite of a demonstration or a pilot.
Python, and ML libraries
Python is the programming language most AI work is written in. ML libraries are ready-made packages of code for building and running models. The post asks for both and names no library.
Foundation model
A very large model trained on broad data that can be adapted to many tasks. Large language models are the best-known kind.
Cloud architecture, and the main clouds
Cloud architecture is the design of systems on rented computing from a cloud provider. The three largest are Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud. The post does not say which one the client uses.
Well-Architected Framework, and the Generative AI Lens
AWS’s published set of design principles, and its supplement for generative AI systems. The research uses the supplement as a public description of what such a platform covers.
System design
Deciding how the parts of a large software system fit together so that it is fast, reliable and affordable. The post’s last skill line.

Large language models

Large language model (LLM)
A model trained on very large amounts of text that can read and write language. Why it matters: the platform exists to support them.
Generative AI
AI that produces new content (text, images, code) instead of only classifying or predicting. LLMs are generative AI.
Large multi-modal model (LMM)
A model that takes more than one kind of input, such as text and images. The World Health Organization’s guidance uses this term.
Prompt, and context
The prompt is the text sent to a model. The context is everything the model is given to work from in one request, including any documents added to the prompt.
Embedding
A list of numbers that captures the meaning of a piece of text, so that texts with similar meaning have similar numbers. It is what makes search by meaning possible.
Confabulation, or hallucination
A model stating something false with confidence. Why it matters: in healthcare a wrong answer can harm a patient, so a platform must measure and limit it.
Groundedness
Whether a model’s answer is supported by the documents it was given. One of the measures Microsoft’s guide names for evaluating answers.
Natural language processing (NLP)
The older, broader name for software that works with human language. It appears in the title of the paper that named retrieval-augmented generation.

Retrieval-augmented generation and agentic AI

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
A technique in which the system first searches a body of documents and then gives the best matches to the model along with the question, so that the answer rests on those documents. Why it matters: it is how a model can answer from an organisation’s own data, such as clinical guidelines, without being retrained. (See Collisions.)
Chunking
Cutting documents into small passages before indexing them, so that a search returns the relevant passage and not a whole document.
Search index, and vector search
A search index is the store that makes documents searchable. Vector search finds passages by comparing embeddings, that is, by meaning instead of by exact words.
Orchestrator
The piece of software that runs the steps of a request: it searches, assembles the prompt, calls the model and returns the answer.
Agentic AI, and an AI agent
A system in which the model itself decides which steps to take and which tools to call, instead of following a fixed sequence. Why it matters: the post names it as a skill, and it raises the stakes on safety controls. (See Collisions: “agent”.)
Tool, and tool calling
A tool is a function an agent is allowed to use, such as a search or a database query. Tool calling is the model asking for one to be run.
Excessive agency
The risk that an agent is able to do more than it should. AWS’s guidance lists it among the security risks to control.

Running models in production

MLOps (machine learning operations)
Google’s definition: “an ML engineering culture and practice that aims at unifying ML system development (Dev) and ML system operation (Ops)”. In plain words, the discipline of getting models into production and keeping them working.
LLMOps (large language model operations)
The same discipline applied to LLM applications: managing prompts, evaluating answers, watching quality over time. It is a trade term with no single official definition.
Continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD)
Automation that tests every change and releases it safely. Google’s highest MLOps level automates this for the model pipelines themselves.
Pipeline
A fixed series of automated steps, for example taking in documents, chunking them, embedding them and indexing them.
Evaluation
Measuring how good a model’s answers are, against test questions and agreed measures, before and after every change.
FinOps (financial operations)
Managing what cloud systems cost to run. It appears in a role title on Recruise’s board.

Healthcare terms

Electronic health record (EHR)
The digital record of a patient’s care, kept by a hospital or clinic. Why it matters: a healthcare AI platform usually reads from, or writes to, EHR systems.
Protected health information (PHI)
The term in United States law for health information that identifies a person. Why it matters: handling it brings HIPAA duties. (See Collisions: PHI and PII.)
Personally identifiable information (PII)
Any information that identifies a person, health-related or not.
Clinical decision support (CDS)
Software that helps a clinician decide about a patient, for example by suggesting a diagnosis or a treatment. Why it matters: such software can be regulated as a medical device.
Clinician, provider and payer
A clinician is a doctor, nurse or other professional who treats patients. In healthcare a provider is a hospital, clinic or clinician who delivers care, and a payer is an insurer or health plan. (See Collisions: “provider”.)
FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources), and HL7 (Health Level Seven)
FHIR is “a standard for exchanging healthcare information electronically”. HL7 is the standards body that publishes it. Interoperability means different systems being able to exchange and use each other’s data.
ICD, SNOMED CT and LOINC
Standard code systems for healthcare data. ICD, the International Classification of Diseases, codes diagnoses. SNOMED CT is a large vocabulary of clinical terms. LOINC codes laboratory tests and other measurements. Why it matters: “healthcare-specific language” in the post may mean these.
Use case
One specific job a system does for a user, such as summarising a clinical note. The post asks for healthcare use cases and names none.

The regulatory regimes named in the research

Regime, and compliance
A regime is a body of law and rules on one subject. Compliance is the work of meeting it and being able to show so.
HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)
The United States law, with its rules, that protects patients’ health information. It applies if the platform handles American patient data.
Covered entity, and business associate
HIPAA’s two kinds of regulated party. A covered entity is a health plan, a clearinghouse or a healthcare provider that sends health information electronically. A business associate is anyone who handles PHI on a covered entity’s behalf, such as a software vendor, and must do so under a written contract.
DPDP Act (Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023), and the DPDP Rules, 2025
India’s data-protection law and the rules that put it into effect. The rules were notified in November 2025 and come into force in stages, the last in May 2027.
Data Fiduciary, and Data Principal
The DPDP Act’s words. The Data Fiduciary is the organisation that decides why and how personal data is used. The Data Principal is the person the data is about.
Notified, and the Gazette of India
In India a law or rule takes legal effect by being published in the government’s official journal, the Gazette. That publication is called being notified.
FDA (Food and Drug Administration)
The United States regulator of medicines and medical devices. Its guidance on clinical decision support software says which such software counts as a device.
Medical device, SaMD and SiMD
In law, a medical device can be software. Software as a medical device (SaMD) is standalone software with a medical purpose. Software in a medical device (SiMD) is software built into a physical device. (See Collisions.)
CDSCO (Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation), and the Medical Devices Rules, 2017
India’s regulator of medicines and medical devices, and the rules it applies to devices. In October 2025 it published draft guidance on medical device software.
EU AI Act
The European Union’s law on artificial intelligence, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. It sets the heaviest duties for “high-risk” systems. It matters only if the product is offered in the European Union.
NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology), the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), and NIST AI 600-1
NIST is a United States government standards body. Its AI RMF is a voluntary method for managing AI risk, and AI 600-1 is the companion document for generative AI.
WHO (World Health Organization), and ICMR (Indian Council of Medical Research)
The United Nations’ health agency and India’s medical research body. Each has published ethics guidance for AI in health; neither is binding law.
ABDM (Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission), and NHA (National Health Authority)
India’s national digital-health programme and the government body that runs it. Named in the research for reference only.
21st Century Cures Act
A United States law of 2016. One of its sections removed some software, including some decision-support software, from the legal definition of a medical device.
Code of Federal Regulations (CFR)
The published collection of United States federal rules. “45 CFR 160.103” is the section holding HIPAA’s definitions.
REC and AESC
The Recruitment & Employment Confederation, a British trade body for recruiters, and the Association of Executive Search and Leadership Consultants, an international one. The research quotes their codes as statements of ordinary good practice; neither binds Recruise.

Client
Here: the hiring company. In software the word also means the program that calls a server.
Provider

CV

MDR

PHI and PII

SaMD and SiMD
One letter apart. SaMD is software that is the medical device; SiMD is software inside a physical one.
LLM and LMM
LLM: large language model. LMM: large multi-modal model, the World Health Organization’s term.
GCC
Here: global capability centre. Elsewhere the letters mean the Gulf Cooperation Council or a well-known software compiler.

Here: individual contributor. In electronics: integrated circuit.
Mandate
