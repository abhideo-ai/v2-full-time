5. What the post’s requirements usually mean (general context)

General context The post gives one sentence and nine skill lines, with no detail. This section explains what such a platform usually involves, from public reference material. It describes the field, not this employer’s system, which nobody outside can see.

“An end-to-end AI platform supporting Large Language Models”

A large language model (LLM) is a model trained on very large amounts of text that can read and write language. A platform for LLMs is the shared set of services that many applications build on, so that each team does not rebuild them. Public reference architectures describe roughly the same parts:

  • The whole life cycle, not only the model. Amazon Web Services (AWS) publishes a “Generative AI Lens” for its Well-Architected Framework, which gives guidance “across the generative AI lifecycle stages of scoping, model selection, customization, development, deployment, and continuous improvement”, under six headings: operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization and sustainability (AWS, Generative AI Lens, published 19 November 2025). Inference “End-to-end” in the post most likely means this whole span.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). The technique was named in a 2020 research paper, “Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks” (NLP: natural language processing) (Lewis and others, 22 May 2020). Microsoft’s architecture guide calls it “an industry-standard approach to building applications that use language models to process specific or proprietary data that the model doesn’t already know”. It describes a data pipeline that breaks documents into chunks, enriches them, turns them into embeddings (lists of numbers that capture meaning) and stores them in a search index, and a request flow in which an orchestrator runs a search and sends the best results to the model with the question (Microsoft, RAG design and evaluation guide).
  • Agentic AI. In a standard RAG pipeline the steps are fixed in advance. The same Microsoft guide describes the agentic form: “an AI agent treats retrieval as a tool that it can invoke on demand”, for cases such as “multistep reasoning, dynamic source selection, query decomposition at runtime, and combining retrieval with actions”. The AWS lens lists “excessive agency” among the security risks to control.
  • Evaluation. Microsoft’s guide names measures for a model’s answers, “groundedness, completeness, utilization, and relevancy”, and says each step should be evaluated on its own as well as end to end.
  • Operations: MLOps and LLMOps. Google defines MLOps (machine learning operations) as “an ML engineering culture and practice that aims at unifying ML system development (Dev) and ML system operation (Ops)”, and describes three levels: a manual process, an automated training pipeline, and automated continuous integration and delivery (CI/CD) of the pipelines themselves (Google Cloud, MLOps). LLMOps is the common name for the same discipline applied to LLM applications; it is a trade term with no single official definition.
  • Risk management. The United States (US) National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published a generative AI profile of its AI Risk Management Framework on 26 July 2024, “a cross-sectoral profile of and companion resource for the AI Risk Management Framework”, which is “intended for voluntary use” (NIST AI 600-1). Its list of twelve risks includes confabulation (a model stating false things confidently), data privacy and information security (NIST AI 600-1, section 2).

“Healthcare-specific language & use cases”

  • Data exchange. Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) is, in its own words, “a standard for exchanging healthcare information electronically”, published by Health Level Seven (HL7), a standards body (HL7, FHIR overview). A healthcare AI platform usually has to read from, or write to, systems that speak it, such as electronic health record (EHR) systems.
  • Clinical vocabularies. Healthcare data is coded with standard vocabularies: the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), kept by the World Health Organization (WHO, ICD); SNOMED CT, a clinical terminology (SNOMED International); and LOINC, “a common language (a set of identifiers, names, and codes) for identifying health measurements, observations, and documents” (LOINC). Inference “Healthcare-specific language” in the post may mean these vocabularies, or clinical text in general, or simply familiarity with the domain. The post does not say.
  • Use cases. The post names none. Inference Which use cases the platform serves (for example summarising clinical notes, answering questions over medical documents, helping with coding and billing, or supporting a clinician’s decision) decides which rules in section 6 apply, so it is one of the first things to ask (forcing questions).

7. Sending a résumé to a recruiter by email: what to check first

Neutral practice, briefly. The post asks for a CV by email. Three things are worth settling before a résumé travels further than the recruiter.

  1. Who the client is, before the résumé goes onward. It is normal for a search firm to hold the name back at first. The Candidate Bill of Rights of the Association of Executive Search and Leadership Consultants (AESC) says “At the appropriate time in the process, the consultant will also identify the client organization” Independent (AESC). Recruise’s own mandate page says the client “is shared once we’re in conversation” Self-claim.
  2. Consent for each submission. The code of the United Kingdom’s (UK) Recruitment & Employment Confederation (REC) says members “must ensure that they have obtained consent or that they have another legal basis which they can rely on (such consent or legal basis to be documented) before disclosing, transferring, displaying, submitting or seeking confidential or personal information” Independent (REC, Code of Professional Practice, principle 10). Recruise’s own promise is the same in substance: “Nothing goes to a client until we have spoken and you have said yes to that specific mandate” Self-claim. Asking for that yes to be in writing costs nothing.
  3. Being put forward twice. Inference, from general practice; no source read for it If the same person reaches the same employer by two routes (two agencies, or an agency and a direct application), the employer has to decide whose candidate he is, and some simply drop the candidate. Knowing the client’s name before submission is the only way to tell whether he is already in its system. Whether Recruise works this search alone or alongside other firms is a fair thing to ask.

Neither code binds Recruise. The REC is a British trade body and the AESC is an international association of executive search firms; we did not check whether Recruise is a member of either. They are quoted as statements of ordinary good practice. His résumé is also personal data: Recruise’s data policy says it keeps candidate profiles for 36 months from the last meaningful contact unless asked otherwise (section 2).
