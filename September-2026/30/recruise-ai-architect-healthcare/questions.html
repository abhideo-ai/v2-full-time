Forcing questions for the recruiter or the hiring manager

A forcing question asks for a specific answer, so that the answer settles something. These come from the research; they are not interview preparation, which waits until he asks for it. Q1 to Q6 and Q12 suit the recruiter; Q7 to Q11 suit the hiring manager, once there is one (Q stands for question). Ask them as curiosity, never as a challenge.

If the first exchange is short, get Q1, Q2 and Q4: who the client is, that nothing goes to them without his yes, and where the seat is.

Q1 Who is the client?

“Before my résumé goes to the client, could you tell me who they are? I’m happy to keep the name confidential.”

Good answer: the company’s name, on a call or in writing, before anything is forwarded. Weak answer: “We will tell you after they shortlist you.”

Nothing about the employer can be checked until it is named, and it is the only way to know whether he is already in its hiring system. Recruise’s own mandate page says the client “is shared once we’re in conversation”, so the request matches its stated practice (research section 3).

Q2 Will you ask me before each submission?

“Could you confirm by email that my résumé will go only to this client for this role, and that you’ll ask me first before sharing it for any other role?”

Good answer: a one-line yes in writing. Weak answer: “We will share it wherever it fits.”

Recruise’s website already promises this: “Nothing goes to a client until we have spoken and you have said yes to that specific mandate.” Getting it in an email turns a web-page promise into a record (research section 7).

Q3 Is the search open, and how old is it?

“Is this search still open, and when did it start? Is it the ‘AI Architect’ role on your website, number 5832?”

Good answer: a start date, the stage it is at, and a yes or no on 5832. Weak answer: “Send your CV and we will see.” (CV: curriculum vitae, the résumé.)

There is no listing and no date, so nothing else can answer this (research section 4). If it is mandate 5832, the search has been open since at least 11 August 2026.

Q4 Which city, and what work mode?

“The post says India. Which city is the role in, and is it on-site, hybrid or remote?”

Good answer: a named city and office, and a stated number of office days. Weak answer: “It is flexible”, with nothing more.

Relocation is open for him, so this is for planning, not a deal-breaker. It also helps confirm whether the Bangalore listing is the same search.

Q5 How firm are the years and the background?

“The post asks for 13 to 16 years and a Big Tech background. Are those firm requirements from the client, or preferences?”

Good answer: which of the two is firm, and what the client means by Big Tech. Weak answer: the line repeated.

The post itself says “preferably from Big Tech organizations” in its body, which is softer than its header line. The recruiter knows how the client reads it.

Q6 Are other firms working this search?

“Is Recruise the only firm on this search, or are other agencies working it too?”

Good answer: a clear yes or no. Weak answer: avoiding the question.

If several firms are on it, the same candidate can reach the employer twice, which some employers resolve by dropping the candidate. This rests on general practice, not on a source read for this page (research section 7).

Q7 A product, or an internal platform?

“Is the AI platform a product the company sells to healthcare customers, or an internal platform its own teams build on? Who uses it today?”

Good answer: named users and a stage (in design, in pilot, in production). Weak answer: “It is a new initiative”, with no users named.

“End-to-end AI platform” in the post could mean either. The architect’s daily work differs a great deal between the two (research section 5).

Q8 Whose patient data, and in which countries?

“Whose data does the platform handle (hospitals, insurers, patients directly), and in which countries?”

Good answer: the customer type and the markets, for example hospitals in the United States. Weak answer: “We are compliant with everything.”

This decides which rules apply: the United States’ Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) for American patient data, India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act for people in India, the European Union’s rules for Europe (research section 6). The post asks for regulatory knowledge and names no law.

Q9 Does any feature inform a clinical decision?

“Does any part of the platform give a clinician a recommendation about a patient, or is it used for administration and documents?”

Good answer: a clear line between clinical and non-clinical uses, and who owns the regulatory position. Weak answer: “We have not looked at that yet.”

Software that informs clinical decisions can be regulated as a medical device, under the United States Food and Drug Administration’s guidance on clinical decision support software and India’s draft guidance on medical device software (research section 6). That changes how the platform must be built, tested and documented.

Q10 What does the architect own, and who does he report to?

“What would I own in the first six months? Who would I report to, and would anyone report to me?”

Good answer: a named area of the platform, a named manager, and no direct reports. Weak answer: a team to manage, or no clear owner for the platform.

The post says “Individual Contributor / Architect” and “design and develop”. Only the employer can confirm both, and the nearest listing on Recruise’s board is labelled “Manager” (research section 3).

Q11 What exists today?

“What is already built and running, which cloud is it on, and which models does it use: hosted models from a provider, models you run yourselves, or both?”

Good answer: a named cloud, named components and an honest account of what is missing. Weak answer: “You will define all of that.”

The post names no technology beyond Python, “Cloud Architecture” and the techniques (large language models, retrieval-augmented generation, agentic AI). Whether this is a first build or the rework of an existing system is the biggest unknown about the work itself.

Q12 What are the interview stages?

“What are the stages after you share my résumé, and who would I meet at each?”

Good answer: the number of rounds and the role of each interviewer. Weak answer: “The client will decide.”

A search firm that is close to its client knows the process. The answer is also a quiet check on how real and how advanced the search is.
