First, the vocabulary

None of his ten roles is recorded in his own material as staff augmentation. Employer type is not established by anything we hold — an earlier pass inferred it from role names, and that inference is withdrawn. His own record cuts against a clean split: the journey document has him at a Houston firm building a supplier–buyer portal, and his own master résumé carries "Secured protected health information inside the client virtual private cloud". So the unfamiliar vocabulary on this page is not technical. It is how a services business is structured, and those structures are what decide what the job actually is day to day, independently of what the title says.

TAG — talent acquisition group
The in-house recruiting function. Not a technical role, and worth expanding because of a genuine collision: TAG is also Google Tag Manager, a marketing tag-management tool, and at least one data aggregator has already made exactly that mistake about this sender. At Aezion the expansion is settled by the company's own applications inbox, tag@aezion.com, printed on its careers page and on both architect postings (AI Architect · Dot Net Architect). The practical consequence: the person who wrote to him is a sourcer, and stack questions will need a second call with an engineer.
Staff augmentation
Selling individual engineers' time into a client's own team, where the client sets the work and the vendor is the employer of record. The engineer takes direction from the client's manager. Titles in this model describe a billing grade, not a scope of authority.
Project-based delivery
The vendor takes a scoped piece of work, staffs it, and is accountable for the outcome. Closer to normal engineering, but the roadmap belongs to the client, the engagement has an end date, and what you work on next is whatever the sales pipeline produces.
Product engineering
Building and operating something the company itself owns and sells, with a roadmap, tenants, an on-call rotation and years of accumulated architecture. This is the shape of his own product record — axe Monitor and the chat platform — and it is the rarest of the three at a services firm. The Amura work does not sit in that column: his own corpus frames it as what was implemented for Amura — client delivery, not a platform his employer owned and sold.
Offshore development centre
A delivery site in a lower-cost country running work for clients elsewhere. The structural consequence that matters more than the cost: the senior, client-facing half of the organisation usually sits at the other end, so an "architect" in the offshore half may be the most senior technical person on site and still not hold the design authority.
GCC — global capability centre
An offshore centre operated for a single named client, often with a path to the client eventually owning it outright. A collision worth naming: GCC here is not the Gulf Cooperation Council. Aezion sells this as one of its seven service lines (Global Capability Centers), which is why "is this seat employed by you doing your own delivery, or is it staffed to a client?" is the single highest-value question on the call.
BOT — build-operate-transfer
The vendor stands up a team in India, runs it for a period, then hands it to the client. Named explicitly on Aezion's own GCC page. The consequence for an individual: under this arrangement your employer can change mid-engagement, by design. That is not a hidden risk — it is the product — but it is worth knowing before a loop rather than after an offer.
The bench
Staff who are employed but not currently assigned to a client engagement. Normal and temporary in Indian services, but it carries a clock, and "how long" is a fair question. Related: bench sales, the practice of circulating unassigned consultants to vendors for contract placement.
Employer of record
The legal employer, which under a dedicated-team or GCC arrangement is the vendor rather than the company whose work you actually do. Aezion states on its GCC page that it "manages the people, infrastructure, technology, security, HR, and operations" (HR — human resources).
Multi-tenant software as a service (SaaS)
One running system serving many customer organisations with their data isolated from each other. It is the phrase the recruiter's email led with, and it is also a phrase Aezion markets on its own custom-software-development page — so it is worth finding out which of the two meanings is in play, because the company's published multi-tenant build and his own are very different systems.
SOC — two different things, and Aezion writes it with no number
SOC 2 is a System and Organization Controls audit report — an attestation a company holds, issued by an auditor. A SOC is a security operations centre, a staffed team watching for intrusions. Aezion's generative artificial-intelligence (AI) page says its platforms carry "Built-in compliance with HIPAA, SOC, and ISO standards" (HIPAA — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act; ISO — International Organization for Standardization). That is a claim about what they build for clients, not an attestation Aezion holds, and no ISO, SOC 2 or CMMI (Capability Maturity Model Integration) certification appears anywhere on their site. He carries real HIPAA certification work, so he is likely to be the person in the room who knows the difference.
MBE and MWBE
Minority business enterprise and minority and women-owned business enterprise — United States supplier-diversity designations used in corporate and public-sector procurement. Aezion displays both badges on its About page. They are procurement credentials, not engineering or security credentials, and should not be read as either.
2-in-the-box
Aezion's own name for a delivery arrangement pairing India-based developers with senior client-facing management in the United States. Named on its blog, in a post dated 27 February 2023. If the seat is shaped this way, an "architect" is an engagement architect rather than a platform architect — the same word, a different job.

Verdict

Take the call — but go in treating it as discovery, not as entering a process

Every research angle failed to identify the seat, and that is an argument for asking rather than for declining. No source anywhere names the role. Aezion runs no applicant tracking system — applications go to a shared mailbox — so there is no requisition to cross-reference, no posting date that means anything, and a careers page that demonstrably omits seats advertised elsewhere. The information is only obtainable by asking, and one call obtains it in about five minutes.

Legitimacy is settled, positively and on registry-grade evidence. The domain is genuine, the recruiting inbox is published on Aezion's own site, the India company is real, active and carries no registered borrowings, and searches for scams, fake offers, impersonation and litigation returned nothing about this company at all. The email carries none of the fraud markers.

The central reservation is not the company — it is that no published Aezion seat matches both his stack and his seniority. The one matching his stack asks four-plus years. The one matching his seniority is a technology he does not have and cannot honestly claim. A third is unreadable. That is the fact the call has to resolve.

The decision point is not now. It is roughly eight minutes into the call. If the answer is a four-to-eight-year band, or a .NET seat, it ends politely there at a cost of twenty minutes. If it is an unposted client engagement at architect altitude, or something Life9-shaped, it earns the next step.

One confirmation for the drafted reply: its choice not to ask for the job description in writing holds up under what was verified here. A company with no applicant tracking system, no requisition dates and a careers page misreporting a fifteen-month-old posting as four days old will answer a document request with silence or a stale template. The call produces the answer faster.

The forcing questions

Who he is talking to, and what that changes. Sravan is a talent acquisition group manager; applications route to tag@aezion.com. He is a sourcer, not an engineer. Questions 1, 2, 4, 6, 7 and 12 are squarely his and he should answer them instantly. Questions 3, 5, 9, 10 and 11 are technical or account-specific and he may genuinely not know. A recruiter who says "let me get the delivery lead on with you" is giving a better answer than one who improvises.

Why these have to be spoken. The written reply deliberately asks nothing — it answers what he asked and puts a call on the table. Every question below is one that would otherwise have cost a round trip of email. This is the only place they get asked. If the call runs short, get 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 at minimum — those five determine whether there is a seat here at all: what it is, what level, what stack, whose employee he would be, where it sits.

Q1 Which role is this, actually?

"Before anything else — which role is this? Is it one of the two architect seats on your careers page, or something that isn't posted?"

Good answer: a title, immediately, and a reason. "It's the AI Architect seat." Or better: "It isn't posted — we're building a team for a new account and I'm sourcing ahead of the requisition."

Bad answer: "A few things could fit, let's talk about your background first." Or a title that arrives only after he asks what you're looking for — that is a role being shaped around whoever picks up the phone.

Aezion has twelve openings live and only two are architect-level. If he names neither, the seat is unposted — which is fine, but it means there is no written specification anywhere and everything you learn about it comes from this call and the next.

Q2 What level is it pitched at?

"What's the experience band on it? I ask because your two architect postings are a long way apart — the .NET one says eleven-plus years and the AI one says four-plus."

Good answer: "Eleven-plus, same as the .NET seat — the AI posting's floor is stale." Or a straight admission: "Honestly the AI one was written for a mid-level hire and we'd need to re-band it for you."

Bad answer: "Experience isn't really the constraint, it's about fit." Or the four-plus figure defended as the real bar.

Q9 What does "ambiguous challenges" mean here, concretely?

Good answer: a real problem with a shape. "A client's data model doesn't support multi-tenancy and we have to get them there without downtime." Anything he has to think about before answering is a good sign.

Bad answer: the phrase repeated back, or "every client is different" — the tell that it came from a template rather than from a team.

The single sentence in the email doing the most work and carrying the least content, so it is the cheapest test of whether there is a specific seat behind the message. It also calibrates scale honestly: Aezion's published client work is small and mid-size United States businesses. That is not a criticism; it is a different kind of hard, and worth knowing which kind.

Q10 What did you mean by "AI-assisted delivery"?

"You mentioned AI-assisted delivery. Did you mean building artificial-intelligence products for clients, or using AI tooling to build faster? Which one is this seat?"

Good answer: a clean distinction and a choice. "Both, but the seat is the first — you'd be architecting retrieval and agent systems for a client." Or: "The second. We want someone to make our delivery teams faster and we don't have anyone driving that."

Bad answer: "Both, it's all AI." Or an answer that turns out to mean reselling Copilot licences.

That phrase is what got him the email, and it is almost certainly a read of the measured Claude Code and OpenAI Codex rollout — a how we build story, not a what we build one. Aezion's two public surfaces point in opposite directions on AI: the marketing page is Microsoft-platform-led, the requisition demands LangChain and LangGraph, and no case study that was actually opened shows Aezion itself delivering the artificial intelligence — on Knomadix, the one AI-named story, the AI is the client's product and Aezion's stated scope is platform modernisation. Ask which, and ask what has actually shipped.

Q11 What's the closest thing you've already built to what I've built?

"What's the closest thing Aezion has already delivered to a multi-tenant SaaS platform? I'd like to hear about one you've actually run."

Good answer: a named build with a stack and an owner. Life9 would be the right answer.

Bad answer: a generic capability pitch, or a case study he cannot describe past its industry.

He led his email with multi-tenant SaaS, which is the axe Monitor work. Aezion's published flagship multi-tenant build, Accubuild, runs on C#, SQL Server 2017, Azure DevOps and Team Foundation Server — the TFS naming places it around 2017–18, so it is evidence of what they delivered eight years ago rather than of current practice. Life9 is the genuinely close analogue and the better thing to draw him onto. One fact to have in advance rather than discover mid-sentence: Life9 serves pro-life clinics.

Q12 What's the process, and what's the timeline?

"If this looks right to both of us — what's the process from here? How many rounds, who's on them, and what's your timeline?"

Good answer: a named sequence with people in it. A named engineer in round one is the strongest signal on this list.

Bad answer: a written test as the first step, or "we'll start with an assessment and take it from there."

Nothing is established about Aezion's interview process. The aptitude-test and difficulty-score picture an earlier pass carried came from search snippets against a page that returned HTTP 403, and it is demoted (see red flag 7). So ask the question cold rather than testing a theory. Ask for the first technical round to be a conversation with the person who would be his peer — as a stated preference at his level, never as a reaction to something read online.

Q13 What does the values framing look like day to day?

"One thing I noticed on your postings — Aezion describes itself as a faith-based company, and the purpose statement is on the job spec itself. What does that look like day to day on a delivery team?"

Good answer: a straight, unembarrassed description of practice. Anything specific and matter-of-fact is a good answer here, in either direction.

Bad answer: deflection, or surprise that he noticed — it is on the postings, so it should be an easy question.

Stated openly by the company about itself, so it is a fact to learn on a call rather than in month two. The About page gives the mission as "To honor God, pursue excellence in servicing others, and grow sustainably to serve more" and the vision as becoming "a globally recognized Software Development Ministry", and both architect postings carry a Purpose line, "Love others like Christ", above the requirements. Neutral information, and his to weigh — but better weighed before an offer than after.

Do not raise: the Aegion Corp litigation that surfaces on a name search — that is a different company, a pipeline-infrastructure business, and it has nothing to do with Aezion. And the "Strategy Consulting" line in his signature, which is almost certainly a platform display artefact.

Methods, and what was cut

  • Seven research angles ran in parallel — company identity, the India entity, the business model, technology and open roles, employee sentiment, legitimacy and risk, and recent signal — each one followed by an adversarial verification pass that re-fetched the sources and adjudicated every claim. This page is written from the verified output of that process and from nothing else. A claim that could not be cited is in the not-found list above rather than in the body.
  • Roughly forty claims were cut in verification. The categories, because the pattern matters more than the count: fabricated verbatim quotations (two); claims whose cited page returned HTTP 403 or 404; numbers that did not reproduce on a second fetch; named private individuals sourced only from contact-data scrapers; and conclusions generalised from a single page onto a whole company.
  • ⚠ Two fabricated quotations were caught, and they must never be repeated. The sentence "builds custom solutions for clients, not products for itself" — presented as Aezion's own self-description and used as the anchor of an entire research angle — is not on the page it was attributed to, and has no near-equivalent. The actual text is "Aezion Custom software development helps Clients realize results that are not possible with off the shelf solutions." Separately, "The role emphasizes hands-on implementation alongside architectural responsibilities" is not on the Dot Net Architect page; its real text, typographical errors included, is quoted in full above. Both would have been indefensible if repeated back to Sravan.
  • Three findings were reversed by verification, not merely trimmed. (1) An angle concluded the AI Architect posting was classical machine learning with "no agent framework named" — the same page explicitly demands LangChain and LangGraph, which inverts the fit conclusion from a stack mismatch to a level mismatch. (2) An angle concluded that nothing Aezion advertises matches a senior architect, having read only the AI Architect posting and never opened the Dot Net Architect one at eleven-plus years. (3) An angle concluded there is "no Hyderabad presence of any kind" from Aezion's careers page, which is not a complete inventory. All three are the same failure — enumerating one source and generalising.
  • Named individuals were held to a higher bar than anything else on this page. Two executive names sourced only to a contact-data scraper that returned HTTP 403 were cut outright. A speculative family relationship between a company director and the founder, inferred from a shared common first name, was removed entirely. Chris Kambala survives because he is named and pictured on Aezion's own homepage and speaks in the first person in a dated third-party profile — but no Aezion page states his title, so it is not asserted here.
  • The standing limit, stated once and applying everywhere: a small, private, unfunded services firm leaves a thin public record. It publishes no press, no leadership, no dates, no engagement models and no client names against roles. Absence of evidence here is weak evidence of absence, and every negative finding on this page should be read that way — including the Java one, which is the one most likely to be over-read.
