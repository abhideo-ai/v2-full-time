First, the vocabulary

Two vocabularies collide in this posting. One is the recruitment-market vocabulary that decides whether the seat is real; the other is the artificial-intelligence (AI) architecture vocabulary that decides what the job is. Both are defined here as concepts, not as expansions, because the expansion of an acronym rarely tells you what the thing does.

How the hiring market works

Blind posting
A job advertisement that describes a role without naming the employer. The recruiter knows the client; the candidate does not. It is a normal and legal practice, not a warning sign in itself. Its consequence is specific and practical: the candidate cannot research the employer, cannot check whether they are already in process there, and cannot judge the seat on anything except the recruiter's description of it.
Contingency mandate
The dominant commercial model in Indian recruitment. The agency is paid only if its candidate is hired — typically a percentage of first-year salary. Nothing is paid for the search itself. The structural consequences matter more than the mechanism: contingency mandates are usually non-exclusive, so several agencies compete on the same requisition, and speed beats care. Fee-model overview, India · Contingency structure explained
Retained mandate
The opposite arrangement: the client pays the search firm in stages regardless of outcome, usually for exclusivity. The distinction is the single most useful thing to establish on a first call, because a retained recruiter almost always names the client early — they have the relationship and the authority to. A contingency recruiter frequently cannot.
Duplicate submission
What happens when two agencies send the same candidate to the same employer, or when an agency sends a candidate who already applied directly. It creates a fee dispute between the agency and the client, and the candidate is often the casualty — some employers reject the profile outright rather than adjudicate. Against a blind posting the candidate has no way to check for this in advance, because they do not know where the profile is going. Why consultants withhold client identities
Ghost job
"A job posting for a non-existent or already filled position." Motives include building a candidate pool for later and "identifying promising future recruits" — that is, sourcing against a requisition that does not yet exist. A 2025 Greenhouse Software study cited by the reference below found "at least one in five job postings in the US is fake or never filled". Read that figure carefully: it is United States data and it does not measure India or this posting. It is here to establish that pipeline-building is a real, documented category, not to price this seat. Reference
Global Capability Centre (GCC)
An engineering or operations centre in India owned by the foreign parent company itself, rather than staffed by an outsourcing vendor. The work is the parent's own product. This is the most common shape a "foreign multinational corporation hiring in Bengaluru" takes, which is why it is discussed below — but the posting never says GCC, and treating it as one is inference, not fact.
WHOIS, and its successor RDAP (Registration Data Access Protocol)
The public registration record every internet domain carries, listing when it was first registered and by which registrar. WHOIS is the original plain-text lookup; RDAP is the modern structured replacement that returns the same facts as machine-readable data. For .in domains the authoritative record is held by the National Internet Exchange of India (NIXI), the registry operator. It is used once on this page, for one fact, and it is the hardest single piece of evidence here.
Corporate Identification Number (CIN)
The registration number the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) assigns to an incorporated Indian company. Useful as a check on whether a firm is a registered company — with an important caveat used below: a sole proprietorship or an unregistered partnership has no CIN at all and is still a perfectly lawful business. Absence of a CIN is not evidence of anything by itself.

What the posting is asking for, technically

Vector database
A store that holds text, images or records as high-dimensional numeric vectors ("embeddings") and answers the question "what is most similar in meaning to this?" rather than "what matches this string?". It is the storage layer under almost every retrieval feature shipped since 2023.
Vector data access layer
The posting's own phrase, and it asks for more than the database. It is the abstraction sitting above the vector store — the component that decides which index is authoritative, enforces which tenant may read which vectors, applies filters before or after the similarity search, and keeps the rest of the application from talking to the store directly. Asking for the access layer rather than the database is a competence signal about whoever wrote this posting: it is the part that actually breaks in multi-tenant production, and generic architect postings do not mention it.
Retrieval and semantic search
Finding the right material by meaning rather than by keyword, and returning it as grounding for something downstream. When the downstream consumer is a large language model, the pattern is usually called retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).
Semantic query routing
Deciding, from what a request means, where it should be answered: which index, which data source, which tool, or which model. In an agentic system it is the traffic-control layer, and it is where correctness and cost are actually won or lost. Named explicitly in the posting's must-have list.
Agentic AI
Systems where a large language model (LLM) plans and carries out multi-step work by calling tools, rather than producing a single answer to a single prompt. Naukri carries "Agentic AI" as the posting's skill tag.
LLM-driven / agentic service interfaces
The contract between an agent and the rest of the estate: how a model is allowed to call an internal service, what it may see, what it may change, and what happens when it gets it wrong. This is an architecture and governance problem more than a modelling one, which is consistent with the seat being an architecture seat.
Spec-Driven Development (SDD)
A methodology in which a structured, machine-readable specification is the authoritative artefact and the implementation, tests and documentation are derived from it — inverting the usual order where documentation trails the code. It became a named, tooled methodology only recently, principally through GitHub's open-source Spec Kit and its Spec → Plan → Tasks → Implement flow. Its presence in this posting is a dating signal, discussed under green flags. GitHub Blog · github/spec-kit · Reference
Horizontal architecture roadmap
The posting's phrase for architecture work that cuts across product teams rather than sitting inside one. It implies an existing architecture function with existing standards. This is not a founding-architect seat by its own description — it says "partner with senior architecture teams", which means those teams already exist.

Red flags

1 — Submitting means authorising an unnamed third party to send his profile to an unnamed company. This is the sharpest flag on the page, and it is the one that can actually cost him something.

Everything else here is uncertainty. This one is a concrete, irreversible action with no way to check it first. Against a blind contingency mandate he cannot know whether the destination is a company he has already applied to directly, is already in process with through another recruiter, or would rather approach himself. Duplicate submission is a live fee dispute between agency and client, and employers not infrequently resolve it by dropping the profile rather than adjudicating it.

The mechanism is well documented and is not a conspiracy theory: consultants withhold the client's identity out of fear the candidate applies directly and removes the fee, fear a competing agency takes the client, and sometimes at the client's own explicit request. The published advice to candidates is exactly the remedy proposed below — "If you want to know who the client is – ask", and if refused, "Ask why they won't tell you". The fix is cheap and it is one sentence before consent, not after.

4 — The title is two titles, and they are not the same level.

"Principal / Chief Architect" hedges across a real distinction. In most technical ladders Chief Architect is normally a single enterprise-wide seat — one per organisation, owning the whole estate's direction — while Principal Architect is a band that several people can hold at once. Offering both in one title generally means one of three things, and they have quite different consequences:

  • The client has not fixed the level and is calibrating against whoever applies. Common, and workable, but it means the seat can be levelled down at offer.
  • The recruiter is describing a requisition in their own words rather than quoting it. Also common. Harmless if they say so.
  • Neither the level nor the requisition is settled yet, because the requisition does not exist. This is the pipeline-building case.

Deliberately weak sourcing, flagged as such. The Principal-versus-Chief distinction is drawn consistently across career-guidance writing but there is no authoritative standard for it, and titles vary enormously by company. Treat the distinction as a reason to ask, not as a finding. The question that settles it is what the requisition literally says, which costs nothing to ask.

Forcing questions

Sharp, answerable on a first call, and ordered so the three that most quickly reveal whether this is worth his time come first. Each is written so that a vague answer is itself the answer.

The three that matter most

  1. "Before you send my profile anywhere — who is the client? I'd like the name before I authorise a submission."

    The whole page in one sentence, and the only question that changes what he can find out for himself. It also puts the consent where it belongs: before the submission, not after. Every possible answer is informative. A name means he can run the real research and the seat becomes assessable. "I can tell you once you're shortlisted" means contingency, non-exclusive, and he should weigh red flag 1 before agreeing. "I'm not able to say" with no reason offered is the answer that should end the conversation politely — and the standard published advice for exactly this moment is to ask why before accepting the refusal.

  2. "Is there a signed mandate and a live requisition for this seat, and is it exclusive to you?"

    This separates a funded requisition from a sourcing exercise, and it does it without the slightly adversarial framing of asking whether the job is real. A recruiter on a retained or exclusive mandate answers this immediately and with some pride, because it is their strongest selling point. Hesitation, or an answer about "an active pipeline" rather than a requisition, is the pipeline-building case from red flag 6 — and if so, that is fine to know, it just means a shorter call and no submission today.

  3. "Does the requisition say Principal Architect or Chief Architect — and how many people already hold that title there?"

    Two questions in one and both land. It resolves the hedged title from red flag 4, and the follow-up about how many people hold it resolves the scope: Chief Architect is normally a single seat, Principal is normally a band, and the difference decides whether this is one voice among peers or the voice. It is also a quiet test of whether the recruiter has actually read a requisition, because someone working from a forwarded description cannot answer the second half at all.

The rest, in order of value

  1. "Does this seat carry direct reports, appraisals or headcount of any kind?" The posting is clean on this and says only "hands-on technical authority" — but the recruiter's own published track record is twelve leadership placements against three individual-contributor ones, so their default reading of an ambiguous requisition will not be his. Ask it as a yes/no and do not accept "it's a leadership role in the technical sense".
  2. "Where does the Chief Technology Officer sit — India or overseas? And who does the person in this seat actually sit with day to day?" The reporting line is the field postings inflate most. This also establishes, without asking about it directly, whether the client is a Global Capability Centre and what the timezone overlap would be. A foreign Chief Technology Officer is not a negative; not knowing about it until month two would be.
  3. "What is the architecture function today — how many architects, and what does the existing horizontal roadmap already cover?" The posting says "partner with senior architecture teams", so they exist. This asks what he would actually be walking into: a functioning group with standards he would extend, or a title with no structure under it. A recruiter who has met the hiring manager can answer; one who has not, cannot.
  4. "They ask for standards that are 'implemented and adopted, not just documented'. What went wrong before, and how would adoption be measured here?" Quotes their own words back, which is disarming, and it is the line in the posting that reads like a scar. The answer describes both the failure they are hiring against and the bar he would actually be judged on. This is also the criterion his own record speaks to most directly, so a clear answer lets him aim the whole conversation.
  5. "Which vector store and which orchestration stack are in production today, and what is already live versus on the roadmap?" Distinguishes a platform being extended from one being started, which changes the job completely. It is also the fastest way to find out whether the technical text was written by an engineer — a recruiter relaying a real requisition will either know or say they will find out, and a fabricated posting produces vagueness.
  6. "What is the hybrid cadence — how many days on site, and at which office?" Location is not a constraint and relocation is open, so this is logistics rather than a gate. But "hybrid" covers everything from two days a month to four days a week, and the posting does not say which.
  7. "What is the travel expectation, and does the seat carry 24/7 on-call?" Both are separate axes from location and from schedule density, and neither appears anywhere in the posting. A horizontal architecture seat at a foreign multinational often carries travel to the parent. Much easier to establish now than after an offer.
  8. "What does the interview process look like, and who runs the technical rounds?" Whether an architect, the Chief Technology Officer or a delivery manager runs it tells him what the seat is really being hired for. It also produces the client's name indirectly more often than people expect, because interviewer names get shared during scheduling.

Where these go. Per this workspace's recruiter-reply rule, a written reply answers exactly what was asked, in the order asked, then puts a call on the table — with no added questions, however useful they look. Questions 3 to 12 belong on the call and get asked out loud. Questions 1 and 2 are the exception worth considering in writing, because the client name and whether a requisition exists both gate the submission itself, and asking them in writing costs one line and can save the call entirely. That is his call to make, not this page's.

Limits of this research

  • The client is unidentified and no guess was attempted. This is the defining limit of the page and it cannot be worked around from outside.   • Interview preparation is deliberately not built here. Prep waits until a company responds. The open claims on his record are flagged for that moment and are untouched: they are his to settle, not this page's.
  • Could not be established: AxiHire Advisors' founding date; its legal form or Corporate Identification Number; any client name; any employee, candidate or client review on Glassdoor, AmbitionBox or Google; any Naukri consultant profile page; whether this specific posting is still live.
