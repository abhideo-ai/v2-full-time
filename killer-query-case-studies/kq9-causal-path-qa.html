Deep-dive Q&A · Auditable decision-chain reconstruction

Why is this patient on this plan — and can you prove every step, twice?

This is the engineering interrogation behind KQ9 on a chronic-care platform covering 80+ chronic conditions: the query a clinician trusts at the bedside and a regulator subpoenas a year later. The honest short answer to "show me the chain" is that it is two questions with two different burdens of proof, and the design is the split between them. Below are roughly a hundred questions a Senior Principal engineer or CTO would actually ask, answered at the level of mechanism and consequence — including the uncomfortable ones.

01 The question, and why it is genuinely hard

›screeningWhat exactly is this query being asked to answer?

Given one patient, return the full chain of reasoning that ends at their current medication plan: the active decision, the evidence nodes that supported it, the policy nodes that constrained it, and the earlier decisions it superseded — each step carrying the identifiers that justify it. The governing rule is "no quote, no claim": every edge in the answer must cite the evidence, policy, or decision it stands on. It is the regulator's view of a treatment, rendered so a clinician can also read it at the bedside.

›screeningWhy is "show the decision chain" two questions rather than one?

A clinician opening the panel mid-visit needs what is true right now, fast enough not to interrupt the consultation, bounded so it shows the decisions that matter and not a decade of history. A regulator asks the same words but means what was true at exactly this instant, reconstructed so precisely that the same query re-run next year returns a byte-identical chain. One wants speed and recency; the other wants provable point-in-time replay. Those are different correctness contracts wearing the same sentence.

›screeningWho are the two audiences, concretely, and what does each actually need?

The bedside clinician needs a low-latency, bounded, current view to answer "why is this patient on this?" without leaving the consultation. The auditor or regulator needs a complete, deterministic, replayable export that reconstructs the chain as it stood at a named historical moment, with full provenance on every node and edge. The clinician trades completeness for speed; the auditor trades speed for provable exactness. Trying to satisfy both from one pipeline forces you to short-change one.

›deepWhy can't a single pipeline serve both audiences well?

The live decision graph is an asynchronously projected copy of the append-only event history, maintained by a projection process. It is excellent at "what is true right now" but cannot, on its own, guarantee point-in-time replay of "what was true back then" — the graph reflects the latest projection, not a frozen past state. A single pipeline built on it must either accept non-replayable audit exports or cripple the clinician path with reconstruction overhead it does not need. That structural conflict, not a bug, is what forced the split.

›deepWhat are the failure modes that quietly destroy trust in an audit surface?

Three. Tenant leakage — one clinic's decision node surfacing in another's chain, which is a reportable privacy incident, not a mere defect. Time leakage — reconstructing against "now" when the question was about a specific moment, so the answer silently includes decisions that had not happened yet. And a narrative that outruns its evidence — a fluent summary asserting more than the cited nodes support, which is exactly the sentence a regulator will pull on. Each is silent when it happens and catastrophic when discovered, so the design treats all three as first-class threats rather than edge cases.

›deepWhy is "no quote, no claim / with citations" a hard requirement, not a nicety?

An audit surface is only worth anything if every assertion in it can be traced back to the record that supports it; an uncited claim is indistinguishable from a fabrication under scrutiny. Making citation mandatory turns the chain into evidence rather than commentary, and it forces the system to degrade honestly — if it cannot cite a step, it withholds the step rather than guessing. That constraint is also what lets a language model touch the output at all: it may only phrase claims that already decompose into cited node fields and edges.

›adversarialIsn't splitting into two products just gold-plating — why not serve the regulator from the same walk with a bigger depth?

Depth is not the problem; replayability is. Even an unbounded live walk reads the current projection, so re-running it next year against the same historical moment can return a different chain — a new correction landed, a node was superseded, the projection advanced. The regulator needs a chain reconstructed from the immutable event history at a named instant, which the live graph cannot promise no matter how deep you walk. The split is not extra polish; it is the only way to give the auditor determinism without taxing the clinician with it.

›adversarialA skeptic says this is just a graph query with a fancy audit log. What is actually hard here?

The graph traversal is the easy part. The hard parts are: guaranteeing that a reconstruction is byte-identical on replay across years and server restarts; ordering a chain by clinical event time while four distinct timestamps compete to represent "when"; keeping a chronic patient's decade of history from either flooding the clinician or truncating the auditor; enforcing tenancy on a live graph walk at the traversal source rather than after the fact; and letting a language model narrate without ever asserting an uncited fact. A graph query answers "what connects." This system has to answer "what was provably true, at this instant, for this tenant, and can you show your work."

›deepWhy is a chronic-care setting what makes this problem bite?

Chronic conditions produce long, revised decision histories — a type-2 diabetic may have been on a medication for years, across multiple protocol phases, with superseded plans and corrections along the way. A short bounded window is fine for an acute encounter but under-shows a chronic patient; an unbounded walk is fine for an audit but overwhelms the bedside. The tension between "recent enough to be usable" and "complete enough to be defensible" is exactly what the two-product split resolves, and it is a tension acute-care systems rarely feel this hard.

02 Architecture and data flow

›screeningWhat are the two products, and what do they share?

A clinician product that walks the live decision graph, bounded and tenant-scoped, for "what is true right now"; and a regulator product that ignores the live graph and reconstructs the chain deterministically from the append-only event spine at an exact historical moment. They share exactly two things: a deterministic seed-derivation contract (how to find the patient's active protocol set and current medication-plan decision), and an optional, heavily guarded narrative layer. Everything else — the data source, the caching, the RBAC, the response shape — diverges deliberately.

›screeningWalk me through the clinician data flow end to end.

The request hits the live clinician endpoint; RBAC authorises the clinician role and an access-log entry is written. The as_of_ms is resolved once at admission and always echoed back. A canonical cache key is built and looked up in Redis; on a hit, a tenant assertion runs over every node and edge and the chain returns. On a miss, projector lag is checked against the clinician SLA (degrade if exceeded), the seed decision is derived, a bounded backward walk runs with edges pre-filtered by event time, the optional narrative is validated, the result is cached with an invalidation subscription registered, and the chain returns with bounded-view metadata and lag.

›deepWalk me through the regulator data flow end to end.

The request hits the regulator export endpoint with a required access reason; a regulator-specific RBAC role, per-tenant and per-role rate limits, and a max page size apply, and an immutable audit record is written before anything is served. A circuit breaker is checked; above a page-count threshold the request becomes an async export job with a webhook on completion. Otherwise the spine is read by source sequence at the requested as_of_ms, the supersedes chain and correction epoch are applied to determine effective state, a deterministic cursor paginates the result, and each page emits every node and edge with full provenance plus replay instructions and an export hash.

›deepWhy does the regulator path read the event spine directly instead of the graph?

Because only the append-only spine can be replayed to a precise past state. The live graph is a current projection: it holds the latest structure, not a history you can rewind. The spine records every event with a source sequence and event-time fields, so the regulator path can read all events up to a named as_of_ms, apply supersedes and correction lineage, and rebuild exactly the chain that stood at that instant. Reading the graph would give you "the chain as it looks now," which is precisely the wrong answer to an audit question.

›screeningWhat is the seed contract, and why is it the only shared compute?

The seed contract is the deterministic recipe for finding the starting point of the chain: the patient's active protocol set and the current medication-plan decision, selected with an explicit medication-plan filter, an as-of valid-time check on the active edges, and a fixed tie-break order of event time, then source sequence, then vertex id. It is shared because both products must agree on where the chain begins or they would answer different questions. Everything downstream of the seed differs, but if the seed drifted between the two, the clinician and the auditor would be looking at different treatments.

›deepHow does the projection process relate the spine to the live graph, and what does it stamp?

A projection Lambda consumes the append-only spine and maintains the live graph, stamping each node and edge with a projection version and exposing a projector-lag figure. That lag is the age of the projection relative to the spine, and it is the honest signal the clinician path uses to decide whether it is serving fresh-enough structure or must degrade. Because projection is asynchronous, the graph is always slightly behind the spine — which is exactly why the regulator path bypasses it and reads the spine directly.

›deepWhere does the narrator sit in the topology, and why "off to the side"?

The narrative layer hangs off both products as an optional post-processing step; it never sits in the authoritative data path. The chain JSON — nodes, edges, provenance — is always the primary artifact; the narrative is a phrasing of it that can be requested, validated, and dropped without affecting the chain. Placing it off to the side means an LLM failure degrades to "chain without prose," never to "wrong chain," and for the regulator export the narrative is explicitly an optional appendix with the chain JSON as primary evidence.

›adversarialIf both products start from the same seed, how can they legitimately return different chains for the same patient at the same moment?

They can, and it is by design. The clinician chain is bounded — depth-limited and windowed — so for a long-history patient it deliberately omits older ancestors, and it marks that omission in bounded-view metadata. The regulator chain is complete at the requested instant. So at the same as_of_ms the clinician sees a correct subset and the auditor sees the whole; the seed is identical, the bounds differ. The design makes the omission explicit rather than pretending the bounded view is the full story.

›adversarialWhat happens if the live graph and the spine disagree?

They will disagree by construction, because the graph lags the spine — that gap is the projector lag, and it is surfaced, not hidden. The clinician path treats the graph as authoritative for "right now" but degrades when lag exceeds SLA; the regulator path treats the spine as ground truth and never consults the graph, so its answer is unaffected by projection state. If the two ever diverged beyond lag — a projection defect — the spine is the arbiter, and the regulator export would still be correct while the clinician panel would show a degraded banner. The design deliberately never asks the two to agree in real time.

03 As-of correctness and future leakage

›screeningWhat is as_of_ms, and why must it be explicit?

as_of_ms is the instant the reconstruction is anchored to — the "when" of "what was true when." It is resolved once at request admission and always returned in the response, never allowed to default silently to "now." Making it explicit is what turns an ambiguous question into a reproducible one: two callers asking about the same moment get the same chain, and an auditor can re-run the export against the same anchor and expect an identical result.

›deepWhat goes wrong if as_of defaults silently to "now"?

You get non-reproducibility and, worse, silent future leakage. If the anchor floats to wall-clock "now," then a chain reconstructed for an audit about a past decision quietly includes evidence and decisions that post-date the moment in question. The export stops being an as-of reconstruction and becomes a moving snapshot that changes every time it runs, which is fatal for anything a regulator would rely on. Resolving the anchor once, at admission, and echoing it back is the cheapest possible defense against an expensive class of bug.

›deepHow does the clinician walk avoid pulling in edges from after the anchor?

The edge allow-list is filtered by event_time_ms against the window and the anchor before each expansion step, so an edge that occurred after the anchor is never traversed in the first place. Combined with resolving the anchor once, this keeps the bounded walk strictly within the as-of horizon. Without it, a fast-moving patient record could splice a later evidence node into a chain that is supposed to represent an earlier state.

›deepWhy must the edge time-filter be applied before expansion, not after?

If you expand first and filter afterward, you can fragment the chain: the walk steps through an out-of-window edge to reach an in-window node, then the post-filter drops the connecting edge and strands or drops the node, producing a broken or incomplete chain. Filtering each edge by event time before the repeat step means the traversal only ever follows edges that belong in the window, so the surviving chain is connected and correct. This was a specific high-severity finding in the first review, and the pre-expansion filter is the fix.

›adversarialTwo clinicians open the same panel 30 seconds apart and see different chains. Is that a bug?

No — it is expected and documented. If a new decision lands in the 30-second gap, the later caller's "now" is genuinely a different world, and the clinician product is supposed to show current reality. The design makes this legible by resolving and returning as_of_ms on every response, so the two views are not "inconsistent," they are anchored to two different instants. The place where a re-run must return the identical chain is the regulator export at a fixed anchor — and there it does.

›deepHow does the regulator reconstruction guarantee the same chain for a historical as_of on a rerun next year?

It reconstructs from the immutable spine, not the mutable graph, and every input is pinned: it reads all events for the patient and tenant up to the anchor by source sequence, applies the supersedes chain and the correction epoch effective at that anchor, and orders the result deterministically. Because the spine is append-only and content-addressed, the same anchor plus the same correction epoch yields the same effective events in the same order, and the export hash lets a reviewer confirm equality. The determinism comes from the structured reconstruction, and the hash is how you check it held.

›deepWhich of the four event-time fields orders the chain, and why not received or written time?

The clinical chain is ordered by event_time_ms — the real-world moment the decision or observation happened — because that is the order a clinician or regulator reasons in. The other three, observed_at_ms, received_at_ms, and written_at_ms, are kept for replay and late-arrival handling but must not drive chain order: received and written time reflect when the system learned of an event, which for backfilled or delayed data can be years after it occurred. Order by ingestion time and a decision made in 2019 but loaded in 2025 would sort as if it happened in 2025.

›adversarialA correction lands that changes what was true at a past instant. Does the regulator export change retroactively?

This is what the correction epoch exists to disambiguate. A correction is itself an event with its own time and epoch, so the export reconstructs the state that was effective at the requested anchor under the correction epoch effective then — it does not silently rewrite a prior export. If you ask for the same anchor but a later correction epoch, you can legitimately get a corrected chain, and the response records which epoch it reflects. The point is that "what was true then" and "what we later learned was true then" are both answerable and distinguishable, never conflated.

›screeningWhat is a "bounded view," and how is the omission surfaced to the clinician?

A bounded view is the clinician chain limited to a configurable depth and time window so it stays fast and readable. The response carries bounded-view metadata — the depth, the window, the edge allow-list, the traversal-policy version, and an explicit flag that older ancestors were omitted — so the panel can tell the clinician the view is partial and offer to request older ancestors or a full regulator export. The omission is a stated fact in the payload, never an unlabelled truncation.

04 Tenancy and isolation

›screeningWhat is the tenant identity, and where does it come from?

Tenancy is a three-field scope — account_id, workspace_id, and controlunit_id — resolved from the caller's authenticated identity, not passed as a query parameter. All three must match on every node and edge that appears in a chain. Treating tenancy as a single field would let a workspace or control-unit boundary leak inside a shared account, so the scope is carried whole through the traversal, the cache key, and the spine read.

›screeningHow is tenant isolation enforced on the live graph walk?

The clinician walk runs through a tenant-scoped traversal source: the graph traversal is constructed already bound to the caller's three-field scope, so the walk cannot step outside it. That is the primary enforcement layer. It is backed by a Redis key prefix that includes the tenant and a response-time assertion that re-checks every returned node and edge — belt and braces, because a single missing predicate on a live graph is a privacy incident, not a rendering glitch.

›deepWhy isn't a post-hoc filter on results enough — why scope at the traversal source?

A post-hoc filter runs after the walk has already visited other tenants' nodes, which means the query touched data it should never have read and any bug in the filter leaks it. Scoping at the traversal source means the walk is structurally incapable of leaving the tenant's subgraph — the isolation is a property of the query construction, not a cleanup pass. The response-time assertion is then a defense-in-depth check that the invariant held, not the thing you rely on to establish it.

›deepHow does the Redis chain cache avoid cross-tenant bleed?

The canonical cache key is prefixed with the full three-field tenant scope alongside the endpoint, bounds, edge allow-list, schema versions, anchor, last event sequence, and projection version. Because the tenant is part of the key, one tenant's cached chain can never be served to another even if every other dimension matches. The key completeness is a cross-cutting requirement across the platform, and here it does double duty: correctness of the cached result and tenant safety of it.

›adversarialA single missing tenant predicate leaks another clinic's decision node. What is the defense in depth?

Three independent layers must all fail for a leak to reach the caller: the tenant-scoped traversal source (the walk can't leave the scope), the tenant-prefixed cache key (a hit can't cross tenants), and the response-time assertion that re-verifies every node and edge against the caller's scope before serving. The assertion exists precisely because human error in a query predicate is plausible; it turns a silent leak into a served-nothing failure. A cross-tenant node is treated as an incident that pages a human, not a metric to trend downward.

›deepHow does the regulator spine reconstruction enforce tenancy?

The spine read predicates on all three tenant fields — account, workspace, and control unit — as part of the query that selects events by source sequence at the anchor, so events outside the caller's scope are never fetched. Tenancy is thus enforced at the point of data retrieval on both products: the traversal source for the live path, the query predicate for the spine path. The regulator export additionally records the tenant in its immutable audit entry, so who read whose data is itself part of the permanent record.

›adversarialYou once called tenant isolation "partial / platform-gated." What does that mean for shipping this KQ safely?

The query first inlined its own enforcement — the tenant-scoped traversal source, the Redis prefix, and the response-time assertion are all specified in this KQ. The shared traversal-source wrapper standardised across every query, so isolation is not re-implemented per query with per-query risk, has since landed, and KQ9 now binds to it. Before it landed, KQ9 was safe on its own terms but carried the dependency openly rather than claiming a platform-level guarantee it did not yet have. Honesty about which layer owns the guarantee is the point.

›screeningIs a cross-tenant node a metric or an incident?

An incident. The evaluation bar for tenant safety is zero cross-tenant nodes or edges on any returned chain, and a violation pages a human rather than being averaged into a dashboard. Framing it as a metric to optimise would implicitly accept a nonzero rate, which for a privacy boundary in healthcare is not an acceptable posture.

05 The core mechanism: traversal and replay

›screeningWhich edge types make up the causal chain?

Three: SUPPORTED_BY (a decision rests on an evidence node), CONSTRAINED_BY (a decision is bounded by a policy node), and SUPERSEDES (a decision replaces an earlier one). These form the causal backbone of the chain. Attribution edges — who made the decision — are handled separately, because "which clinician acted" is not part of the clinical justification and must not be folded into it.

›deepWhy is attribution separated from the causal path rather than folded in?

The causal chain answers "what justified this decision" — evidence and policy. The attribution edge answers "who recorded it," which is a different question with different governance. Folding the actor into the causal path both muddies the justification and creates a scorecard risk, so attribution is rendered as a sibling block carrying a pseudonymous actor id, not a link in the reasoning. Keeping them distinct means a regulator reads the clinical logic without it being tangled up with clinician identity.

›screeningHow is the seed decision derived deterministically?

Starting from the patient, the walk finds the active protocol set via as-of valid-time checks on the enrolment and phase edges, then selects the medication-plan decision with an explicit event-type filter, breaking any ties by ordering on event time, then source sequence, then vertex id. That fixed ordering is what makes the seed deterministic: given the same anchor and data, the same starting decision is chosen every time. A non-deterministic seed would let two runs begin the chain at different decisions and diverge from the first step.

›deepHow is the walk bounded, and why depth plus a time window rather than one bound?

The clinician walk is bounded both by depth — a pre-registered target of four hops — and by a time window, defaulting to a year, with both configurable per patient. Depth alone would let a dense recent cluster of edges explode the result; a time window alone would let a single deep chain run past what a clinician can read. Together they keep the bedside view fast and legible. The regulator product removes the bounds entirely, which is exactly why it needs the separate deterministic engine.

›adversarialA four-year diabetic has chains older than the window. Doesn't the clinician panel under-show and mislead?

It under-shows by design, and the design refuses to let that be silent. When older ancestors fall outside the window, the response sets an explicit "older ancestors omitted" flag and the panel offers to request older ancestors or the full regulator export. So the clinician is told the view is bounded rather than shown a truncated chain dressed up as complete. The bounds are also per-patient configurable, so a long-history chronic patient can be given a wider window when that is clinically warranted.

›deepHow is SUPERSEDES lineage handled so a superseded decision isn't shown as current?

Supersession is a first-class edge, and the regulator reconstruction walks the supersedes chain to determine the effective state at the anchor — an earlier decision that was superseded before the anchor is represented as history, not as the current plan. Each vertex also carries the source sequence it supersedes, so the lineage is explicit and replayable. Ignoring supersession would show a patient as being on a plan that a later decision had already replaced, which is both clinically wrong and an audit failure.

›deepWhat actually makes the regulator reconstruction deterministic?

A deterministic cursor over the tuple (event_time_ms, source_event_seq, vertex_id, edge_position). That composite ordering is total — no two elements tie — so the reconstruction emits nodes and edges in one and only one order for a given anchor and correction epoch. Determinism is a property of this structured ordering over immutable events, not of any model or heuristic. It is what lets the same export be reproduced byte-for-byte and verified against its hash.

›deepHow does the cursor stay stable across server restarts and reject misuse?

The cursor encodes the ordering tuple plus the anchor and the traversal-policy version, so it is a pure function of the data and the request, not of server-local state — a restarted server resumes exactly where the previous one left off. Critically, it is bound to its as_of_ms anchor and is rejected if reused when freshness changes without that same anchor, which stops a caller from paging through a moving target and stitching together an inconsistent export. Stable pagination is what makes a multi-page audit export trustworthy rather than a race against ingestion.

›adversarialWhat stops the walk from looping or exploding on a densely connected patient graph?

The clinician walk uses simple-path semantics so it never revisits a vertex within a path, and it is hard-bounded by depth and the pre-expansion time filter, which together cap how far and how wide it can go. Cycles in supersedes or evidence links therefore terminate rather than spin. The regulator path does not walk at all — it reads events in cursor order — so it has no traversal blow-up to worry about, only pagination, which the deterministic cursor and async job mode handle.

06 The narrator: gates, faithfulness, suppression

›screeningWhat is the "claim grammar," and what does it enforce?

The claim grammar is a field-level validator that every factual sentence in a narrative must satisfy: the sentence has to decompose into allowed templates backed by cited node fields and cited edges that actually exist in the chain. A sentence like "metformin was started because HbA1c crossed the first-line threshold" only passes if the drug field, the evidence value, and the policy threshold it names are all present and linked. It is what keeps the narrator from asserting anything the structured chain does not already contain.

›screeningHow is a factual sentence validated against the chain?

Each candidate sentence is parsed against the allowed claim templates, and every field and edge it references is checked to exist in the chain JSON with the values it asserts — cited node ids must resolve to real nodes, and the "X led to Y" structure must match a real edge direction. Both a citation check (do the referenced nodes exist) and a structural check (does the asserted relationship match an edge) are necessary; either alone is insufficient. A sentence that fails any part is not repaired — it is removed.

›deepWhen a sentence can't be cited, is it softened or dropped?

Dropped. The principle is suppress, don't soften: a claim that fails validation is removed and the response is labelled narrative_suppressed rather than being quietly weakened into a vaguer version that might still overreach. Softening is dangerous because it produces a claim that looks supported but isn't; suppression produces an honest gap. The chain JSON is always returned regardless, so the caller loses prose, never evidence.

›adversarialCan the narrator assert "the patient's diet improved" without a node id? Why not?

No. "The patient's diet improved" is a factual clinical claim, and if no node and edge in the chain encode that observation, it cannot decompose into a cited template, so the claim grammar rejects it and the sentence is suppressed. This is the exact failure the guardrail exists to catch: a fluent model inferring a plausible-sounding fact the record does not support. Allowing it would be precisely the sentence a regulator pulls, so the system would rather say less than say something it cannot back.

›deepWhy is "suppression over emission" the governing principle here?

Because the cost of a wrong or unsupported claim in a clinical audit surface vastly outweighs the cost of an omitted sentence. A missing sentence is a visible, recoverable gap; an unsupported sentence is an invisible liability that surfaces at the worst possible moment. Designing the system to withhold when uncertain — in the narrator, in the degraded clinician path, in the bounded-view omission flag — makes its failures honest and legible rather than confidently wrong. Emission-by-default would optimise for looking complete over being correct.

›deepThe chain links decisions to evidence. How do you keep the language observational rather than causal?

The edges record what a decision was supported by and constrained by in the record — an association a clinician documented — not a proven cause. The narrative templates and the surrounding copy stay in that register: "this decision cited this evidence and this policy," never "this evidence caused this outcome." The graph reconstructs the documented reasoning; it does not certify that the reasoning was correct or that the treatment worked. Keeping the language observational is what stops an audit trail from being misread as a causal claim about clinical efficacy.

›adversarialIsn't "causal path reconstruction" inherently a causal claim? How do you square that with keeping statistics observational?

The "causal" here is the documented decision causality — "this plan was recorded as resting on this evidence and this policy" — not statistical causal inference about outcomes. The system reconstructs the chain of reasoning a clinician actually recorded; it makes no claim that the evidence caused a good result, and it computes no treatment effect. Where the platform does compare cohorts elsewhere, matching balances only the covariates that were measured and never rules out unmeasured confounding, so those results are framed as observational too. In KQ9 specifically there is no effect estimate at all — only a provenance chain — so the causal-inference caveat is about being careful with the word, not about a hidden statistical claim.

›deepWhat is emitted when the narrator fails validation, and how does the caller know?

The response carries a narrative_suppressed status alongside the full chain JSON and validation-failure metadata describing which check failed and on which sentence. The caller therefore receives the authoritative evidence and an explicit signal that the prose was withheld and why, rather than a silently shortened paragraph. For the regulator export this matters doubly: the chain JSON is the primary evidence and the narrative was only ever an optional appendix, so its suppression costs nothing of substance.

07 Caching, determinism and versioning

›screeningWhat is in the clinician cache key?

The canonical key binds the three-field tenant scope, the endpoint, the bounds and edge allow-list, the schema and traversal-policy versions, the resolved as_of_ms, the last event sequence, and the projection version. Each dimension changes the answer, so each must be in the key or the cache would serve a chain computed under different assumptions. The point of a canonical key is that two requests share a cache entry if and only if they would compute the same chain.

›deepWhy does the key include last_event_seq and projection_version, not just patient and anchor?

Because patient plus anchor does not uniquely determine the chain: the same patient at the same anchor can reconstruct differently after a correction lands or the projection advances. The last event sequence is a freshness marker that changes when new events arrive, and the projection version changes when the graph is re-projected; folding both into the key means a stale entry can never masquerade as current. Without them, the cache would happily return a chain that new events had already invalidated.

›deepWhy is a 15-minute TTL alone the wrong invalidation strategy for an in-room clinician?

Because the clinician who just recorded a decision would not see it for up to the TTL, which is exactly the moment they most need the chain to reflect their own action. A TTL bounds staleness by time, but the event that matters is a write, not a clock tick. So the cache invalidates on decision-writes and correction events for the patient via a subscription registered when the entry is set — the chain updates when the world changes, not on a timer. A TTL can still exist as a backstop, but it cannot be the primary mechanism.

›deepDoes temperature-zero make the narrator's output deterministic?

No. Temperature-zero, a pinned model version, and a pinned prompt reduce variance and make regeneration reproducible enough to audit, but they do not guarantee a language model returns byte-identical text — floating-point non-associativity, batching, and hardware differences can still shift a token. So the design does not claim generation determinism from the narrator. It claims something narrower and honest: the hashes detect drift; they do not prevent it. Any real determinism in this system comes from the structured chain, not from the model that describes it.

›deepIf temperature-zero isn't deterministic, what do the chain and narrative hashes actually buy you?

Drift detection and reproducible auditing. The chain-json hash pins the exact input the narrator saw, the narrative hash pins the exact text served, and the validation version pins the rules it passed. Stored together, they let a later regeneration be compared against what was actually served, so a divergence is caught rather than assumed impossible. You cannot promise the model never drifts, but you can promise that if it does, the audit trail shows it — which is the guarantee an auditor actually needs.

›adversarialSo the clinician dashboard could flicker on cache rehydrate. How is that prevented?

By not regenerating the narrative per view. Narratives are cached and served keyed by the chain hash, so a rehydrate returns the same stored text rather than re-invoking the model, which is where flicker would come from. Regeneration only happens when the underlying chain actually changes — a new decision-write or correction — at which point a different narrative is expected and correct. The structured chain is the source of stability; the cache-by-hash discipline is what protects the panel from the model's non-determinism.

›deepWhere does real determinism in this system come from, then?

From the structured reconstruction, not the prose. The regulator chain is a pure function of immutable spine events, the correction epoch, and a total ordering over (event_time_ms, source_event_seq, vertex_id, edge_position); given the same anchor those inputs are fixed, so the output is fixed and its hash is verifiable. The narrator sits downstream of that determinism and inherits none of the model's uncertainty into the evidentiary artifact. That separation — deterministic chain, best-effort narration — is the whole reason the LLM is allowed near a regulated surface at all.

›deepWhy is the regulator export not cached in Redis?

Because it is a deterministic stream reconstructed from the spine, not a point lookup that benefits from a cache. Every export is anchored, paginated by a stable cursor, and reproducible on demand, so caching it would add invalidation complexity for little gain and risk serving a stale page under a moving anchor. Large exports are instead handled by an async job with a webhook. The clinician path caches because it is latency-sensitive and repeatedly asked; the regulator path streams because it is correctness-sensitive and replayable.

›adversarialA model or prompt version bumps. What happens to previously served narratives?

Previously served narratives keep their stored hashes and the versions they were produced under, so they remain auditable exactly as served — the bump does not retroactively rewrite history. A new request runs under the new versions, and because the versions are part of what is pinned and stored, a regeneration under a different version that diverges is flagged rather than silently accepted. This is why the model and prompt versions are treated as first-class recorded inputs, not deployment details: they are part of what makes a past narrative explainable.

08 Nine years of hydration — what it buys

›screeningWhy does a decision-chain query care about nine years of history?

Because a regulator can ask about a decision made years ago, and there is nothing to reconstruct unless that history lives in the spine. Roughly nine years of records were replayed through the same append-only spine that carries live traffic, so the system starts with its full past rather than accruing one. For a chronic patient whose current plan rests on a chain that began long before, the historical backfill is the difference between a complete chain and a stub.

›deepWhat breaks if legacy event time is collapsed to load time during hydration?

Every as-of reconstruction — the entire regulator product — becomes void. If nine years of events all carry a load-day timestamp, the chain can no longer be ordered by when things actually happened, and any "what was true at this instant" query returns nonsense because everything appears to have occurred at once. That is why each legacy record keeps its real-world event time and observed time separately from the received and written timestamps that record only when the system learned of it. Collapsing them apart is not a data-quality nit; it is a correctness-ending mistake.

›screeningHow is the backfill made idempotent and resumable?

Content-addressed event ids plus conditional writes: re-running the pipeline over the same corpus produces a byte-identical spine because a duplicate event addresses to the same id and the conditional write is a no-op. That makes the load safe to interrupt and resume, and safe to re-run without doubling anything. Idempotence is what lets a nine-year backfill be operated like a normal pipeline rather than a one-shot migration you are terrified to touch.

›adversarialHow do you know the hydration didn't silently corrupt or reorder history?

Because re-running it is an executable proof: the same corpus yields a byte-identical spine and an identical snapshot manifest hash, so equality of the hash is a check you can run rather than an assurance you have to trust. If a load had corrupted or reordered anything, the hash would differ and the divergence would be caught. This turns "we believe the backfill was faithful" into "the backfill reproduces to the same bytes, here is the hash" — the kind of claim an auditor can independently verify.

›deepWhat specifically does nine years of hydration buy this query that a fresh system couldn't offer?

The ability to answer the regulator's oldest questions at all. A decision made years before the platform went live is only reconstructible if its events exist in the spine with faithful event times, so hydration is what makes a long-tenure chronic patient's chain deep and complete rather than starting from whenever the platform switched on. It also means the deterministic reconstruction has real, messy history to be tested against — corrections, supersessions, protocol changes — not a clean synthetic past.

›deepWhy are hydration-time lateness and live-time lateness handled as different modes?

Live traffic tolerates a small operational window of late arrivals; hydration deliberately expects a nine-year spread of "late" data, because every backfilled record arrives long after it occurred. Treating hydration under the live watermark would flag the entire backfill as pathologically late and either drop or misclassify it. So lateness runs in a distinct hydration mode that keeps that wide spread as normal, preserving the real event times instead of forcing legacy data through an operational-window assumption built for streaming.

›adversarialA chronic patient's chain spans the full nine years. How does the regulator export page through that without falling over?

Above a page-count threshold the export switches to an async job mode: it returns a job id and notifies via webhook on completion instead of holding a synchronous request open. Within a job, the deterministic cursor pages the reconstruction in stable, restart-safe chunks, and per-tenant and per-role rate limits plus a circuit breaker keep one large export from starving the endpoint. So a decade-spanning chain is handled as a bounded stream of pages, not a single unbounded response that times out.

09 Governance, PHI and the LLM boundary

›screeningWhere does the narrator run, and does PHI leave the network?

The production narrator is a self-hosted language model running inside the platform's VPC, so the chain — which contains patient identifiers and clinical detail — is never sent off the network to generate prose. In-VPC self-hosting is the single, explicit production path for the LLM boundary. That choice is deliberate: the cheapest way to keep clinical text safe is to not let it leave in the first place.

›deepUnder what single condition is an external provider permitted?

Only with a de-identified payload — the chain must be stripped of protected health information before it ever leaves the network to an external model. That is the one permitted alternative to the self-hosted in-VPC path, and it changes the residency of clinical text rather than relying on a promise about what the external service does with it. If the payload still contains PHI, the external route is not available, full stop.

›adversarialIsn't a no-retention contract with a provider equivalent to keeping the data in-VPC?

No — and conflating the two is the mistake to avoid. A contractual no-retention agreement is a legal control over what a provider promises to do with data it has already received; it is not a network-residency control, because the PHI still left your network to reach them. In-VPC self-hosting prevents the data from leaving at all, which is a categorically stronger guarantee than a promise not to keep what was sent. The design treats residency and retention as different axes and only accepts an external provider when residency is handled by de-identification, not by a retention clause.

›screeningHow is regulator access governed — RBAC, audit, rate limits?

The regulator export runs under a distinct RBAC role, separate from the clinician role, with per-tenant and per-role rate limits and a max page size. An immutable audit record — viewer, purpose, tenant, export id, and export hash — is written before the export is served, so access is logged even if the request later fails. Circuit breakers protect the endpoint independently of the clinician path. Access to the audit surface is itself audited.

›deepThe chain attributes decisions to clinicians. How do you keep it from becoming a clinician scorecard?

Through a set of mitigations that make misuse hard and visible rather than any single guarantee. Attribution carries a pseudonymous actor id rather than a name; the attribution block is rendered as a sibling to the causal chain, not folded into it; where actors are listed the ordering is alphabetic and never rankable; viewing attribution is RBAC-gated toward leadership-and-self views; and every regulator access is written to an immutable log before serving. None of these makes a scorecard impossible, but together they make building one require deliberate, logged, cross-purpose effort.

›adversarialCouldn't leadership use this to rank clinicians anyway?

Honestly, a determined actor with enough access could try to derive one — no design can make a dataset that names who did what incapable of being misused. What the system does is raise the cost and the visibility: pseudonymous ids, never-rankable alphabetic ordering, RBAC scoping, and an immutable access log mean the raw material is not shaped like a league table and any attempt to assemble one leaves a trail. The claim is not "this can never become a scorecard"; it is "misuse is made hard and visible," which is the honest and enforceable version.

›deepWhat is written to the audit log, and exactly when?

For a regulator export: the viewer id, the stated purpose or access reason, the tenant scope, the export id, and the export hash — and it is written before the chain is served, not after. Writing it first means an access is recorded even if the export subsequently errors or is abandoned, so the log reflects intent to access, not just successful completion. Clinician requests are logged through the standard access pipeline. The ordering — log, then serve — is the small detail that makes the audit trail trustworthy.

›deepWhy does attribution use a pseudonymous actor id rather than the clinician's name in the graph?

Because the graph's job is to record which actor a decision is attributed to, not to broadcast identity everywhere the chain travels. A pseudonymous id lets the attribution be joined back to a real identity through a governed, access-controlled resolution when there is a legitimate need, while keeping raw names out of chains that may be widely read. It reduces the blast radius of any chain that leaks or is over-shared and supports the never-rankable posture around clinician attribution.

›adversarialIf the narrator is optional and can be suppressed, why include an LLM at all?

Because a cited natural-language summary is genuinely useful at the bedside — a clinician reads "started because HbA1c crossed the threshold, superseding the diet-only plan" faster than a node-and-edge graph — and the guardrail makes that convenience safe by refusing any sentence it cannot cite. The value is accessibility; the risk is overreach; and the design keeps the value while capping the risk by making the narrator strictly downstream of the deterministic chain, validated per sentence, and droppable without loss. If the guardrail ever felt insufficient, a deterministic template renderer is the fallback, since the chain already carries everything a template needs.

10 Failure modes, degradation and operations

›screeningWhat happens when projector lag exceeds the clinician SLA?

The clinician path returns a degraded response — a bounded-view-degraded state with the last event sequence it actually saw and an explicit banner that it is serving a stale projection — rather than pretending the graph is current or hanging the request. The projector lag is always returned so the degradation is legible. The clinician still gets a usable answer with an honest caveat, which is far better than a silently outdated chain or a spinner mid-consultation.

›deepWhy return a degraded response instead of blocking or serving silently-stale data?

Blocking hangs the consultation; serving silently-stale data lies to the clinician. A labelled degraded response does neither — it hands over the best available chain and tells the reader exactly how fresh it is, so the human can decide whether to trust it or wait. This is suppression-over-emission applied to operations: when the system cannot meet its freshness bar, it says so instead of hiding it. Degraded responses are also held to the latency budget, so degradation never means slow.

›deepHow does the regulator endpoint protect itself under load?

With per-tenant and per-role rate limits, a max page size, a circuit breaker independent of the clinician path, and an async export job mode above a page-count threshold. These exist because a full historical export is expensive and a burst of them could starve the endpoint or the spine. Isolating the regulator controls from the clinician path means a heavy audit run cannot degrade the bedside experience, and vice versa — the two products fail independently.

›adversarialThe narrator times out or fails mid-request. Does the whole chain fail?

No. The narrative is off to the side of the authoritative path, so an LLM timeout or failure yields a narrative_suppressed response with the full chain JSON and failure metadata — the caller loses the prose, never the evidence. Because the chain is computed before and independently of the narrator, there is no path where a model problem produces a wrong or missing chain. This is precisely why the narrator was architected as an optional post-processing step rather than an inline dependency.

›screeningWhat do the green, yellow, and red degradation tiers mean operationally?

Green means projector lag is within SLA and the clinician chain reflects fresh structure. Yellow and red are escalating degraded states where lag exceeds the threshold: the response still returns within the latency budget but is explicitly marked stale, with the last event sequence seen so the reader knows how far behind it is. The tiers turn a continuous lag figure into an operational signal the UI and on-call can act on, rather than a raw number nobody reads.

›deepHow are very large exports handled without timing out the request?

Above a page-count threshold the export becomes an async job: the endpoint returns a job id immediately and delivers a webhook notification when the reconstruction completes, committing an export hash. Synchronous requests stay small and fast; unbounded historical exports run offline as jobs. This keeps the endpoint responsive and lets a decade-spanning chain be produced reliably instead of racing a request timeout it would eventually lose.

›adversarialA clinician just wrote a decision they can't see because the live graph is stale. How is that handled?

This is the exact case a TTL would mishandle. The chain cache invalidates on decision-writes for the patient, so the clinician's own action busts the relevant cache entry rather than being hidden behind a timer. If the projection itself is lagging, the response degrades with a stale banner and the last event sequence, so the clinician can see that their write may not yet be reflected and why. The combination of write-driven invalidation and honest degradation is what keeps the "I just did this and it's not there" failure from happening silently.

›deepWhat observability is needed to run this safely?

Per-endpoint latency at the median and tail, the narrative faithfulness-rejection rate, the audit-export volume, and the projector lag distribution that drives degradation — plus tenant-assertion failures, which should be zero and page if not. These are the signals that tell you whether the clinician SLA is holding, whether the narrator is suppressing more than expected (a prompt or model regression), and whether the regulator endpoint is being stressed. Without them you would learn about a degradation or a guardrail regression from a user, not a dashboard.

11 Evaluation and acceptance criteria

›screeningWhat are the two things evaluation must never let break?

A chain that reconstructs differently on a rerun, and a narrative that says more than the chain supports. Everything in the evaluation suite is organised around those two failures, because they are the ones that destroy an audit surface's credibility. Both are checked on seeded fixtures where the correct answer is known by construction, so a regression is caught deterministically rather than argued about.

›screeningWhat is a "golden chain," and what edge cases does the set cover?

A golden chain is a hand-curated fixture whose correct reconstruction is known, used to assert byte-for-byte output. The set is expanded well beyond the archetype's baseline of twenty to cover the cases that actually bite: chronic histories over a year long, multiple protocol sets, corrections and supersedes chains, same-timestamp events that stress the tie-break ordering, missing projection edges, and paginated audit fixtures. It exists so the hard cases are tested on purpose, not discovered in production.

›deepHow is replay determinism tested?

The spine reconstruction is run repeatedly against a fixed historical anchor and asserted to produce an identical chain each time, including across simulated server restarts, and the cursor is checked to reject reuse when freshness changes without the same anchor. The acceptance bar is "identical at any as-of," verified against the export hash. This directly tests the property the regulator depends on — that next year's rerun matches this year's — rather than assuming the deterministic ordering is enough.

›deepHow is narrative faithfulness tested per sentence?

A faithfulness suite runs the claim grammar over each factual sentence on seeded chains where the supportable claims are known, asserting that every sentence decomposes into allowed templates backed by cited node fields and edges and that unsupportable sentences yield narrative_suppressed. The bar is "suppress, don't soften," so a fixture that plants an uncitable claim must produce suppression, not a weakened variant. Testing per sentence rather than per paragraph is what catches a single overreaching clause hiding in otherwise-supported prose.

›adversarialGolden chains are seeded fixtures. How do you know they resemble real chronic histories?

They are constructed to reproduce the structural features real chronic histories have — long windows, multiple protocol sets, corrections, supersessions, same-timestamp collisions, missing projection edges — rather than clean happy-path chains, precisely so the suite exercises what production throws at it. That said, seeded fixtures cannot prove behaviour on the full messiness of nine years of real data, and the honest position is that they test the known hard cases well and the unknown ones not at all. This is a real limitation, not a solved problem, and it is why no measured production accuracy is claimed.

›deepWhat is the clinician latency acceptance target, and what about degraded responses?

The pre-registered targets are a P99 under 500 ms on a cache hit and under 1.5 s cold, with degraded responses required to return within the same budget rather than hanging. The budget is tight because the chain has to open inside a live consultation. Crucially these are the targets the system was designed and reviewed against — not measured production figures, which are not published here.

›adversarialAll of these are targets. Is anything actually measured and published?

No measured production numbers are published here — every latency, depth, and quality figure on this page and its case study is a pre-registered acceptance target the system was designed, reviewed, and deployed against, not an observed result. The observed outcomes are not mine to publish. This is a deliberate honesty constraint: it would be easy and misleading to dress a target up as a measurement, so the portfolio states targets as targets and stops there.

›deepWhy is tenant safety an eval bar of "zero / page-out" rather than a percentage?

Because a cross-tenant node on a returned chain is a reportable privacy incident, and a percentage target would implicitly sanction a nonzero rate of them. The bar is that any such node is an incident that pages a human, so the acceptance criterion is a hard zero backed by the response-time assertion that fails the request rather than serving a leak. Some failures are best expressed as invariants, not rates, and tenant isolation in healthcare is one of them.

12 Tradeoffs and what I would do differently

›deepWhy not snapshot the graph periodically and serve the regulator from snapshots instead of spine replay?

Snapshots only let you replay the instants you happened to snapshot; a regulator can ask about any moment, including one between snapshots, so you would either store impractically many or return an approximation. Reconstructing from the append-only spine at an arbitrary anchor answers any instant exactly, with correction and supersedes lineage applied. The cost is that reconstruction is more work than reading a snapshot, which is why it is the regulator path's job and not the clinician's. For point-in-time audit, exact-any-instant beats fast-some-instants.

›deepWhy not make the clinician path also spine-deterministic for consistency?

Because the clinician needs "what is true right now" at bedside latency, and reconstructing from the spine on every panel open would blow the sub-500-ms budget for no clinical benefit — the clinician does not need byte-identical replay, they need current and fast. Using the live projected graph, bounded and cached, is the right tool for that job. Forcing one engine on both audiences is exactly the mistake the first design made; the two-product split is the correction. Consistency of implementation is not worth degrading both audiences to serve one contract.

›adversarialTwo products doubles the surface area. Was the split worth the operational cost?

It is a real cost — two endpoints, two RBAC roles, two sets of limits and audit paths, two failure modes to run. But the cost is bounded because they share the seed contract and the narrative guardrail, so the duplication is at the edges, not the core, and the products fail independently, which is itself an operational benefit. The alternative — one pipeline — was reviewed and judged unable to meet both bars, so the split is not gratuitous surface area; it is the minimum structure that makes both correctness contracts satisfiable at once.

›deepCould a deterministic template renderer replace the LLM narrator entirely — what would you gain and lose?

Yes, and it is a live alternative, because the chain already carries every field a template would need — drug, evidence value, policy threshold, supersession. You would gain true output determinism and remove the PHI-boundary and drift concerns entirely. You would lose fluency and the ability to phrase varied chains naturally, and you would trade a claim-grammar guardrail for a fixed set of sentence templates. The current design keeps the LLM for its readability while confining it behind the same claim grammar a template would enforce, so the two are closer than they look — the honest answer is that a template is a reasonable fallback the design is structured to allow.

›deepWhat was platform-gated and could not be closed by this KQ alone?

Several shared contracts: a standardised tenant-scoped traversal-source wrapper, a shared correction- event backbone with decision-write invalidation, a centralised four-field event-time schema in the spine, and — used by this query and the cross-domain drill-in query — a signed shared narrative-guardrail contract. The regulator path additionally depended on spine indexing being in production. All of these have since landed except the narrative-guardrail contract, which remains unsigned: the query now binds to the shared wrapper, backbone, event-time schema, and spine index, while the synthesis narrator ships off by default until the guardrail contract is signed. KQ9 designed its own versions first so each was specifiable and testable, and carried every dependency explicitly rather than assuming it.

›deepWhat would you do differently with hindsight?

Bring the two-product split to the very first design review instead of arriving with the single pipeline that the review sent back for redesign — the structural conflict between "right now" and "back then" was discoverable up front, and I now treat "does one artifact serve two burdens of proof" as a question to ask before drawing the architecture. I would also freeze the shared seed contract and the narrative-guardrail contract earlier, since both turned out to be the real coupling points across products and across queries. The redesign was the right outcome; it just did not need to be a redesign.

›adversarialWhat is the single biggest residual risk in this design?

The dependency chain under the regulator product: its determinism guarantee rests on spine indexing, the correction-event backbone, and the four-field event-time schema all being correct and in production, and a defect in any of those upstream contracts would undermine replayability in a way KQ9 cannot fully compensate for on its own. The mitigations — export hashes, replay instructions, the deterministic cursor — make such a defect detectable, but detection is not prevention. That is the honest residual risk, and it is why those platform items are named as gating dependencies rather than glossed over.

Honest gaps

What this query cannot claim today

The real design-review verdict was redesign. The first design — a single live-graph pipeline serving both the clinician panel and the regulator export — was returned by a structured adversarial review with one unambiguous recommendation: redesign, behind two structural blockers, thirteen high-severity findings, and six mediums. The v2 described throughout this page is that redesign: the two-product split is the answer to the verdict, and telling it as anything softer would be dishonest. Every blocker and high is dispositioned — most to a named mechanism, the rest carried openly as tracked platform dependencies.

The shared backbone, and the one contract still open. KQ9 first built its own tenant scoping, cache-key completeness, correction invalidation, event-time semantics, and narrative guardrail. The corresponding shared platform contracts — a standardised tenant-scoped traversal-source wrapper, a correction-event backbone, a centralised four-field event-time schema, and spine indexing for the regulator path — have since landed, and the query now binds to them rather than its own inlined copies. The one exception is the signed shared narrative-guardrail contract, still unsigned: tenancy, correction invalidation, and event-time now rest on a platform-wide guarantee, while the narrative guardrail stays "partial" and the synthesis narrator ships off by default until the contract is signed.

What the query genuinely cannot answer. It reconstructs the decision reasoning a clinician recorded; it does not certify that the reasoning was correct or that the treatment worked, and it makes no causal claim about outcomes. The language model narrating the chain is not made deterministic by temperature-zero — the hashes detect drift, they do not prevent it — so determinism lives in the structured chain, never in the prose. Governance mitigations make turning the attribution into a clinician scorecard hard and visible, not impossible. And the evaluation rests on seeded golden fixtures whose coverage of the known hard cases is strong but which cannot prove behaviour across the full messiness of nine years of real data.
