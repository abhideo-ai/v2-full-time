Ten killer queries, one spine

A chronic-care platform covering 80+ conditions, carrying 9–10 years of patient history. Ten analytics and machine-learning services, all shipped, all built on the same architecture and the same discipline: deterministic code owns every number, and a language model is only ever allowed at a gated edge.

The ten

KQ4Early-signal detection Which patients show the ordered 30-day run-up of events that precedes a relapse? Never scores an outcome window that has not matured. Sorts by (event_time_ms, source_event_seq, event_id) so two events in one millisecond cannot yield two scores.
KQ5Decision latency How long from a high-risk lab result arriving to the treatment decision that answers it? Unanswered labs stay in the denominator as right-censored observations rather than being quietly dropped.
KQ6AI versus clinician When a clinician accepted an AI suggestion versus rejected it, what was the 90-day outcome delta? Held dark until an upstream team shipped the audit trail that made the comparison honest — a system that knew which question it was not yet entitled to answer.
KQ7Confidence calibration Was a clinician's own stated certainty borne out by the 90-day outcome? Immature decisions are not merely excluded — they are not yet countable, which is a different and stricter thing.
KQ8Policy friction Per policy version, how often did clinicians override each rule, and how did those patients do? Emits only six neutral zone codes, deliberately engineered to resist being turned into a staff scorecard.
KQ9Causal path Why is this patient on this medication plan — answered for a clinician at the bedside, and for a regulator asking about an exact past instant? Two products, one question: a bounded live graph walk, and a byte-deterministic replay from the event spine.

The shared spine

All ten sit on the same stack. Being able to draw this from memory, in order, is worth more in a room than any individual query.

  1. DynamoDB — an append-only event spine. The source of truth; nothing is ever updated in place, so any past instant can be reconstructed.
  2. Amazon Neptune — the decision graph, traversed with Gremlin. Relationships between decisions, patients, protocols and outcomes.
  3. Parquet snapshots — frozen nightly. A query reads a snapshot, not the live graph, so the same question asked twice gives the same answer.
  4. DuckDB — columnar analytics over those snapshots.
  5. FastAPI (+ Redis where a cache earns its place) — audit-first serving, tenant asserted on every row, canonical cache keys carrying every version pin.
  6. LangGraph / LangChain, with Ollama and self-hosted LangSmith inside the virtual private cloud (VPC) — used at gated edges only. Several queries call no model at all.

The one-sentence version: deterministic, seeded, replayable code computes every number; a language model may propose candidates or narrate figures the code already produced, and only after every gate has passed.

The vocabulary this work runs on

These are the terms a Staff or Principal interviewer will pull on. Each one below is what it means, why it is in this system, and the question it invites.

Causal inference and statistics

Confounding

Did the treatment help, or were the treated patients already healthier?

When something other than the intervention explains the difference in outcomes. The whole reason KQ2, KQ3, KQ6 and KQ8 exist in the shape they do.

Here: every covariate is rebuilt as-of the decision from a frozen snapshot, so a comparison never uses information that did not exist when the decision was made.

Expect: "How do you know the difference you measured isn't just selection?"

Propensity score · IPW

Matching like with like, then weighting what is left

A propensity score is the modelled probability a patient would have received the treatment given their covariates. Match on it and you compare comparable patients. IPW — inverse probability weighting — instead re-weights the sample so the treated and untreated groups look alike on average.

Here: matching produces the pairs; IPW is the headline switch once balance is achieved. Appears in KQ3, KQ6 and KQ8.

Expect: "Why match and weight? When does matching alone fail?"

SMD · balance

Standardised mean difference — the check that matching actually worked

The difference in a covariate's mean between two groups, divided by the pooled standard deviation, so it is comparable across variables with different units. Below roughly 0.1 is the conventional threshold for "balanced".

Here: the first rung of the gate ladder. Fail it and the headline number is literally unrepresentable in the output type — a design choice worth stating exactly that way, because it means the failure cannot be ignored downstream.

Expect: "What's your balance threshold, and what happens when you miss it?"

Caliper

How far apart two matched patients are allowed to be

The maximum distance on the propensity score at which two patients may still be paired. Too wide and you match dissimilar patients; too narrow and you discard most of your sample.

Here: caliper stability is its own gate — the result must not swing when the caliper is varied, which is a stronger claim than simply picking one and reporting it.

Expect: "How did you choose it, and does the answer survive changing it?"

Right-censoring · survival analysis

The patients whose event has not happened yet

A censored observation is one where you know the event has not occurred by a given time but not whether it ever will. Dropping them biases the result — the ones who take longest are exactly the ones you lose. Kaplan–Meier is the standard estimator that keeps them in.

Here: KQ5's decision-latency denominator is ungated, carrying unanswered labs as right-censored rather than silently excluding them.

Expect: "What did you do with labs that were never answered?" — the honest answer is the whole point of the design.

Bootstrap · seeded

Confidence intervals by resampling, made reproducible

Resample the data with replacement many times, recompute the statistic each time, and read the spread as a confidence interval. It needs no distributional assumption. Seeded means the random draw is fixed, so the same input always yields the same interval.

Here: every interval in the programme is seeded and replayable. An unreproducible confidence interval in a clinical setting is not evidence.

Expect: "Why bootstrap rather than a closed-form interval?"

Calibration · Brier score

When you say 70% confident, does it happen 70% of the time?

Calibration is agreement between stated confidence and observed frequency. A reliability diagram plots one against the other; the Brier score summarises it as mean squared error between predicted probability and outcome. A model can rank well and still be badly calibrated.

Here: KQ7 applies this to humans — checking clinicians' own low/medium/high confidence against 90-day outcomes.

Expect: "Discrimination versus calibration — why do you care about both?"

Small-cell suppression · power

Refusing to answer when the data cannot carry it

Below a minimum count, a rate is both statistically meaningless and re-identifying. Suppression withholds it. Power analysis asks the prior question: is this sample even large enough to detect the effect you are looking for?

Here: a 30-patient floor, and a power gate on the ladder. The underlying rows stay available in a drill-down, so a genuine association resurfaces once the data supports it.

Expect: "What does the user see when you suppress, and why not just show it?"

Pre-registration

Committing to the analysis before seeing the result

Fixing windows, thresholds and comparisons in advance, so the analysis cannot be tuned until it says something interesting. The defence against p-hacking and window-shopping.

Here: pre-registered windows and evaluation floors appear across the programme — 117 mentions in the source material.

Expect: "What stopped you from moving the window until the result looked good?"

Retrieval and machine learning

As-of featurisation · leakage

Reconstructing the world as it was, not as it is

Leakage is information from the future reaching a model or comparison about the past. As-of featurisation is the defence: rebuild every feature using only what was known at that instant.

Here: the single most-used idea in the material — 394 mentions. A latest-BMI lookup was a P0 leakage finding, now enforced by feature-freshness audit fields carried on every row. This is the strongest answer available for "tell me about a subtle bug you found."

Expect: "How do you know your training set doesn't contain the future?"

Filtered kNN · hybrid retrieval · NDCG

Hard filters first, similarity second

k-nearest-neighbour search finds the closest vectors. Filtered kNN applies structured constraints first, so similarity only ranks within an already-valid set. NDCG — normalised discounted cumulative gain — scores ranking quality with position weighting.

Here: clinical filters are contracts. A soft weight would let a very similar protocol-B decision leak into a protocol-A result. Hard filters also keep empty-result rate and NDCG separately measurable, which a blended score would hide.

Expect: "Why not just weight the clinical criteria into the similarity score?"

Outcome maturity · cold start

Some labels are not ready to be labels

An outcome window that has not elapsed cannot yet be a positive or a negative. Treating it as negative teaches the model that recent cases are safe. Cold start is the mirror problem: a new patient or tenant has no history to retrieve.

Here: KQ4 never trains on or scores an unmatured window; KQ7 treats immature decisions as not countable. KQ1 has an explicit cold-start quality gate.

Expect: "What's your positive label, exactly — and when does it become one?"

Data engineering discipline

Canonical cache keys

A key that omits something is a correctness bug in a performance optimisation's clothing

Every input that can change the result belongs in the key: tenant, patient, window, tokenizer version, model version, snapshot manifest hash, last event sequence. Built from sorted JSON so field ordering cannot cause hash divergence.

Here: the sharpest hands-on detail in the material. KQ3 pins the matcher binary and the statistical plan as their own key fields; KQ4 orders a live model swap so the cache namespace is invalidated before the endpoint is repointed — reverse it and a coach reads a score from a model that no longer exists.

Expect: "Walk me through invalidation." Then: "What if two things change at once?"

Freshness over TTL

"How old is this?" is the wrong question

A time-to-live answers how old an entry is. The query needs to know whether it is still correct. Content freshness — snapshot hash plus correction epoch plus maximum source sequence — answers that directly.

Here: stated as an explicit rejection of TTL caching, with the reasoning given. KQ4 still uses a 6-hour TTL where staleness is genuinely bounded — knowing when each applies is the point.

Expect: "Why not just cache for five minutes?"

Event-time versus processing-time

Four timestamps, two you trust

event_time_ms and observed_at_ms come from the record itself. received_at_ms and written_at_ms describe your pipeline, not the world.

Here: the untrusted pair is pinned to a load-fence constant and survives only as provenance, with a load-failing assertion that event time precedes receive time on every historical row.

Expect: "What happens when events arrive out of order?"

Determinism and replay

The same question, asked twice, gives the same answer

Frozen snapshots, seeded randomness, versioned computation, stable tie-breaking. KQ9 goes further to byte-deterministic replay — a regulator can reconstruct an exact past instant years later.

Here: KQ4 sorts by (event_time_ms, source_event_seq, event_id) before tokenisation, because two events sharing a millisecond would otherwise produce two different token strings and two different scores on replay.

Expect: "How would you reproduce a number a clinician saw six months ago?"

Governance and multi-tenancy

Tenant isolation

Asserted on every row, not checked at the door

In a multi-tenant system, isolation enforced only at the API boundary fails the moment an internal path bypasses it. Row-level assertion fails closed instead.

Here: three-field tenant isolation, asserted per row; cache namespaces are per-tenant. This connects directly to the axe Monitor tenant-isolation work at Deque — the same instinct, two companies apart.

Expect: "How would a cross-tenant read happen, and what stops it?"

Designing against misuse

Building an analytic that resists becoming a staff scorecard

Per-clinician analytics invite performance ranking. The design answer is structural: pseudonymous identifiers, audit-first access, deliberately unrankable views, and neutral output vocabulary.

Here: KQ8 emits only six neutral zone codes; KQ5's per-clinician views are explicitly unrankable. Governance designed to resist a use the data would otherwise permit.

Expect: "Who could misuse this, and what did you do about it?" — a question most candidates have no answer to at all.

Adversarial design review

Reviews that returned "do not implement as written"

Structured self-review under a named lens before any production code. Verdicts across the programme included ship-with-changes, redesign, and — hardest — block-on-prereq: "you do not yet have the data to answer this honestly, so do not build it."

Here: KQ10 absorbed twenty findings into a hardened v2; KQ2 sixteen; KQ3 nineteen; KQ8 seventeen; KQ6 all thirteen of thirteen. KQ6's block-on-prereq was later met and the query went live.

Expect: "Tell me about design feedback you received." The strongest version of this answer is the review you ran on yourself and then obeyed.
