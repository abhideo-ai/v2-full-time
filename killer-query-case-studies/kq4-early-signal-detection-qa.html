Deep-dive Q&A · Early-warning sequence model

Before a patient relapses, can the warning signs be caught in time?

This query mines the ordered sequence of clinical events in the thirty days before a known relapse, learns which run-ups precede one, and scores active patients nightly so a coach can be warned while there is still time to act. It is the highest-value and slowest-to-mature query in the platform, because its labels — confirmed relapses — accrue over months, not minutes. The answers below are the questions a senior engineer or CTO tends to ask once they realise the model is the easy part and the labels, the windowing, and the time geometry are where a system like this lives or dies.

01 · The question and why it is hard

What the query is really asking, and why a relapse detector is not a normal classifier.

screeningWhat question does this query actually answer?

It answers a retrospective and a prospective version of the same thing. Retrospectively: for patients who went on to relapse, what ordered signals showed up in the thirty days beforehand, and did anyone act on them? Prospectively: for an active patient right now, does the recent sequence of events resemble those pre-relapse run-ups strongly enough to warn a coach. The second only earns trust because the first is answerable on hydrated history with known outcomes.

screeningWhy thirty days, and is the window fixed?

Thirty days is the pre-registered observation window — long enough to capture a developing run-up (missed doses, glucose drift, restriction violations) yet short enough that the signal is actionable rather than a background trait. The window length is a design parameter carried on every derived row and in the cache key, so it can be re-tuned per programme without silently mixing windows of different lengths in one training set. It is fixed per model version, not per request: changing it is a retrain, not a query flag.

screeningWhy is this called the slowest-to-mature query in the portfolio?

Every other query can run the day its inputs exist. This one cannot, because its supervision is a confirmed relapse, and a relapse only becomes a usable label after enough follow-up time has passed to know the outcome actually resolved. The design fixes a floor of roughly six months of accrued relapse_detected outcomes before any model is trusted, which is why this query is built last in the platform’s build order.

deepWhy is early-warning relapse detection harder than a typical binary classifier?

Three things stack. The positive class is rare (relapses run roughly 5–15% of at-risk windows), so label count, not algorithm, is the binding constraint. The signal is temporal — it lives in the order and spacing of events, not a static feature vector. And the label is time-to-event, not a clean 0/1: a patient who relapses on day thirty-one looks identical to a never-relapser inside a thirty-day window. A classifier that ignores any of those three learns the wrong thing confidently.

deepWhy does the order of events matter more than their presence?

Presence-only features collapse a trajectory into a bag: a missed dose, a glucose excursion, and a second missed dose become three counts with no shape. But the clinical meaning is in the shape — a missed dose followed by rising glucose followed by another missed dose is a deteriorating spiral, whereas the same three events scattered and self-corrected are noise. Sequence mining preserves that order, which is exactly what a tabular model discards, and it is why the top explanatory patterns are written as ordered chains rather than feature weights.

deepWhy are the labels the bottleneck rather than the model?

You can swap PrefixSpan for a transformer in an afternoon, but you cannot manufacture a confirmed relapse. With a sparse positive class and a maturity gate that discards outcomes whose follow-up window has not fully run, the effective training set is small and grows only with calendar time. That reality drives the whole design: the maturity gate, the hazard framing that is more sample-efficient than binary 0/1, and per-tenant calibration all exist to extract signal from few labels rather than to chase a fancier architecture.

adversarialIsn’t this just a churn model wearing medical vocabulary? What is genuinely hard?

Structurally it rhymes with churn, and the transferable spine is real — an order-dependent run-up before a rare, expensive event. What churn rarely forces is the label discipline: a relapse is a clinical outcome that must mature before it is trusted, a false negative can mean a hospitalisation, and acting on the alert changes the very outcome you are trying to predict. Those three — maturity, cost asymmetry, and the self-fulfilling feedback loop — are what turn a churn-shaped problem into a system that has to be argued for, not just trained.

adversarialIf relapses are only 5–15%, why not alert everyone and let coaches triage?

Because “alert everyone” is precisely the baseline the model must beat, and it is a bad one: it spends the scarcest resource in the system — coach attention — on the 85–95% who will not relapse, and within weeks every alert is ignored. The acceptance bar is a lift of at least three times the base rate and a per-coach daily alert budget, so a model that cannot concentrate risk better than paging the whole panel is rejected. The point of the query is triage the humans cannot do by eye, not a firehose they will learn to mute.

02 · Architecture and data flow

How a relapse event on the spine becomes a warning on a coach’s screen.

screeningWhat are the moving parts, end to end?

An append-only event spine holds the ground-truth events and the canonical relapse outcome. A projector writes a graph and, separately, an ungated observation snapshot. A nightly exporter freezes a point-in-time Parquet snapshot. A sequence builder turns that snapshot into ordered token windows with labels. A sequence model (logistic baseline, then pattern miner, then transformer) scores those windows in bulk. An alert-lifecycle service turns scores into owned, acknowledgeable alerts, and a cache plus a small API serve the result to the coach UI. Every stage runs inside the tenant’s VPC.

screeningWhere does the model actually run?

On a self-hosted, in-VPC managed-training-and-inference stack. Training and the nightly bulk scoring run as batch jobs; a live endpoint exists only to serve an on-demand coach check on a cache miss. No patient data leaves the client’s network at any stage, because the sequence model is a small model over discrete event tokens, not a call to any external service.

deepWhy is scoring a nightly batch job rather than a live per-request traversal?

Because the alternative — walking the graph per patient on demand — does not scale and is not reproducible. The design pre-materialises thirty-day windows for all active patients from the frozen snapshot and runs one batch transform, explicitly forbidding per-patient graph scans. That makes the nightly cost predictable, lets the result be pinned to a single snapshot hash, and means a coach’s on-demand check is almost always a cache read that was prewarmed overnight rather than a cold, variable-latency traversal.

deepWhy read a separate observation snapshot instead of just the decision graph?

Because the projector historically emitted an observation only when a decision referenced it, which means the graph is silently biased toward cases where someone already responded. For a query whose entire job is to find the tail where no one reacted yet, that bias is fatal. So the builder reads an ungated observation snapshot — one not conditioned on decision attachment — so a pre-relapse drift with no follow-up decision is still visible. This is the single largest data exposure across the platform’s queries, and it is why the ungated projection is a named platform dependency.

deepWhat does the sequence builder produce, and where is it stored?

It produces a Parquet dataset of sequences: for each anchor (a matured relapse, or an at-risk negative window), an ordered, tokenised list of the events in the window, plus the label status, the tokenizer version, the snapshot hash, and the source event-sequence range. It is partitioned by the three tenant fields and the snapshot hash, and every row carries a dependency set so a later correction can invalidate exactly the rows it touched. Nothing downstream re-derives ordering — the deterministic sort happens once, here, before tokenisation.

deepHow does an on-demand coach check differ from the nightly batch?

Same model, same cache key, different trigger. The nightly job scores the whole active panel and prewarms the cache. When a coach opens a patient, the API writes an audit entry, builds the canonical cache key, and reads the prewarmed score; only on a genuine miss does it build that one patient’s window, call the live endpoint under a single-flight lock, and cache the result. The response also joins in the actions already taken from the spine, so the coach sees not just “risk is high” but “and here is what has already been done.”

deepWhy does the alert lifecycle emit its own events back onto the spine?

Because an alert is not a UI state, it is a clinical fact with a history. Creating, acknowledging, dismissing, and escalating an alert each land back on the append-only spine as first-class events. That gives you an auditable record of who was warned and what they did, it lets the response-window join reconstruct “a decision was made in response” from immutable sequence numbers rather than a mutable status column, and it closes the loop the query’s own wording asks about — were decisions made in response.

adversarialSnapshot-only scoring means a coach can be looking at yesterday’s risk. Is that acceptable for a clinical alert?

It is acceptable for this class of signal and it is made explicit rather than hidden. A thirty-day run-up does not turn on a dime overnight, so a nightly cadence matches the physiology, and the freshness of every score is stamped with the snapshot age and manifest hash so no one mistakes a stale score for a live one. The degradation tiers are enforced: under 24 hours is green, 24–48 hours is yellow, past 48 hours is red. What this query is deliberately not is a seconds-fresh vital-sign monitor — if a patient acutely deteriorates, that is a different system, and the honest gaps say so.

adversarialDynamoDB, Neptune, Parquet, SageMaker, Postgres, Redis — isn’t that over-engineered for a relapse flag?

Each store earns its place by doing a job the others cannot. The spine is the immutable ledger; the graph is the projection; Parquet is the frozen, hashable point-in-time state that makes scoring reproducible; the batch transform is the bulk scorer; Postgres holds the explainable patterns and the alert lifecycle with row-level tenancy; the cache serves coaches in milliseconds. Collapse any of them and you lose either reproducibility, isolation, explainability, or latency. The genuinely removable piece is the live endpoint — it exists only for cache-miss checks — and that is the one part kept deliberately small.

03 · As-of correctness and future leakage

The centre of the system is not the model — it is the time geometry that decides what counts as evidence.

screeningWhat is the single biggest way this pipeline could cheat?

By letting the future leak into the past. In sequence mining that happens in three quiet ways: a negative window that is actually a pre-relapse run-up, a train/test split that puts a patient’s later events in training and earlier ones in test, and a feature computed from a value observed after the moment being scored. Each is invisible in a metric and each inflates it. The design names and guards all three, because an early-warning model that looks brilliant in backtest and useless in production almost always leaked.

deepHow does negative sampling avoid labelling a pre-relapse run-up as “healthy”?

Negatives are drawn from active patient time-at-risk, not from “any window without a relapse.” For every patient, the sampler removes two zones before drawing: the thirty-day pre-event window and a ninety-day future-relapse buffer around any relapse that patient later has. So a window that sits inside a developing run-up is not handed to the model as a confident zero. Get this wrong and the model is taught that the exact pattern it should fire on is normal — the most damaging single bug in this archetype.

deepWhy a ninety-day future-relapse exclusion buffer specifically, and what does it cost?

The buffer has to be wider than the observation window so that windows drawn near an upcoming relapse — which carry early signal but sit outside the strict thirty-day mark — are not mislabelled as negatives. Ninety days is the pre-registered choice, deliberately generous. The cost is real: excluding those windows discards some of the hardest, most informative negatives and shrinks an already small training set. That trade — cleaner labels over more labels — is made on purpose, and the buffer width is an open tuning question, not a proven optimum.

deepHow is a random-split leak prevented in the trainer?

By an assertion in the training pipeline, not a convention in a notebook. The backtest is time-cut only: the build fails if any validation event-time is at or before the maximum training event-time, and it fails again if any patient or enrollment appears on both sides of the fold. A random k-fold would let a patient’s later week teach the model about their earlier week, which is leakage dressed as generalisation. Making it a hard assertion means the pipeline stops rather than quietly reporting an inflated number.

deepWhy sort events deterministically before tokenising?

Because tokenisation turns an ordered list into the model’s input, and if that order is even slightly non-deterministic — two events with the same millisecond, say — the same patient can produce two different token strings and two different scores on two replays. The builder sorts by (event_time_ms, source_event_seq, event_id) once, before tokenising, so ties are broken by immutable identifiers. That is what lets the same snapshot hash reproduce the same sequences, and it ties directly into the deterministic-exporter guarantee the whole snapshot chain depends on.

deepThe “risk level was raised” signal is powerful and leaky — how is it kept honest?

“A clinician upgraded this patient to high risk” is never used as a label, only as a feature, and only behind an event-time guard. If it were a label the model would learn to predict the clinicians who react rather than the relapses themselves — proxy-label circularity, flagged as a high-severity item in review. As a feature it is admitted only when its event-time and source-sequence sit strictly before the moment being scored, so a reaction that happened after the window cannot be read back into it. The model predicts outcomes, not the people watching them.

adversarialTime-cut splitting still lets the tokenizer see the whole corpus vocabulary — isn’t that leakage?

It is the honest edge of the design and worth naming plainly. Vocabulary leakage — the token set being derived from the full corpus, including the future — is subtler than value leakage and the mitigation is a versioned, frozen tokenizer whose vocabulary is fixed at train time and monitored for drift, rather than re-fit per scoring run. That reduces the exposure but does not make it zero: a rare token that only appears post-cutoff would still be unknown to a strictly time-honest tokenizer. The design treats tokenizer construction as a versioned artifact with drift triggers precisely because this is not fully closed.

adversarialIf you exclude the ninety-day pre-relapse zone from negatives, aren’t you discarding the hardest cases?

You are discarding the hardest negatives, and that is a deliberate, uncomfortable trade. Those near-relapse windows are the most informative to learn from but the most dangerous to mislabel, because their true status is ambiguous — signal is present, the outcome just has not landed. The design chooses label purity over label volume, accepting a smaller, cleaner training set rather than a larger one seeded with mislabelled hard cases. Whether ninety days is the right frontier of that trade is explicitly left open for tuning, not asserted as solved.

adversarialA patient who relapses on day thirty-one is a zero in a thirty-day window — doesn’t that poison training?

It would, under a naive binary framing, and it is exactly why the design does not use one. Binary 0/1 treats a day-thirty-one relapser and a never-relapser as identical, throwing away that the first was days from the edge. The model is instead framed as a discrete-time hazard on matured rows, so the question becomes “what is the risk in the next interval” rather than “did it happen inside an arbitrary thirty-day box.” The binary number survives only as an operating-point summary for reporting, never as the loss the model is trained on.

04 · Tenancy and isolation

One forgotten predicate is a cross-tenant leak that propagates into every downstream artifact.

screeningHow is one tenant’s data kept out of another’s model?

By belt-and-braces isolation rather than per-query care. A tenant context is threaded through the whole stack: the snapshot builder filters at the Parquet scan and partitions output by tenant, the scoring request carries a tenant assertion, Postgres uses row-level tenant keys, the cache is namespaced per tenant, and a fail-closed assertion checks that every served cell carries the caller’s scope. Any one of those failing does not silently pass; it stops.

screeningWhat are the tenant fields?

Tenant identity is a three-field scope — account_id, workspace_id, and controlunit_id — resolved from the caller’s authenticated identity, never passed in the request body. Every derived artifact, cache key, and partition uses all three. A single-field check would be a real hole: two workspaces under one account, or two control units under one workspace, would be treated as one tenant.

deepWhere exactly is tenancy enforced across the stack?

At five points, deliberately redundant. The DuckDB predicate filters the snapshot before any join runs; the Parquet output is partitioned on the three tenant fields plus the snapshot hash; the batch and live scoring requests carry the tenant context and assert the response matches; Postgres pattern and alert rows are keyed by tenant; and Redis keys are namespaced. The response-time assertion is the backstop — if any returned cell lacks the caller’s scope, it fails closed rather than returning a possibly-cross-tenant row.

deepWhat does a fail-closed tenant assertion do, and why per-cell?

Fail-closed means the default on any ambiguity is to refuse, not to serve. Per-cell means the check is on every returned score and pattern row, not once at the top of the request, because the failure mode being defended against is a single row from the wrong tenant slipping through an otherwise correct query. A top-level check would pass while one contaminated cell rode along; a per-cell check turns that into a hard error. The cost is a cheap comparison per row against the certainty that no cross-tenant cell is ever surfaced.

deepIs the model per-tenant or global, and how does that interact with isolation?

It is a global model with per-tenant calibration, until a tenant crosses a minimum effective event count. The model’s weights are shared — that is a pooled-learning decision, not a data-isolation breach, because no tenant can read another’s rows — but the operating threshold and calibration are fit per tenant and programme, so a tenant with a different base rate gets a threshold tuned to its own reality rather than a global average that over- or under-alerts.

deepHow is the batch scoring job kept tenant-safe when it scores everyone at once?

The nightly job pins a tenant context per partition and scans active patients one tenant partition at a time, so the payload sent to the batch transform is already scoped, and the response is asserted back against that scope before any alert is created. Because the snapshot is partitioned by tenant, “score everyone” is really “score each tenant’s partition,” not one undifferentiated pool. Alerts and prewarmed cache entries are then written under each tenant’s namespace.

adversarialA shared global model trains on all tenants’ sequences — isn’t that already a cross-tenant leak?

This is the fair question to press on, and the honest answer distinguishes two things. Data isolation — no tenant can retrieve another’s rows, scores, or patterns — is enforced end to end. Statistical pooling — the model’s weights are informed by all tenants — is a modelling choice made because label scarcity would leave most single tenants unable to train at all. It is not zero-risk: a global model can memorise a rare pattern. The mitigations are that explanations are drawn from a per-tenant pattern store and outputs are per-tenant calibrated, but pooled learning is a deliberate trade, and a client who requires fully siloed model weights would move this to per-tenant models at a real cost in label efficiency.

adversarialWhat stops one forgotten predicate in a DuckDB join from silently pooling tenants?

Nothing stops the mistake from being written — humans forget predicates — but the design is built so the mistake cannot reach a coach silently. The tenant filter runs at the scan before joins, the output partition would land in the wrong path, and the response-time per-cell assertion fails closed on any row without the caller’s scope. The intent is that a missed predicate becomes a loud failure at serve time rather than a quiet contamination that propagates into the next night’s training set. It is defence in depth precisely because per-query discipline is the thing you cannot rely on.

05 · The core mechanism — sequence models

Tokens, pattern mining, a transformer that must earn its place, and hazard rather than a coin flip.

screeningWhat is a “token” here?

A token is a discretised clinical event — a missed dose, a glucose reading bucketed into a band, a restriction violation, a weight change past a threshold. The thirty-day window becomes an ordered string of these tokens. The model reads the string, not raw values, which is why the token vocabulary and its bucketing are first-class, versioned decisions rather than incidental preprocessing.

screeningWhat are PrefixSpan and the transformer actually doing?

PrefixSpan is a sequential-pattern miner: it finds ordered sub-sequences that occur more often before relapses than by chance, which gives directly readable patterns like “missed dose → glucose high → missed dose.” A transformer over the same tokens can capture longer-range and non-contiguous dependencies a pattern miner misses. Both are ordinary sequence models over event tokens — neither is a language model — and the design tries the interpretable one first.

deepWhy the three-way promotion gate instead of just training the best model?

Because “best on a metric” is the wrong objective for a clinical alerter. The gate is three rungs — a logistic baseline sets the floor, PrefixSpan must beat it on calibrated precision/recall at the operating point, and only then may a transformer run, and only if it beats PrefixSpan. A rung that does not clear the one below does not ship, so complexity is admitted only where it pays. This is the “know when not to reach for the fancier model” discipline made into a build rule rather than a preference.

deepWhy does the transformer carry an extra bar beyond precision and recall?

Because in a clinical setting an unexplained alert is a weaker product than a slightly-less-accurate explainable one. The transformer must not only beat PrefixSpan on calibrated precision/recall, it must also preserve explanation fidelity — its attribution has to remain faithful enough that the top patterns shown to a coach genuinely reflect what drove the score. A model that wins on the metric but turns the explanation into a black box has not earned its opacity, and the gate holds the previous model.

deepWhat is discrete-time hazard framing, and why not plain binary 0/1?

Discrete-time hazard models the probability of relapse in the next interval given no relapse yet, across a sequence of intervals, rather than a single yes/no over a fixed box. It is more sample-efficient with sparse labels because each surviving interval contributes information, and it stops treating a near-miss relapser as identical to a never-relapser. Immature and censored rows are excluded from the likelihood or handled by inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting rather than being dropped in as zeros, so drop-outs and program switches do not masquerade as clean negatives.

deepHow are the “top patterns” that explain a score produced?

Attribution — sequential-pattern lift for PrefixSpan, or attention/feature attribution for the transformer — is computed by the same non-LLM pipeline that produced the score, and the top contributing ordered patterns are surfaced with a weight. They are stored per tenant and model version in the pattern store so the same score always explains itself the same way. No language model writes these; they are the model’s own internals, rendered, not narrated.

deepWhat is the tokenizer and why is it versioned?

The tokenizer is the map from raw events to the discrete vocabulary the model reads — the bucketing of a continuous glucose reading, the naming of an event class. It is versioned because a change in that map silently changes every sequence: a device that reports at a new frequency or a new glucose scale shifts the token distribution, and a model trained on the old vocabulary would quietly mis-read the new one. The tokenizer version is persisted on every sequence, score, and cache key, and drift in vocabulary or frequency distribution triggers a retrain or recalibration.

adversarialA transformer on a few thousand sparse labels will overfit — why is it even on the menu?

Because it is on the menu conditionally, not by default, and the gate is designed to reject it when exactly this happens. If labels are too few, the transformer will not beat PrefixSpan on calibrated, time-cut, leakage-checked validation, and the promotion gate holds the simpler model — a fancier model that does not earn its opacity does not ship. The value of keeping it on the menu is that when labels do accrue over calendar time, the ceiling is already wired in without a redesign. Until then, the honest expectation is that the baseline or the pattern miner is what ships.

adversarialAttention weights are not explanations — how is “why this fired” trustworthy?

This is a legitimate limitation and the design does not pretend otherwise. Attention and attribution are indicative, not a causal account, which is one reason PrefixSpan — whose patterns are literally the sub-sequences it counts — is the preferred explainable rung, and why the transformer must clear an explanation-fidelity bar before it can displace it. The patterns are also labelled observational: a shown pattern is associated with relapse in the cohort, not proven to cause it. The trustworthy claim is “here is what the model weighted,” not “here is why the patient will relapse.”

06 · Statistics, gates and suppression

Calibration over raw accuracy, censoring done honestly, and every surface kept observational.

screeningWhat is the headline acceptance metric?

Precision at K days early, with a pre-registered target of at least 0.40 calibrated, paired with a lift over base rate of at least three times. The first says that when the model fires, it is right often enough to be worth a coach’s time; the second says it concentrates risk far better than paging everyone. Both are targets set before build, not results claimed after.

screeningWhy “calibrated” precision rather than raw precision?

Because raw precision can look healthy while masking that the model is miscalibrated on a particular tenant or programme — a global number averages over slices that behave very differently. Calibrated precision, measured per tenant and per programme, forces the score to mean the same thing across cohorts, so a threshold set on it behaves as intended everywhere rather than only on the majority slice.

deepWhy is lift over base rate the gate rather than raw accuracy?

Because with a 5–15% positive class, a model that predicts “no relapse” for everyone scores 85–95% accuracy and is worthless. Accuracy rewards the majority class; lift asks the only question that matters operationally — among the patients you flag, how much more concentrated is relapse than in the panel at large. A three-times lift floor is a direct statement that the model must beat the do-nothing triage baseline by a wide margin before it is allowed to consume coach attention.

deepHow are censored and immature outcomes handled statistically?

They are not counted as negatives. An enforced label-status enum — mature, not-mature, censored-dropout, program-switch, by-later-relapse — means only matured rows carry supervised labels, and censored cases are handled by Kaplan-Meier or inverse-probability weighting rather than being silently swept into the zero class. The trap this avoids is counting a patient who simply left the programme before day N as a “success,” which would inflate every rate and teach the model that disengagement looks like health.

deepWhat does per-tenant calibration buy, and what does it cost?

It buys slice fidelity: a threshold and reliability curve fit to each tenant and programme, so a cohort with a different base rate or documentation style is not scored against a foreign yardstick. The cost is statistical power — slicing spends your scarce labels, so a small tenant’s calibration is noisier than the global one. The design manages that by keeping a global model and only calibrating per slice once a tenant crosses a minimum effective event count, trading global power for local honesty deliberately.

deepWhy is coverage measured only on mature relapses?

Because coverage on all relapses would be measured against a denominator that includes outcomes the model was never allowed to learn from or score — immature and censored cases — which is not a fair or meaningful rate. The target of at least 80% coverage is defined on the matured relapse denominator, so it answers “of the relapses we can actually evaluate, how many did we catch,” not a diluted figure that quietly credits or penalises the model for cases outside its remit.

deepHow does the system avoid causal overclaiming?

By keeping every surface observational in wording and by refusing absolutes. A pattern shown to a coach is associated with relapse in the cohort, not proven to cause it; the buffering, calibration, and maturity gate reduce known biases but never rule out unmeasured confounding. The dashboard carries an observational tag, and the language is “this run-up preceded relapse in similar cases,” not “this will cause relapse.” Suppression over emission is the default: where the evidence is thin, the system says less.

adversarialWith relapses this rare, isn’t any precision estimate hopelessly noisy?

Noise is exactly the risk, which is why the design leans on a maturity gate before evaluating, a hazard framing that extracts more per label, and per-slice calibration monitored for drift rather than a single point estimate trusted as truth. It is also why the whole query is gated behind six months of accrued labels — evaluating precision before enough matured outcomes exist would produce a confident number built on a handful of cases. The honest position is that early estimates are wide, the targets are pre-registered, and no measured precision is published here at all.

adversarialPer-tenant calibration means a small tenant gets an uncalibrated model — what happens then?

Below the minimum effective event count, that tenant does not get its own calibration — it falls back to the global calibration and is flagged as such, rather than being handed a per-tenant curve fit on too few events that would be worse than the global one. That is the correct failure mode: a slightly-mismatched but stable global threshold beats an overfit local one. The trade is transparent — the tenant is told its scores are globally calibrated — and the slice is promoted to its own calibration only once it has the events to support it.

07 · Caching, determinism and versioning

A cache key that drops a field is a stale or cross-tenant score. TTL is not freshness.

screeningWhat is cached, and for how long?

The per-patient risk score, its top patterns, and its provenance are cached with a six-hour TTL, keyed canonically. Most reads are hits, because the nightly batch prewarms the cache for the whole active panel by that same key. The TTL bounds staleness within a day, but freshness is really enforced by what is in the key, not by the clock.

deepWhat goes into the cache key, and why every field?

The key is q4:risk over the three tenant fields, the patient, the window length, the tokenizer version, the model version, the snapshot manifest hash, and the last event sequence. Each field is a result-affecting input: drop the tokenizer or model version and a promoted model serves an old score; drop the snapshot hash or last event sequence and a fresher snapshot serves a stale one; drop a tenant field and you risk a cross-tenant hit. A key that omits any of these is a correctness bug wearing a performance optimisation’s clothes.

deepWhat happens to the cache when a new model is promoted?

Model promotion invalidates the q4:risk:* namespace and the endpoint is swapped, so no coach can be served a stale score from the previous model. Because the model version is part of the key, the new model’s scores land under fresh keys and the nightly batch reprewarms them. Umbrella review flagged that an earlier design let a model swap leave old keys live; the current design makes invalidation on promotion explicit rather than relying on TTL to eventually age them out.

deepHow is a stampede on a cache miss prevented?

With request coalescing — a single-flight lock on the canonical key. If several coaches open the same freshly-invalidated patient at once, only one request builds the sequence and calls the endpoint; the rest wait on that in-flight result and share it. Without coalescing, a popular patient after a model swap would fan out into many identical, expensive builds. It is a small mechanism that matters most exactly when the cache is coldest.

deepHow is scoring made reproducible when a transformer is in the loop?

Determinism here comes from the structured pipeline, not from any single component being magically stable. The sequence is built by a deterministic sort before tokenisation, the tokenizer and model versions are pinned, and the snapshot hash freezes the input state, so the same inputs under the same pinned versions produce the same score. Reproducibility is a property of the chain — frozen snapshot, deterministic order, pinned artifacts — not an assumption that the model would re-derive the same answer from shifting inputs.

adversarialA six-hour TTL on a clinical risk score — what if the patient deteriorates at hour two?

Two mechanisms cover the interval. The freshness marker in the key is the patient’s last event sequence, so a new event that changes the window changes the key and the old cached score no longer matches — a coach’s on-demand check would miss the stale entry and rebuild. And a correction or material spine event triggers targeted invalidation rather than waiting out the TTL. The honest caveat remains that this is a daily-cadence run-up detector, not a real-time acute monitor; catching a two-hour physiological crash is a different system with a different SLA, named in the honest gaps.

adversarialIf the tokenizer version changes, every cached score is silently wrong — how is that caught?

It is caught structurally, not by hoping someone remembers. The tokenizer version is part of the cache key, so a changed tokenizer produces a different key and cannot collide with scores computed under the old vocabulary — the old entries are simply unreachable rather than silently reused. A tokenizer change is also a retrain event, which produces a new model version and invalidates the namespace anyway. The failure this defends against — a new vocabulary quietly reinterpreting old cached tokens — is designed out by making the vocabulary version a key input.

screeningWhat is the snapshot manifest hash, and why is it in the key?

It is a content hash of the frozen point-in-time snapshot the score was computed against — the fingerprint of exactly which vertices, edges, and observations existed at that as-of moment. Putting it in the key means a score is bound to the state it was derived from, so a re-exported or corrected snapshot produces a different hash and a different key rather than colliding with a stale score. It is the single field that ties a cached number back to a reproducible input.

08 · Historical hydration — what nine years buys

Deep history is not a nicety here — it is the precondition that makes the query answerable at all.

screeningWhy does nine years of history matter for a thirty-day-window model?

Because the window is thirty days but the labels are spread across years. A thirty-day model still needs thousands of matured relapse episodes to learn from, and those only exist if the platform has run long enough to accumulate them. Nine years of hydrated history is what turns a sparse, slow-accruing label into a training set large enough to model at all.

screeningDoes hydration give you labels or features?

Both, and the labels are the scarcer prize. Hydration replays historical events into the spine including the outcomes, so past relapses become supervisable episodes and the pre-relapse windows around them become training features. Without the outcomes hydrated too, you would have plenty of feature sequences and almost nothing to predict.

deepHow does deep history change which backtests are possible?

It lets the time-cut backtest have real depth on both sides of the cut. With years of history you can train on an early period and validate on a strictly later one, repeatedly, so the event-time-leakage-controlled evaluation the design insists on — with its disclosed vocabulary look-ahead — is not starved of data. A shallow history forces a choice between a time-honest split with too few labels or a leaky random split — hydration removes that dilemma and makes the honest evaluation the affordable one.

deepWhat does hydration do for label scarcity specifically?

It converts scarcity from “wait years of wall-clock time” into “already accrued.” A relapse label needs its follow-up window to fully run before it matures; on a greenfield platform that means waiting. On a platform with nine years replayed in, most of those windows have already run their course, so the maturity gate passes a large backlog on day one of the build rather than dripping out over the next several quarters.

deepHow are nine years of legacy payload schema versions handled?

Nine years spans many payload-schema versions, and the design requires every queried payload path to carry a documented up-conversion or schema-version filter rather than assuming a single shape. For this query that matters at tokenisation: an event class recorded under an old schema must map to the same token as its modern equivalent, or the same clinical fact would split into two tokens and dilute the signal. Schema versioning is what lets nine years of heterogeneous events collapse onto one coherent vocabulary.

deepWhat did hydration NOT solve for this query?

It did not make the labels trustworthy on its own — a hydrated relapse still has to pass the maturity gate, still has to be attributed correctly, and can still be corrected later. It did not remove the self-fulfilling feedback problem, which is about the future, not the past. And it did not shortcut the platform dependencies — the ungated observation projection, the deterministic exporter, the event-time split — that the query is gated on regardless of how much history exists. Hydration filled the training set; it did not close the design.

adversarialNine-year-old sequences reflect old care protocols — doesn’t training on them hurt?

It is a real concern and the honest answer is that it is managed, not eliminated. Care protocols, devices, and documentation drift over nine years, and a run-up recorded under an old protocol may not map cleanly onto today’s. The defences are the versioned tokenizer with drift monitoring, the time-cut evaluation that weights recent performance, and per-tenant recalibration — but a client who cares about distribution shift should expect older episodes to be down-weighted or windowed out over time, and that windowing policy is a legitimate tuning question rather than a settled one.

screeningCould this query have launched on day one of the platform?

No, and that is stated plainly in the build order. Without accrued, matured relapse labels there is nothing to train, so on a greenfield platform this query is a promise, not a product. It is built last precisely because it depends on time having passed — hydration is what let it be built without waiting for that time to pass in the future.

09 · Governance, PHI and the LLM boundary

The clearest example in the portfolio of knowing when not to use a language model.

screeningDoes a language model touch the risk score?

No. Nothing in the scoring or alerting path routes through a language model. The score comes from a sequence model over discrete event tokens, and the pattern attribution is computed by that same non-LLM pipeline rather than generated as prose. This query is the portfolio’s cleanest case of keeping the language model well away from the numbers.

screeningWhere does PHI live during scoring?

Entirely inside the client’s VPC. The spine, the graph, the snapshot, the model, the pattern store, the cache, and the API all run in-network, and no patient data crosses that boundary at any stage of scoring or alerting. Because there is no external model call on this path, there is no payload to de-identify or route off-network in the first place.

deepIf the model is a transformer, why isn’t this an “LLM system”?

Because “transformer” is an architecture, not a mandate to process natural language. The transformer here reads a short string of clinical event tokens and emits a probability — it has no vocabulary of English, no prompt, and no generative output. Calling it an LLM would confuse the shape of the network with the job it does. The distinction matters operationally: none of the governance, determinism, or residency concerns that attach to a text-generating model apply to a token classifier that never emits language.

deepHow is access to the patient risk panel governed?

Every read writes an audit entry — viewer, target, purpose, and time — before the score is served, and the response carries the access-log id. Access is scoped by role within the tenant, and PHI is protected by tenant-scoped encryption boundaries with access logs on the pattern store, cache, and scoring endpoint, plus a redaction policy on free-text pattern explanations. These are mitigations that make misuse hard and visible, written down as enforced controls rather than promised as guarantees.

deepWhat is the stepped-wedge / IRB rollout, and why is it a governance concern and not just an eval one?

Acting on a relapse alert is an intervention on a patient, so who gets the intervention and when is an ethics question, not only a statistics one. The design uses a stepped-wedge or delayed-start cohort assignment under an IRB-approved evaluation plan, recording model_exposed and intervention_received on the spine. That gives a defensible way to estimate whether the alert actually helps — because some patients are exposed later — without an unethical pure holdout where high-risk patients are knowingly left unwarned. Governance and evaluation are the same mechanism here.

adversarialYou claim determinism — but doesn’t temperature zero on a language model already give you that?

No, and it is worth being precise because this is a common overclaim. Temperature zero does not make a language model’s output deterministic; a pinned model, prompt, and temperature-zero setting with output hashes give you drift detection and reproducible auditing — the hashes detect drift, they do not prevent it. This query sidesteps the whole issue: its determinism comes from the structured chain — deterministic sort, pinned tokenizer and model versions, frozen snapshot — and not from a narrator at all, because there is no narrator on this path.

adversarialA dashboard of patient risk scores is a surveillance surface — what stops misuse?

Nothing makes misuse impossible, and the design does not claim it can — it raises the cost and the visibility. Every view is audited before serving, access is role-scoped within the tenant, the panel is a patient-care tool for the assigned coach rather than a ranked league table, and explanations are redaction-policed. Note this surface shows patient risk for care, not clinician performance, so the actor-scorecard governance that other queries need is not the primary risk here. The controls that do apply are the audit trail and RBAC that make any inappropriate access reconstructable after the fact.

deepWhat actually crosses the VPC boundary, if anything?

On the scoring and alerting path, nothing patient-bearing crosses it — the only thing that leaves is the coach’s rendered result over TLS to their browser inside the same trust boundary. The query inherits the platform’s model seam and observability tooling, all self-hosted in-VPC, but it never invokes the language-model side of that seam. The production path is single and explicit: in-network. There is no external-provider branch on this query to reason about.

10 · Failure modes, degradation and operations

What breaks, how it degrades, and how the system stays honest when it does.

screeningWhat happens when the snapshot goes stale?

Freshness is tiered and enforced: under 24 hours is green, 24–48 hours is yellow, and past 48 hours is red. A projector lag over 30 minutes also flags yellow. The score is served with its snapshot age visible, so a coach knows when they are looking at an aging picture, and at the red tier the system stops presenting scores as current rather than quietly serving them as if nothing were wrong.

screeningWhat is alert fatigue and how is it bounded?

Alert fatigue is what happens when a model fires so often that coaches stop reading its alerts, at which point precision on paper is irrelevant. It is bounded by a per-coach daily alert budget that constrains the threshold, plus suppression on cooldown or an open alert so the same patient does not re-page repeatedly. If the budget is breached, the threshold is raised or the situation is escalated to leadership rather than the firehose being left on.

deepWhat is the self-fulfilling-prophecy failure, and how is it addressed?

If coaches act on flagged patients and prevent their relapses, the model’s own success erases the signal it was trained on — measured precision decays and the model looks like it is failing when it is actually working. The design confronts this with the stepped-wedge / delayed-start rollout that records exposure and intervention on the spine, so retraining can account for the treatment effect the model creates rather than reading its own effectiveness as drift. It is the difference between observing precision fall and understanding why.

deepHow does a relapse correction propagate through the derived artifacts?

Every derived artifact — sequences, scores, alerts — records a source event-sequence range, a snapshot hash, and a dependency set. When a correction event lands, especially a relapse correction, a listener uses those dependency sets to invalidate exactly the affected rows and cache entries and triggers a targeted rebuild, with a P95 SLA of five minutes from correction to fresh artifact. Relapse corrections are the most consequential kind, because a corrected label can flip a training example, so this loop closes fast rather than waiting for the next nightly batch.

deepWhat happens when a coach’s alert budget is exhausted?

The nightly job applies the per-coach budget after thresholding, so once a coach’s budget for the day is spent, further candidate alerts for that coach are held rather than created. The intent is that the budget forces prioritisation — the highest-risk patients consume the limited attention — and a persistent breach is a signal to retune the threshold or escalate, not to silently drop the tail. The trade-off is explicit: a hard budget can defer a genuine alert, which is why breach is a monitored condition rather than an invisible one.

deepWhat is the degradation behaviour under projector lag?

Projector lag — the delay between an event landing on the spine and appearing in the projection the snapshot reads — degrades the picture the same way snapshot age does. A lag over 30 minutes flags yellow, signalling that recent events may not yet be reflected. Because this query reads a snapshot rather than live graph, lag manifests as “the score is built on a slightly older world,” which is surfaced rather than hidden, and the freshness tier governs whether the score is presented as current.

adversarialThe intervention works, precision decays, the model looks broken — how do you tell that from real drift?

This is the sharpest failure in the whole design and the answer is that you cannot tell them apart from precision alone — which is exactly why precision alone is not the monitor. The spine records who was exposed and who received intervention, so retraining and evaluation can separate “precision fell because the model prevented relapses” from “precision fell because the world changed.” Without the exposure record you are blind to the difference; with it you have a defensible, if imperfect, way to attribute the decay. It remains one of the genuinely hard parts, named in the honest gaps.

adversarialIf the nightly batch fails one night, coaches see no new alerts and may never know — how is silence detected?

Silence is the dangerous failure because a missing alert has no error message, so the snapshot-age freshness tier is the backstop: a batch that did not run ages the snapshot into yellow and then red, and the panel reflects that rather than showing a stale-but-confident green. The correct operational response to a failed batch is that the freshness signal degrades visibly and the previous night’s scores are not passed off as today’s. The honest caveat is that detecting the absence of a job is only as strong as the freshness monitoring around it — which is why that monitoring is a first-class SLA and not an afterthought.

adversarialAn alert with no owner sits unactioned — who is accountable and how is that enforced in the data?

Every alert has an owner field, an acknowledgement timestamp, a dismiss reason, and an escalation due time, and each transition lands on the spine as an event. An unassigned or unacknowledged alert is therefore a queryable state, not a lost message: the escalation SLA fires if a high-risk alert is left unacknowledged, and the lifecycle is auditable end to end. The system cannot force a human to act, but it makes inaction a recorded, escalatable fact rather than something that quietly falls through.

11 · Evaluation and acceptance criteria

Every figure is a pre-registered target; the promotion gate decides what actually goes live.

screeningWhat are the pre-registered acceptance targets?

Precision at K days early of at least 0.40 calibrated; lift over base rate of at least three times; coverage of at least 80% on matured relapses; a per-coach daily alert budget; calibration tracked by Brier score and reliability slices; and tokenizer-drift monitoring on vocabulary and frequency. These are the bars set before build. No measured result against them is published here.

screeningWhat backtest mode is allowed?

Time-cut only, with a leakage assertion. The trainer fails the build if any validation event-time is at or before the maximum training event-time, or if a patient or enrollment crosses folds. Random k-fold is not permitted, because in sequence data it leaks a patient’s future into their past.

deepWhat is in the golden regression suite, and why those fixtures?

The golden suite pins fixtures with late arrivals, corrections, and SUPERSEDES chains from the shared exporter golden-replay test. Those are the three ways a snapshot can be built two different ways from the same events, so they are exactly the cases that would silently change a sequence or a score. Pinning them means a change that alters ordering or label status shows up as a failing regression rather than as a quietly different production result.

deepHow is tokenizer drift monitored as an acceptance gate?

The tokenizer version is persisted on every sequence, score, and cache entry, and its vocabulary and token-frequency distribution are monitored. When drift crosses a threshold — a device change shifting the distribution, a new event class appearing — it triggers a retrain or recalibration rather than letting the model keep scoring a vocabulary it no longer matches. The gate exists because the most insidious degradation is not a wrong weight but an input distribution that quietly moved out from under the model.

deepWhat is the promotion gate, and when does a model actually go live?

A weekly challenger is trained on matured label windows and must clear the three-way gate — beat the logistic baseline, then PrefixSpan, on calibrated precision/recall at the operating point, and for a transformer also preserve explanation fidelity — and then beat the live champion at the operating point. Only then is the endpoint swapped and the risk cache flushed; otherwise the champion holds. A model is never promoted just because it is newer or scored higher on an unguarded metric.

deepWhy report Brier and reliability rather than just precision?

Because precision at a threshold tells you nothing about whether a score of 0.7 means a 70% chance — and if it does not, the per-tenant thresholds and alert budgets built on top of it are misgrounded. Brier score and reliability curves measure calibration directly, per tenant and programme, so drift in calibration triggers recalibration before it corrupts thresholding. A well-ranked but poorly-calibrated model can pass a precision check and still mislead every downstream decision that treats the score as a probability.

adversarialEvery number here is a target, not a result — why should a client trust the design?

Because the trustworthy artifact of a design engagement is the discipline, not a leaderboard number, and the numbers that would matter belong to the client, not to a public portfolio. What is offered is a design that pre-registers its bars, refuses to evaluate before labels mature, fails its own build on leakage, and gates promotion on beating a live champion — the machinery that produces a trustworthy number rather than a claimed one. A page that published impressive measured results would be violating the very confidentiality a serious client expects.

adversarialPrecision of 0.40 sounds low — is that good enough to page a human?

In a 5–15% base-rate setting, 0.40 calibrated precision means a flagged patient is several times more likely to relapse than a random one — that is the three-times-lift floor stated a different way, and it is a strong result for a rare event, not a weak one. Whether it justifies a page depends on the cost of the intervention versus a missed relapse, which is why the operating point is tuned per tenant against a per-coach load model rather than fixed globally. The bar is set where a coach’s attention is better spent on the flagged set than on the panel at large.

12 · Tradeoffs, alternatives, and what I would change

Where the design commits, where it could go another way, and its weakest seam.

screeningWhy sequence mining rather than a tabular feature model?

Because the signal is in the order and spacing of events, and a tabular model discards exactly that by collapsing a trajectory into counts and aggregates. Sequence mining keeps the shape of the run-up, which is both more predictive and more explainable — the output is a readable ordered pattern rather than a coefficient. A tabular baseline still exists as the logistic floor the sequence models must beat.

deepWhy not Cox or DeepSurv for a time-to-event problem?

They are close cousins of the chosen framing and a legitimate alternative — the design deliberately adopts a discrete-time hazard formulation, which is the same survival-analysis instinct expressed over discrete intervals and event tokens rather than continuous-time proportional hazards. Discrete-time hazard composes naturally with a token sequence model and per-interval censoring, which continuous Cox does not. A full DeepSurv-style model is a reasonable thing to trial as a rung, but it would have to clear the same promotion gate on calibrated performance and explanation fidelity before displacing the simpler models.

deepWhy build this last in the platform, and was that the right call?

It was built last because it is gated on things the earlier queries produce — matured labels, the ungated observation projection, the deterministic exporter, the event-time split — and on six months of accrued relapse outcomes. Building it earlier would have meant shipping a relapse detector with too few labels to trust, which is worse than not shipping one. The right call was to let the platform earn the labels and harden the shared backbone first, then build the highest-value query on a foundation that could actually support it.

deepWhat would you change about the negative-sampling design?

The ninety-day buffer and the small fixed number of negatives per patient are reasonable defaults, not proven optima, and I would treat both as tunable and validate them empirically once labels accrue. I would also want to test whether stratifying negatives by patient tenure and programme phase — rather than sampling uniformly from time-at-risk — produces a more representative negative set. The current design is deliberately conservative on label purity; the open question is how much informative signal that conservatism leaves on the table.

adversarialWould a simple rules engine have beaten this, and how would you know?

It might, for some cohorts, and the design is structured so you would actually find out rather than assume. The logistic baseline is a stand-in for “a simple model,” and a hand-written rule (“two missed doses plus a glucose excursion”) can be encoded and evaluated on the same time-cut backtest as any other candidate. If the rule beats the learned models on calibrated precision/recall at the operating point, the rule should win — that is the whole point of the promotion gate. The honest position is that a learned model has to earn its complexity against exactly that kind of baseline, every week.

adversarialWhat is the weakest part of this design as it stands?

The self-fulfilling feedback loop and the fragility of any precision estimate on sparse labels are the two weakest seams. The stepped-wedge rollout gives a principled way to reason about the first, but it is operationally heavy and depends on IRB approval and disciplined exposure recording. And with a rare positive class, early evaluation is inherently wide, so confident claims about performance are premature by design. Both are named in the honest gaps rather than smoothed over — a system that pretends these are solved is more dangerous than one that admits them.

adversarialIf you had to ship in three months instead of waiting for labels, what would you cut?

I would not cut the maturity gate or the leakage assertions — those are what keep the thing honest — so what I would ship is the pattern-mining and baseline rungs on whatever matured labels exist, with the transformer left off the endpoint until it can clear the gate. I would ship the alert lifecycle and freshness tiers because they are what make it safe to operate, and I would defer per-tenant calibration to the global model until slices have the events. The one thing I would refuse to do is claim a precision number on immature labels to make a deadline — that is precisely the shortcut this design exists to prevent.

screeningWhat is the single most transferable idea here?

Earn the labels before you model. Any early-warning system where an order-dependent signal precedes a rare, expensive event — churn, fraud escalation, equipment failure, hospital readmission — lives or dies on having enough matured outcomes, windowing that does not leak the future, and a detector that beats “alert everyone,” with the language model kept well away from the numbers. The architecture is portable; the discipline is the product.

Honest gaps · what this query cannot claim today

What is settled, what is gated, and the real review verdict

The design-review verdict is ship-with-changes, not approved. An adversarial design review raised twenty-three findings across correctness, modelling, operational, and scalability. Sixteen were local to this query and are absorbed into the hardened v2 design; six were deliberately promoted into shared platform components — the tenant wrapper, the outcome-maturity and censoring library, the correction-invalidation contract, the ungated observation projection, the deterministic exporter, and the four-field event-time split — so sibling queries inherit the same fixes. Only the precedent-search query in this portfolio has cleared a full hiring-CTO work-sample review to an approved verdict; the honest claim here is narrower — a design hardened against a hard review and held out of go-live on purpose until its preconditions closed.

What was platform-gated — now landed. This query could not be trusted until those six shared components landed and until at least six months of relapse labels had accumulated and matured. Both conditions closed and the service was built on the platform’s self-hosted, in-VPC stack, with the maturity gate left standing as a permanent guard over every newly-accruing window. The dependency is structural: a shortcut on any one of the six would have re-opened a leakage, isolation, or freshness hole the review had closed.

What it genuinely cannot answer. It is a daily-cadence run-up detector, not a seconds-fresh acute monitor — a two-hour physiological crash is a different system with a different SLA. Its precision estimates on a sparse positive class are inherently wide early on, so confident performance claims would be premature; and the self-fulfilling feedback loop — the model erasing its own signal by working — is mitigated by the stepped-wedge rollout but never fully resolved. Everything the system surfaces is observational: a detected pattern is associated with relapse, not proven to cause it, and unmeasured confounding is never ruled out. And no measured production metric appears anywhere on this page — every figure is a pre-registered acceptance target, by design and by confidentiality.
