The same discipline applies anywhere retrieval must not be confidently wrong: legal, underwriting, incident response, multi-tenant SaaS.

01 The problem

Why this is not a SQL query

The structured half of the question — HbA1c band, BMI, country, protocol — is a cohort filter any database can run. The hard half is "cases like this one": similarity of symptom phrasing, decision rationale, and the evidence pattern in front of the clinician at the time. SQL returns a cohort; the clinician needs the most analogous decisions inside that cohort. That takes hybrid retrieval: structured filters plus dense-vector similarity.

Retrieval in a clinical setting also has three silent failure modes, and any one of them quietly destroys trust in the product:

  • Future leakage — describing a past case using information that arrived after the decision was made, which makes precedents look wiser than they were.
  • Tenant leakage — one clinic's patients surfacing in another clinic's results, a reportable privacy incident rather than a bug.
  • Immature outcomes — showing a 6-month outcome for a decision made 6 weeks ago, or attributing an outcome to the wrong one of two overlapping decisions.

The design treats these as first-class requirements with named controls — not as edge cases to patch later.

02 The architecture

An append-only event spine, a decision graph, and one index per question

The platform's source of truth is an append-only event spine (DynamoDB). A projection keeps a decision graph (Neptune) current, and a nightly snapshot feeds the indexing pipeline. The pivotal design decision: the unit of retrieval is the Decision, not the Patient — one Elasticsearch document per qualifying decision, keyed by tenant · patient · enrollment · decision · timestamp. The query is about an action taken at a point in time; patient-level retrieval answers the wrong question.

The as-of rule

The nightly indexer reconstructs each decision's context as of the moment it was made, replaying the append-only spine up to that decision's event sequence — never reading current graph properties. The embedded document contains the clinical state, rationale, and evidence available at the time, and nothing that arrived later. Outcomes are computed in a separate pass, only once follow-up has matured, and are stored alongside the card — never inside the embedding.

03 Hydration

Nine years of prior decisions, replayed into the spine

This system was not built against an empty world. A private production system had already answered this question for roughly nine years, and that history is the raw material a precedent panel runs on. So the platform ships a first-class hydration pipeline: the full multi-year corpus is replayed through the append-only spine to pre-seed the decision graph, the nightly snapshots, and the index — a quarantine-first, proof-carrying load, not a throwaway backfill.

Five properties make that load trustworthy rather than merely large:

  • Event time is not ingestion time. Each legacy record keeps the real-world moment the decision was made as its event time; separate fields record when the system learned of it. Collapse the two and nine years of decisions would look as though they all happened on load day — and every as-of reconstruction the index depends on would be void. The watermark runs in a distinct hydration mode, where a nine-year spread of "late" data is expected, and flips to a small live value at cutover.
  • Hydrating twice is a no-op. Content-addressed event IDs and conditional writes make the load idempotent and resumable: re-running the pipeline over the same corpus yields an identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. That is an executable proof of determinism, not an assurance.
  • Nothing is silently coerced. Nine years spans many legacy payload-schema versions; each gets an upconverter, and any row that cannot be mapped to a schema — or to a tenant — is quarantined with a reason, never defaulted. A defaulted tenant would be a cross-tenant leak, the exact failure this system exists to prevent.
  • The same projection code serves history and live. History is replayed through the identical projection logic that serves the live stream. Otherwise the hydrated graph would be a different product from the streaming one, and every determinism guarantee would be theatre.
  • The load is proof-carrying. Zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before anything downstream is built. A fenced cutover then attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate at the boundary.
What nine years buys precedent search

A precedent panel is only as convincing as the depth of history behind it. Nine years of prior decisions — each embedded as of the moment it was made, with its matured outcomes stamped alongside — is what fills the index. With that much precedent in the corpus, the cold-start gate that refuses to answer on thin data fires far less often, because the comparable cases usually exist. A retrieval service launched against an empty index would suppress most queries on day one; hydration is why this one has something to retrieve.

04 The query path

Gate first, cache aggressively, assert tenancy on every result

A request is refused before it is answered badly. A cold-start quality gate checks that the draft case carries all seven required at-decision clinical fields, at least three pieces of evidence, a minimum local graph neighbourhood, and a large-enough candidate pool. If any check fails, the clinician gets an honest "insufficient comparable precedents" state with a plain cohort count — never a confident-looking list built on thin data.

Every cached answer is keyed by a canonical hash of everything that could change the result — the tenant scope, the query case, the draft decision, every filter, the dedup policy, the feature-schema version, the embedding-model version, the index manifest, and the patient's latest event sequence. A stale cache entry is therefore unreachable rather than merely unlikely, and a correction event upstream invalidates dependent keys within a five-minute SLA.

06 The LLM boundary

Where the language model is — and where it is not allowed

The through-line of this system: know when not to use an LLM. The language model appears in exactly two thin, gated roles — parsing a request in, summarising a card out. It never ranks, never filters, never computes. Code makes every decision and every number.

  • Structured output, not free text: the parser persona returns a typed, schema-validated object (Pydantic), so a malformed parse is a retry, not a corrupted query.
  • Self-hosted inference for PHI: the deployment target is Llama 3.1 (8B workhorse, 70B escalation) running inside the VPC — patient data never leaves the network. External embedding providers are permitted only with de-identified payloads under a signed DPA.
  • Deterministic ranking, optional narration: similarity search and ordering are vector math and filters, and each precedent card is fully readable from its structured fields. The one-sentence summary is an optional convenience — it can be rendered from a deterministic template, and if the LLM is unavailable the cards serve without it. The model is an enhancement on a short leash, never a dependency.
07 The RAG pipeline

Retrieval-augmented, on a short leash

This is the one place the system earns the name RAG, and it is worth being precise about what that means here. The retrieval is a governed query against the context graph, not a similarity search over a pile of documents: structured filters for the cohort, plus a dense-vector kNN over per-decision embeddings for the most analogous cases inside it — with the tenant filter inside the query and every candidate reconstructed as of the decision it records. The model never runs the search and never touches the ranking; that is vector math and code. Its entire job is to say, in one sentence, what the retrieved evidence already shows — and a validator rejects the sentence if it says anything more.

Five stages, and the language model appears in exactly one of them:

  • Retrieve — the hybrid precedent query: structured filters for the cohort, a dense-vector kNN for the most analogous decisions inside it. The tenant filter lives inside the kNN query, never as a filter applied after the fact.
  • Assemble — a typed, as-of context object: only the clinical fields, rationale, and evidence that existed at decision time, each carrying its provenance. Post-decision evidence and matured outcomes never enter the context the model sees.
  • Generate — a pinned model version and a pinned system prompt at temperature 0, self-hosted in-VPC so PHI never leaves the network. The prompt forbids computing, re-ranking, or concluding; it may only restate.
  • Validate — the leakage guard scans the sentence for any post-decision value, and a claim grammar decomposes it into templates backed by cited as-of fields, rejecting anything it cannot decompose. A sentence that cites a value not in the card is not served.
  • Serve or suppress — a failed validation sets a suppression flag with failure metadata. The ranked precedent cards, their evidence, and their outcomes are served regardless: the answer never depends on the model.

Three invariants hold across every response:

  • No number in any response is written by a language model. Code computes and ranks; the model only narrates.
  • Every generated sentence is backed by cited as-of-decision fields, or it is not served.
  • Hashes pin the inputs and the output — content hash, model version, validation version — so any card summary can be re-derived and audited, and the precedent panel stays authoritative whether or not the sentence survives.

08 The stack

Boring infrastructure, deliberate framework choices

  2. Design review · round 4 Approved with minor follow-ups (lock-TTL hardening, a correction-invalidation SLA) — each folded into the design as a named task.

12 The decision loop

The panel is evidence, not advice

At the moment a decision is made, two things sit side by side in front of the person on the call. One is an AI recommender — a separate service, not this one — putting forward an option, carrying a raw confidence and an emitted confidence bucket. The other is this precedent panel. The panel puts forward nothing: it emits no option and no confidence. Its entire job is to show what happened to comparable patients who faced this decision before. It is the evidence a human weighs when deciding whether to accept the recommender's option — the thing that makes an override informed rather than a gut call.

This page has called that person a clinician throughout. In practice three roles open the panel: a guidance counsellor, sitting with a prospective patient before enrolment; a doctor, with an existing patient; a health coach, on a minor ailment. All three see the same two surfaces, and all three reach the panel through the same auth-resolved tenant scope — account · workspace · control-unit — never a scope the caller can assert for itself.

The human then decides, and that decision lands on the append-only spine as first-class events: the option the recommender emitted, whether a human accepted it, and — if they rejected it — which option they chose instead. This service does not write those events. It emits no option, no confidence, and no accept-or-reject record; it is read by the surface that captures them. But those events are the reason the platform's AI-versus-human outcome comparison is answerable at all — that query was blocked at design review precisely because no trustworthy record of what the recommender put forward, whether a human accepted it, and what they chose instead yet existed. The contract was written and signed and the query is live; its final approval is still pending. A second query asks the complementary question of the human: when a clinician recorded high confidence in their own decision, did the outcome bear that out. And the recommender learns from these decisions only once the outcome is verifiable — a matured label, by the platform's shared definition of maturity — never from the mere fact that someone accepted.

The strongest thing to say about this panel, though, is the thing it refuses to do. When the cold-start gate fails — a required at-decision field missing, evidence too thin, the local graph neighbourhood too small, the candidate pool too shallow — the panel does not manufacture a thin ranked list to fill the silence. It returns the honest empty state and a plain cohort count, labelled as a count and never as vector similarity. The human decides without precedent evidence, and is told that is what is happening.

For the guidance counsellor, that refusal is the common case, not the edge case. A prospective patient has no decision history and no graph neighbourhood; there is nothing for the gate to pass. The role most likely to open this panel on a case with no history behind it is the role the panel most often has to decline. That cuts against the under-5%-per-program empty-result floor above, and the honest thing is to say so rather than smooth it over: a floor that is comfortable across a doctor's caseload is not automatically comfortable for a counsellor sitting with someone who has no history at all. Where the two actually meet is a question for measured behaviour, and no measured production numbers are published here.

A scoping caveat, stated on purpose

It follows that the "human" arm of any AI-versus-human comparison built on these events is an evidence-assisted human — and sometimes an unassisted one, on precisely those decisions where the gate refused. That is a real bound on how such a comparison should be read, and it is not a small one. Naming it makes the downstream query more defensible, not less: a comparison that quietly assumed every human decision was made with precedent evidence in view would be measuring something it had never defined.
