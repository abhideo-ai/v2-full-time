Case study · ML system design · Healthcare

Before a patient relapses — what were the warning signs, and did anyone act?

For a chronic-care platform covering 80+ conditions, I designed the early-warning system that mines the event sequences leading up to a relapse, took it through an adversarial review that told me to earn its labels first, and deployed it in-VPC — with the discipline to not trust a model trained on too few matured outcomes. The same pattern applies anywhere order-dependent signals precede a rare, costly event: churn, fraud escalation, equipment failure, readmission.

01 The problem

The signal is in the order, and the labels barely exist

Relapse in chronic care is rare — a 5–15% base rate — and by the time it is recorded, the interesting part has already happened: the sequence of missed check-ins, drifting biomarkers, and quietly worsening self-reports in the weeks before. A histogram of event counts misses it entirely, because the signal is in the order, not the totals. This is not a GROUP BY and it is not a similarity search; it is a "what came before X?" question where sequence matters, which is a different class of model.

It also has a failure mode that has nothing to do with the algorithm. A sequence model needs outcomes to learn from, and outcomes accumulate over months. Train too early and you fit a handful of relapses — a model that looks plausible and is quietly worthless. Many machine-learning efforts fail here — on label count, not on architecture. So the hardest decision in this whole system was a scheduling one: sequence this query last, and do not train it until the labels are real. That discipline is now a standing guard — the model never learns from, or scores against, an immature outcome window.

Three silent traps then sit inside the modelling itself, each of which quietly manufactures a fictitious signal:

  • Proxy-label circularity — training on "the clinician raised the risk level" instead of on relapse itself teaches the model to predict clinicians, not outcomes.
  • Biased negatives — sampling "healthy" windows without excluding the run-up to a future relapse silently mislabels the very signal you are trying to detect.
  • Leaked time — a random train/test split lets the future teach the past, so a back-test looks brilliant and production does not.

And one trap sits in the deployment: the moment a coach acts on an alert, the intervention can change the outcome — so a naive retrain reads its own success as the signal disappearing. The design treats all of these as first-class requirements with named controls, not edge cases to patch later.

02 The architecture

Snapshot-only sequence mining over an append-only spine

The platform's source of truth is an append-only event spine (DynamoDB). A projection keeps a decision graph (Neptune) current, and a deterministic nightly export freezes it to columnar snapshot files. Unlike the other queries, this one reads two feeds: the graph snapshot for decisions and outcomes, and a separate observation snapshot that is deliberately not gated on whether a decision ever referenced it. That second feed is the whole point — sequence mining lives in the "no one reacted yet" tail, exactly the pre-relapse signals a decision-gated projection would drop.

Why the ungated feed matters

A projection that only surfaces observations once a clinician acts on them is fine for retrieval and analytics. For early-signal detection it is the single biggest exposure of the whole programme: the observations that matter most are precisely the ones no one reacted to yet. So the observation snapshot is materialised without the decision-attachment gate — and every downstream store is version-pinned to the same snapshot_manifest_hash so a score can always be traced back to the exact frozen state it came from.

03 Hydration

Nine years of history, replayed into the spine — the labels included

This system was not built against an empty world. Roughly nine years of prior history — check-ins, labs, decisions, and the relapse outcomes themselves — were hydrated through the same append-only event spine that carries live traffic, so the model has real history to learn from on day one. Hydration is a first-class, quarantine-first, proof-carrying pipeline, not a throwaway backfill, and five rules keep the replayed history faithful to what actually happened:

  • Event time is not ingestion time. Each legacy record carries the real-world moment it happened — when a check-in was missed, a lab was drawn, a relapse surfaced — plus a companion time for when the source observed or resulted it; only the received and written timestamps are stamped from the load, when the system learned of the record. Collapse the real-world event time into load time and nine years of events all appear to land on load day, voiding every as-of reconstruction and scrambling the very ordering a sequence model reads. The watermark runs in a distinct hydration mode where a nine-year spread of arrivals is expected, not the small live watermark.
  • Hydrating twice is a no-op. Content-addressed event IDs and conditional writes make the load idempotent and resumable: re-running the pipeline over the same corpus yields a byte-identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. That equality is an executable proof of determinism, not an assurance.
  • Nothing is silently coerced. Nine years spans many legacy payload-schema versions; each gets an explicit upconverter, and any row that cannot be mapped to a schema — or to a tenant — is quarantined with a reason rather than defaulted. A defaulted tenant would be a cross-tenant leak, and here it would poison a training set assembled from every tenant's history.
  • The same projection code runs for history and for live. Replayed history passes through the exact projection logic that serves the live stream, so the sequences the model trains on have the same shape as the sequences it will score tomorrow. Run history through different code and training and serving would drift apart while every determinism guarantee became theatre.
  • The load is proof-carrying. Zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before a single model is trained on it. A fenced cutover then attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate at the boundary.

The five rules above all rest on one field getting its provenance right, and for a sequence model that field is load-bearing in the most literal sense. The signal this query reads is an order — so if the replay ever lets ingestion time stand in for real-world event time, nine years of events collapse onto load day and the very ordering the model learns from is scrambled beyond repair. Hydration therefore splits time into four fields with explicit provenance: the moment it happened and the moment the source resulted it are read from the record; the moments the load received and wrote it are pinned to a single corpus fence, never to the wall clock. A hard invariant — every historical event's real-world time must fall strictly before its received time — turns the cardinal sin into a caught error rather than a silent one, and as-of order is always event time first, source sequence as the tiebreak.

Without that split the back-test would still be deterministic — and deterministically wrong: every sequence would sort by the day it was loaded, so the model would learn an order that never happened and score tomorrow's patients against it.

History is only useful if it joins the live stream without a seam. The load runs against a frozen copy of the spine taken at a point-in-time fence; the live consumer is switched on in a buffering mode before the last historical chunk seals, a delta pass replays anything that arrived in between, and only then does a boundary proof check that the first live sequence number is exactly one past the fence, that no hydrated event reappears as a fresh write, and that the two ranges are contiguous. The served spine does not flip to the hydrated copy until that proof is green — a single missing sequence number aborts the cutover.

For an order-sensitive model a one-event hole at the boundary is not cosmetic — it is a phantom gap in a patient's timeline that could invent or erase a pre-relapse pattern, so the cutover fails closed rather than papering over it.

For early-signal detection the payoff is the one input this query cannot fabricate: the matured relapse labels themselves. The review's hardest demand was to refuse to model until the outcome labels were real, and nine years of hydrated history is what makes that demand satisfiable — the corpus already holds a deep supply of outcome windows that have fully run their course, so the sequence model trains on real, matured history rather than on the thin handful a freshly started clock would yield. The six-month maturity gate is not retired once those labels are loaded; it stays on as a standing guard over every newly-accruing outcome window, so the model still never learns from, or scores against, a relapse that has not yet had time to resolve.

04 The core discipline

The hard part is the labels, not the model

The centre of this system is not the transformer; it is the time geometry that decides what is a positive, what is a negative, and what is not yet allowed to be either. Get this wrong and the fanciest model learns from a contaminated dataset. Three windowing rules do the work, and they are the same rules the review forced into the design:

  • The positive example is the 30-day window before a relapse — the sequence of events the model is asked to recognise early.
  • Negatives are drawn from a patient's active time-at-risk, but an exclusion buffer around any future relapse is removed first, so a pre-relapse run-up is never accidentally labelled "healthy".
  • A row only counts once its follow-up has matured. label_status is an enforced enum — mature, not_mature, censored_dropout, program_switch, or by_later_relapse — and only mature rows carry supervised labels. A binary 0/1 is reported as an operating-point summary, never as the model's loss.
The data-readiness gate

The tempting misread — and the one the original plan made — was to treat this as a calendar wait: ship the label typing, then idle for months while post-launch relapses accrued. Nine years of hydrated history retires that wait, because the matured labels already exist. What does not go away is the maturity requirement per outcome window: a relapse only becomes a supervised label once its follow-up has fully run, so the six-month maturity gate is enforced on every relapse_detected window, historical and newly-accruing alike, and training draws only on the windows that have cleared it. A model fit on too little matured signal is worse than no model, because it looks like it works — so the gate did not retire once the model shipped; it stays live, and no patient is scored against an outcome window that has not matured.

05 The scoring path

Score in bulk overnight, answer in milliseconds

Two things must never happen: a per-patient scan of the graph database, and a stampede on a cold cache key. So the heavy work runs once a night as a batch job over the frozen snapshot, and the on-demand path is audit-first, tenant-asserted, and coalesced. Scores live behind a canonical cache key that pins the tokenizer, the model version, the snapshot hash, and the patient's latest event sequence — so a stale score is unreachable rather than merely unlikely, and a model swap invalidates the entire risk namespace.

The nightly batch is where scale is won. Instead of walking the graph one patient at a time — the design the review killed — it scans active patients from the tenant-partitioned parquet, pre-materialises every 30-day window, and hands one batch payload to a SageMaker transform job. Only above-threshold, in-budget, un-suppressed candidates become alerts, and the cache is bulk-prewarmed so the morning's on-demand checks are warm hits:

Every way this model could lie has a named control

What could go wrongThe control
Proxy-label circularity Only relapse_detected supervises the model. "The clinician raised the risk level" is used as a feature behind an event-time guard, never as a label — the model predicts outcomes, not the people who react to them.

Immature or censored outcomes An enforced label_status enum — mature, not-mature, censored-dropout, program-switch, or by-later-relapse — with only mature rows carrying supervised labels; a binary 0/1 is an operating-point summary, never the loss.
Cross-tenant leakage A tenant context on all three tenant fields, with fail-closed assertions through the whole stack — the DuckDB predicate, the SageMaker request, Postgres row keys, the Redis namespace, and a response-time assertion on every cell.
A self-fulfilling model Acting on an alert changes the outcome. An IRB-approved stepped-wedge rollout records model_exposed and intervention_received on the spine, so retraining accounts for the treatment effect it creates instead of reading its own success as the signal vanishing.
Alert fatigue A per-coach daily alert budget, suppression on cooldown or an open alert, and a full lifecycle — owner, ack, dismiss reason, escalation SLA — with every action landing back on the spine as a first-class event.
Stale snapshot or ignored corrections Freshness tiers — under 24h green, 24–48h yellow, past 48h blocks; projector lag over 30 minutes flags yellow. A correction listener invalidates sequences, scores, and alerts on a dependency-targeted rebuild within a P95 five-minute SLA.

None of these are absolutes dressed up as guarantees. Buffering, calibration, and the maturity gate reduce known biases; unmeasured confounding is never ruled out, and every surface stays observational — a detected pattern is associated with relapse, not proven to cause it.

07 The LLM boundary

Here, the language model is almost absent

The through-line of this portfolio is knowing when not to use an LLM, and this query is its purest case. Sequence mining, scoring, calibration, thresholding, and suppression are all deterministic code, and the pattern attribution itself — SHAP or attention weights — is computed by that same non-LLM pipeline rather than generated by a language model. The model driving the numbers is a sequence model, not a language model, and nothing in the scoring or alerting path routes through an LLM at all.

The platform's model seam exists — the sibling queries that genuinely narrate use it — but this query never calls it. There is no persona, no prompt, and no generated sentence anywhere in the alerting path. What the coach sees is the calibrated score and the ranked patterns that drove it: three missed check-ins and a rising fasting-glucose trend in the last two weeks is not prose a model wrote, it is the attribution the pipeline already computed, rendered by a template. A model asked to restate it could only add a failure mode — a paraphrase that sounds more certain than the number underneath it.

And there is deliberately no retrieval-augmented generation surface layered on here: an early-warning alert is a calibrated risk score bound to the ranked sequence patterns that triggered it, so pointing a retriever at documents to justify a signal the model has already computed and attributed would be adding a narrator, not adding evidence — and the discipline of this query is knowing that the signal stands on the cells the pipeline already holds.

08 The stack

Boring infrastructure, deliberate framework choices

The data and scoring core is deliberately unexciting — proven stores, each doing one job. This query inherits the platform's LangChain seam and LangGraph runtime and its PHI-safe observability, but — unlike the queries that narrate — it never invokes a language model.

The tables below say what each component is; the diagram first says when each one is touched and where the trust boundary sits. Three lanes tell that story. The request path is the short list a coach actually waits on — the API, the audit-and-tenant gate, the Redis risk cache, and, only on a cache miss, the model endpoint. The nightly lane does the heavy lifting on a schedule — snapshot export, sequence build, batch scoring, the weekly retrain, and the alert lifecycle — and it prewarms the cache so the morning's requests are hits. The one-time lane is the historical hydration that pre-seeds the spine. Everything runs inside the client VPC; the only thing outside it is the clinician's browser, reaching in over TLS.

Drawing the absences is the point: the graph database serves audit and the nightly export only and is never walked per patient at scoring time, and the inherited language-model seam is never called on any path this query owns — both hang off the diagram on dotted edges, outside the request lane.

09 Evaluation

Report lift, cut time, never random-split

For a rare-event detector, accuracy is a trap — a model that predicts "no relapse" for everyone scores 85–95%. So evaluation is built on lift over the base rate and precision when the signal fires, on time-cut back-tests where no future event can teach the past — the trainer fails the build if any validation event-time is at or before the maximum training event-time, or if a patient or enrollment crosses folds — and every alert is paired with its top contributing patterns so a coach can see why.

One channel stays open, and it is named rather than buried: the token vocabulary is derived from the full corpus, so the token set — not the events, the values, or the labels — reflects periods after the cut. The mitigation is a versioned, frozen tokenizer, fixed at train time and monitored for drift rather than re-fit per scoring run. That reduces the exposure; it does not make it zero. A rare token appearing only after the cutoff would still be unknown to a strictly time-honest tokenizer, and the design treats tokenizer construction as a versioned artifact with drift triggers precisely because this is not fully closed.

Promotion is itself a gate, and the gate is a ladder rather than a bake-off. A weekly challenger is built as three rungs — a logistic baseline, then PrefixSpan pattern mining, then a transformer — and each rung has to earn its place: a fancier rung is kept only if it beats the simpler one below it on calibrated precision-recall at the clinical operating point, and the transformer carries an extra bar, because a clinical alert has to be explainable — it must also keep its attribution faithful to the score. The simplest model that cleared its bar becomes the candidate, and even the candidate does not ship unless it also beats the model currently live:

Clearing the ladder decides which model should win; the weekly retrain job then has to enact that win on a running system without ever serving a stale score. Before it trains, it asserts the split cannot leak — every validation event must post-date the last training event, and no patient or enrollment may straddle the fold boundary — so a model is never scored on the very history it was fit on. On a promotion it orders the swap deliberately: it invalidates the entire risk-cache namespace before it repoints the serving endpoint at the new model version, so the first request after a swap cannot read a score the old model wrote; a hold leaves the champion and its warm cache untouched. That ordering is the whole reason a promotion is safe to run mid-day — reverse it, and a coach could read a fresh cache entry scored by a model that no longer exists.

10 The review gauntlet

A review that said: earn the labels first

Before anything was built, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens. It returned twenty-three findings and an unusually strict verdict — ship-with-changes, with an explicit instruction not to implement the alerting path until six preconditions closed. For the highest-value, slowest-to-mature query in the portfolio, the design's most useful decision was to refuse to train until the outcome labels were real — loaded, validated, and matured — rather than fit a hopeful handful; nine years of hydrated history is what let the query clear that bar without idling a calendar.

11 Delivered

What was delivered — and where it transfers

  • A production deployment — the early-signal service live on the platform's self-hosted, in-VPC stack, with the nightly batch scorer, the alert-lifecycle service, and the correction listener wired to the shared backbone. Shipped after executive sign-off on the design.
  • A complete design packet — the sequence-builder spec, the label-maturity and censoring contract, the leakage-safe windowing rules, the three-way promotion gate, API envelopes with full provenance pins, the RBAC/audit and IRB governance plan, and freshness SLAs.
  • A label-readiness discipline — a six-month outcome-maturity gate enforced on every relapse window, so the model trains only on outcomes that have fully resolved instead of a hopeful handful.
  • A review audit trail — a twenty-three-finding adversarial design review, its absorption into a hardened v2, and the six platform gates tracked to closure as its promotion conditions.

The pattern generalises well beyond healthcare: any early-warning system where an order-dependent signal precedes a rare, expensive event — churn, fraud escalation, equipment failure, hospital readmission. If your win depends on having enough labelled outcomes before you model, on windowing that does not leak the future, and on a detector that beats "alert everyone" — with the language model kept well away from the numbers — this is the discipline I bring.
