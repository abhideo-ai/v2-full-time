Interview prep · Killer query 5

Decision latency — how long until someone decides?

A production analytics service on a chronic-care platform covering 80+ chronic conditions, with roughly nine years of patient history behind it. It measures the time from a high-risk lab result arriving to the treatment decision that answers it — split by clinician, by geography, and by whether artificial intelligence (AI) assisted the decision. Sole owner, design through production.

Open with these two sentences

“A high-risk lab result lands on a chronic-care platform at two in the morning. Leadership wants one number — how long until a doctor decides something about it — and the dangerous version of that dashboard is one join away.”

“I built the honest version: the denominator counts the labs nobody ever answered, every statistic is seeded and replayable, and there is not a single language-model call anywhere in the pipeline.”

01 What is it?

A subtraction that hides four ways to be confidently wrong

Start with the human situation, not the schema. A patient on a chronic-care programme has blood drawn. The result comes back flagged high-risk — a kidney marker moving the wrong way, a glucose reading that needs a medication change. Somewhere on the other side of that result is a clinician who has to decide something: change the drug, escalate, order a follow-up, bring the patient in. The operational question the platform's leadership asks is the obvious one: how long does that take, and where is it slowest?

The regulator's version of the same question is sharper. A regulator does not want the average; a regulator wants to know about the labs where nobody decided anything, and wants the number traced back to the exact data that produced it. Both audiences are served by one query, written out verbatim in the specification:

The query the system must answer

“What is the median time between a high-risk lab result received and the corresponding treatment decision? Segmented by doctor, geography, and AI usage.”

On paper that is one join and one subtraction: pair each high-risk lab with the next treatment decision, subtract two timestamps, take the median, group by clinician. The reason this is a Staff-level problem and not a ticket is that every clause of that sentence hides a way to ship a confident, wrong number — and a wrong number here does not look wrong. It looks like a clean dashboard that sends leadership chasing the wrong clinic.

The four failure modes a naive implementation produces

1 · “Corresponding” is a clinical judgment

A nearest-in-time join happily pairs a high-risk lab with the next scheduling message, supplement note, or appointment reminder — an action that has nothing to do with the lab. You then report the time to some decision, not the decision the lab demanded.

2 · Survivorship bias

An inner join silently deletes the labs nobody answered — which are precisely the ones leadership most needs to see. A missing response drops out of the numerator and the denominator at once, so the platform looks fastest exactly where it is failing worst.

3 · The wrong clock

Subtract an ingestion timestamp from a write timestamp and you are measuring the database, not the clinic. Outpatient labs can be resulted days before the platform receives them, so picking the wrong pair of timestamps moves the answer by that entire gap.

4 · It renders as a scorecard

Per-clinician latency is performance data the instant it draws on screen. Pretending otherwise would be dishonest; the design's job is to make misuse harder, slower, and provable after the fact — not to claim it is impossible.

Each of those is treated as a first-class problem with a named control, rather than trusted to a one-line query. That framing — four traps, four controls — is the spine of the whole answer, and it is what to fall back on if the interviewer takes the conversation anywhere.

The vocabulary, defined once — say the expansion the first time

Right-censoring
A statistical way of recording “this had not happened yet when we stopped looking”. A lab with no decision after fourteen days is not dropped and not recorded as “took fourteen days” — it is recorded as at least fourteen days and still open. It is the same treatment a clinical trial gives a patient who has not had the event by the end of the study.
Kaplan–Meier estimate
The standard survival-analysis curve that turns a mix of completed and censored observations into a time-to-event estimate. Here the “event” is a treatment decision, so it yields a time-to-decision curve that correctly accounts for the labs still waiting.
Bootstrap, seeded and clustered
Resampling the data thousands of times to see how much a statistic wobbles, which gives a confidence interval. Seeded means the random draws come from a fixed seed, so the interval is reproducible. Clustered by enrollment means whole patients are resampled rather than individual rows.
CI — two different things, watch this one
Confidence interval in the statistics sections; continuous integration in the testing sections (“the golden regression fails CI”). Say the full words in an interview; the collision is a real source of confusion.
P90 / 95th-percentile (P95)
The 90th and 95th percentiles. P90 latency is the value that 90% of labs beat — it exposes the slow tail a median hides. P95 ≤ 5 minutes is a freshness target on corrections, a different axis entirely.
As-of reconstruction
Computing what a field was at a past moment by replaying the event log up to that point, instead of reading today's value. It is what stops a patient who moved country last year from rewriting the geography of a two-year-old measurement.
Event spine
The append-only log of everything that happened, in order — the source of truth. Nothing in it is edited; corrections arrive as new events.
Snapshot manifest
The record of exactly what frozen state a nightly build read: the as-of watermark, a content hash of the graph's vertices and edges, and the highest source event sequence included.
Evidence edge
A link in the decision graph where a clinician's decision explicitly cites a lab result as the evidence for it. This is the single most load-bearing object in the design.
Tenant, three-field scope
Who the data belongs to: an account, a workspace, and a control unit. All three travel together, because one account can span workspaces that must not see each other's data.
Others, expanded
virtual private cloud (VPC) — virtual private cloud (the client's own isolated network). PHI — protected health information. RBAC — role-based access control. LLM — large language model. application programming interface (API) — application programming interface. user interface (UI) — user interface. TLS — transport layer security. service-level agreement (SLA) — service-level agreement. structured query language (SQL) — structured query language.
02 How did we solve it?

Snapshot-only analytics, two upstream feeds, and gates that fire in a fixed order

The whole system is a nightly build plus a deliberately boring read path. Nothing a clinician waits on ever touches the live decision graph, and nothing statistical is recomputed at request time.

The two feeds — this is the line to lead with

The platform's normal projection materialises an observation only once it attaches to a decision. That is fine for most queries and a quiet catastrophe for this one: labs nobody answered would never enter the data at all. So the system reads a second, ungated lab feed that returns every high-risk lab whether or not any decision ever cited it. That feed is the denominator. Everything else in the design is downstream of getting the denominator right.

The nightly build, step by step

  1. Freeze the graph. A nightly export writes out the decision graph's vertices and edges at a single as-of watermark, plus a manifest carrying the watermark, a content hash, and the maximum source event sequence included. Every artefact downstream carries that snapshot hash, so any number can be traced to the exact frozen state behind it.
  2. Pair, tenant-scoped, in columnar joins. The pair builder runs analytical joins over the frozen snapshot in an embedded columnar engine — no per-lab graph traversal — and emits one row per high-risk lab. The join predicates require account, workspace and control unit to match on both sides, so a cross-tenant pair is not even a candidate.
  3. Assign exactly one of three pair methods. supported_by_edge when the decision explicitly cites the lab as evidence; heuristic_next_relevant when no such edge exists but a clinically relevant decision follows within fourteen days; unpaired when no decision follows at all. The method decides what the row is allowed to do — and only the first may produce a headline number.
  4. Replay the spine for covariates. Country, programme and phase are reconstructed as of the lab's received time, because that is the context in which the clock started. AI attribution — usage type, the agent event, the human sign-off — is reconstructed as of the decision's own event time, because that is when AI did or did not assist. Each field carries its own as-of timestamp and source event sequence.
  5. Compute the two numbers that matter inside the builder. The latency is decision event-time minus lab received-time. The row's identity is a stable hash over five inputs: tenant scope, lab id, decision id, pairing-rule version, snapshot-manifest hash. Both are computed off the snapshot, so nothing statistical ever touches the live graph.
  6. Build the metric tables. Median and P90 on edge-supported pairs only, with confidence intervals from a shared seeded bootstrap — two thousand resamples, clustered by enrollment, seeded from the snapshot manifest hash. Unanswered labs are right-censored at the fourteen-day window and feed a Kaplan–Meier-style time-to-decision estimate plus an explicit unpaired rate. A tenant-local, versioned business-hours latency is computed alongside wall-clock, and the dashboard can toggle between them.
  7. Listen for corrections. A correction listener maps each spine correction — to a lab, a decision, an evidence edge, an actor, or an AI attribution — through a dependency index to the exact pair rows whose identity depends on it, rebuilds only those, recomputes the affected cells, and bumps a correction epoch so the cache invalidates. Designed against a P95 of five minutes from correction to rebuilt rows.

The read path, and the order it runs in

Serving is deliberately boring, and the ordering is itself the guarantee:

  • Audit first, before any data is fetched. Viewer, target, purpose and time are written before the read, so an access is provable even if the response then fails midway. The access-log id comes back in the response envelope.
  • Build the canonical cache key. Three-field tenant scope, query namespace, the split-and-filter shape, pairing-rule version, metric schema hash, snapshot manifest hash, maximum source event sequence, and the correction epoch. Every one of those is something that, if it changed, would change the answer — so all of them are in the key.
  • Cache hit or miss changes only the recompute, never the governance. On a miss the API scans the metric table filtered to the caller's tenant partition. On a hit it reads finished cells. Either way the tenant assertion and the pseudonymisation still run.
  • Branch on role. Leadership sees all splits including per-doctor; a clinician sees the per-doctor split restricted to their own pseudonym; everyone else gets the per-doctor split stripped out of the response entirely — not blanked, absent.
  • Two gates, in this order. First pairing quality: if a segment's evidence-paired share falls below 0.80, that segment's headline is suppressed and only the pair-method breakdown is served. Then sample size: a cell with fewer than 30 supported pairs reports “insufficient data” with its full denominator breakdown. A segment must be trustworthy enough to headline before it can be large enough to headline.
  • Assert tenant on every cell, then return with provenance pins. A mismatch fails closed and pages the on-call. The envelope carries snapshot manifest, pairing-rule version, metric schema hash, correction epoch and the access-log id.

Nine years of history, hydrated rather than backfilled

This was never pointed at an empty graph. Roughly nine years of prior labs and treatment decisions already existed and were hydrated through the same append-only spine the live stream writes to — quarantine-first and proof-carrying, not a throwaway backfill. Each legacy payload-schema version gets its own upconverter; anything that cannot be mapped to a known schema or to a single tenant is quarantined with a reason rather than defaulted. Every event gets a content-addressed id and a conditional write, so hydrating twice is a no-op that yields an identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. Historical events replay through the identical projection and pairing code that serves the live stream. Proof gates — zero-event-loss reconciliation, hydrate-twice hash equality, as-of spot checks, a golden replay — all pass before a single latency table is built, and then a fenced cutover attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate.

Depth of history is not decoration here. It is what gives each segment — clinician, geography, AI-assisted or not — enough supported pairs to clear the thirty-per-cell floor and report a median and a P90 instead of collapsing into a wall of “insufficient data”.

Where the language model is: nowhere

Zero language-model calls anywhere in the pipeline, and that is an evaluation criterion rather than an aspiration. Latency is timestamp arithmetic; the quantiles, intervals and censoring-aware estimates are seeded, replayable statistics; the gates and access controls are deterministic code. There is no retrieval-augmented surface either, and that absence is a decision, not a gap: a median is read from a pre-computed cell and served with its confidence interval and pairing-quality caveat already attached, so there is nothing left to fetch and nothing a generated sentence could add. The platform's model seam — a self-hosted Llama 3.1 inside the VPC, used by sibling systems for parsing and narration — sits one layer up, and this service takes no dependency on it.

“Segmented by AI usage” means AI is measured, not used. The pipeline reconstructs from the spine, as of each decision's moment, whether and how AI assisted that decision, and reports it as an observational split.

03 Why did we do what we did?

Every significant decision, and the alternative it beat

This is the section that separates a Staff answer from a senior one. The mechanism is recallable; the reasoning is what an interviewer probes. Each block below is one decision, the alternative that was rejected, and why.

Only evidence-linked pairs may produce the headline

Decision

The reported median and P90 are computed only on supported_by_edge rows — pairs where the decision explicitly cites the lab as its evidence in the decision graph. This is enforced in code and checked in evaluation; it is called the headline lock.

Rejected

A nearest-in-time join, or folding the heuristic “next clinically relevant decision within fourteen days” into the headline to raise coverage.

Because

Correspondence is a clinical judgment, not a temporal fact. An explicit evidence link is the only signal that a decision actually answered the lab rather than merely followed it. The headline is built on the smallest, strongest evidence set, not the largest available one.

“The lock is what stops ‘more coverage’ from silently becoming ‘less truth’.” Verbatim, from the design

Heuristic pairs stay a diagnostic slice — no confidence weighting

Decision

Heuristic pairs are computed, kept and shown as a separate slice, promotable only when a clinical validation study earns it — and then as a new pairing-rule version, never a silent reweighting.

Rejected

Folding them into the headline with a confidence weight, which is the sophisticated-sounding option.

Because

Choosing that weight requires knowing how often the next-relevant decision really answered the lab — which is precisely the clinical question the design refuses to guess at.

“A weight is an unvalidated assumption dressed as rigor.” Verbatim, from the design

A second, ungated lab feed supplies the denominator

Decision

Read a separate ungated feed that returns every high-risk lab, paired or not, rather than the platform's standard projection. Described in the case study as “the single most important line in the design”.

Rejected

Reusing the standard projection every sibling query reads — cheaper, one less dependency, and structurally wrong here.

Because

That projection materialises an observation only once it attaches to a decision, so unanswered labs would never enter the data.

“The platform would look fastest exactly where it is failing worst.” Verbatim, from the design

Right-censor the unanswered labs

Decision

Labs with no decision are right-censored at the fourteen-day window and flow into a Kaplan–Meier-style time-to-decision estimate, alongside an explicit unpaired rate reported on every surface. Nothing is deleted; everything is labelled.

Rejected

Two tempting shortcuts — drop them (survivorship bias, the number flatters itself), or set them all to fourteen days (invents a latency they never had and inflates the tail with a fiction).

Because

Censoring uses the fact that the lab was unanswered as of the window edge to shape the curve, biasing neither toward optimism nor pessimism — which is exactly why survival analysis exists.

“Censoring is the honest middle… It is the opposite of avoidance: it forces the unanswered cases into the estimate honestly.” Verbatim, answering the sceptic

Four timestamps kept, exactly two subtracted

Decision

Every event carries four distinct timestamps — when the thing clinically happened, when it was observed, when it reached the platform, when it landed in the store — and the latency definition pins exactly two: decision event-time minus lab received-time.

Rejected

Collapsing to a single “timestamp” column, the shape most pipelines default to.

Because

Outpatient labs can be resulted days before the platform receives them, so measured-at and received-at differ by hours to days; picking the wrong pair moves the answer by that whole gap. Keeping four and pinning two means the number always means the same thing.

“Subtract an ingestion timestamp from a write timestamp and you are measuring the database, not the clinic.” Verbatim, from the design

Covariates reconstructed as-of, never read from current state

Decision

Replay the append-only spine to recover country, programme and phase as of the lab's received time, and AI attribution as of the decision's event time. Each field carries its own as-of timestamp and source event sequence; ordering breaks ties by sequence, never by write order.

Rejected

Reading the patient's current record — one join, no replay cost. Also rejected: a single as-of timestamp for the whole pair, for simplicity.

Because

A patient who later moved country or switched programme would silently rewrite the segment of a two-year-old pair, so the geography split would measure migration as much as clinical speed. And the two sides of a pair genuinely live in different moments — the lab's context is what was true when it arrived; AI assistance is a property of the decision made later. Forcing one timestamp would either backdate the AI attribution to before the decision existed or advance the patient's context past the lab.

Snapshot-only, never a live graph traversal at request time

Decision

Freeze the graph once nightly at a single watermark and run the whole build over the frozen copy; the request path reads pre-computed cells.

Rejected

Per-lab live traversal of the graph at query time.

Because

A live traversal is non-deterministic under concurrent writes, hard to tenant-partition, and slow enough to be a poor fit for a batch analytics question. Freezing makes the build replayable to a byte-identical result, lets output be partitioned and cached by tenant and snapshot hash, and moves all the graph cost into one nightly job. The trade is freshness — handled explicitly with staleness tiers rather than by pretending the number is real-time.

A deterministic pair identity over five inputs

Decision

Each row's name is a stable hash of tenant scope, lab id, decision id, pairing-rule version and snapshot-manifest hash — plus a deterministic sort (decision event time, then source event sequence, then decision id) that breaks every tie identically every night.

Rejected

Surrogate row ids and a full-table rebuild on every correction.

Because

A full rebuild cannot meet a five-minute correction-freshness target — a corrected lab receipt time would wait for the next nightly pass. Stable ids are what make a targeted rebuild possible, and what let the golden regression demand equality down to the row id.

“The stable-id investment pays for itself the first time a correction lands.” Verbatim, from the design

Every provenance input goes into the cache key

Decision

Tenant scope, query namespace, split-and-filter shape, pairing-rule version, metric schema hash, snapshot manifest hash, event-sequence watermark, correction epoch — all in the key.

Rejected

The conventional key of query shape plus a time-to-live.

Because

The failure being designed against is serving a stale number under a fresh name — the classic cache bug where the data or the rule moved but the key did not. Having the tenant in the key also means a cache hit can never become a cross-tenant read.

“An under-specified key is worse than no cache, because it fails silently and confidently.” Verbatim, from the design

Suppression gates instead of a best-effort number

Decision

Below 0.80 evidence-paired share in a segment, suppress the headline and serve the pair-method breakdown; below 30 supported pairs in a cell, emit “insufficient data” with its full denominator breakdown. Pairing quality gates first, sample size second.

Rejected

Showing a smooth median with a caveat in a tooltip.

Because

Leadership then sees the data-completeness problem — which is the actionable finding — rather than a confident number that would send them chasing a phantom.

“A suppressed cell is a visible admission, not a hidden failure; hiding would be emitting the untrustworthy median.” Verbatim, answering “aren't the gates just hiding bad numbers?”

Bootstrap clustered by enrollment, not by row

Decision

Two thousand resamples of whole enrollments, seeded from the snapshot manifest hash, intervals returned for both the median and the P90 — one shared implementation used by every cohort query on the platform.

Rejected

Resampling individual pair rows, and hand-rolling the bootstrap per query.

Because

A single patient contributes several correlated pairs; treating each row as independent would understate the uncertainty and produce an interval that is too narrow. Resampling enrollments makes the interval reflect how many independent patients stand behind a cell.

“It is the difference between an honest interval and a confidently wrong one.” Verbatim, on clustering

Audit written before the read, and governance on the warm path too

Decision

Viewer, target, purpose and time are logged before any data is fetched; the tenant assertion and pseudonymisation run identically on a cache hit and a cache miss; ordering in the per-clinician view is alphabetic and deliberately not rank-sortable.

Rejected

Logging after a successful response, and letting the warm path skip the checks for speed.

Because

Logging afterwards misses exactly the reads that errored — the ones most worth having a record of. And the warm path skips the recompute, never the governance.

“A determined misuse is possible but it is deliberate, effortful, and provable after the fact, which is the honest bar for governance of performance data.” Verbatim, on whether leadership could just re-rank the data themselves

Fail closed on tenancy, and page rather than degrade

Decision

Three redundant layers — a tenant-scoped build, tenant-partitioned files, and a fail-closed assertion on every served cell. A mismatch refuses the response and pages the on-call; the acceptance bar is zero, as a page-out, not a rate to trend down.

Rejected

Trusting the partition filter alone, and treating cross-tenant leakage as a metric to drive toward zero over time.

Because

The filter and the assertion fail for different reasons, so trusting only the filter means one query bug is enough to leak. And a single leaked cell is already a breach, not a statistic.

“The cost of a false alarm is a page; the cost of failing open is a disclosure.” Verbatim, on fail-closed

A business-hours view alongside wall-clock

Decision

A tenant-local, versioned business-hours calendar computes an adjusted latency that excludes closed hours; both views ship and the dashboard toggles between them.

Rejected

Wall-clock only — simpler, and quietly unfair to any clinic in an inconvenient time zone.

Because

Without it the geography split would partly be measuring time zones and opening hours rather than clinical responsiveness.

“A lab arriving at midnight is not evidence that the clinic slept on it.” Verbatim, from the design

Zero language-model calls, by design and by evaluation

Decision

No model on the request path, none in the build path, and no retrieval-augmented surface — with “zero language-model calls” written as an evaluation criterion so accidental use would show up as a violated invariant rather than a production surprise.

Rejected

Using the in-VPC model seam the sibling systems already depend on, for narration or for a natural-language query surface.

Because

There is no step here a language model would improve, and every step is one it could quietly degrade. A median read from a pre-computed cell already ships with its interval and its caveat attached.

“Numbers a regulator may one day audit should not have a language model anywhere in their provenance.” Verbatim — the line to land this on

The design review this absorbed — and what changed

Before anything shipped, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens. The first pass returned eighteen findings, five of them high-severity in correctness alone: unstable pairing rules, an undefined notion of the “corresponding” decision, wrong time semantics, missing tenant isolation, the gated-denominator exposure, and the governance finding that per-clinician latency is performance data. The verdict was ship-with-changes, and all eighteen were dispositioned.

Eleven absorbed into the v2 design — including
  • The pair-method split, with its headline lock
  • Versioned pairing rules and a versioned decision-type allowlist
  • The four-timestamp contract
  • Tenant-scoped build and tenant partitioning
  • Censoring-aware statistics
  • The canonical cache key
Seven routed to shared platform components
  • The tenant-scoped traversal wrapper
  • The correction backbone
  • The ungated observation projection
  • The deterministic exporter
  • The event-time schema
  • The shared bootstrap library
  • The actor-access policy

All seven have since landed at the platform layer, and this query now binds to the shared versions rather than the inline stand-ins it was originally wired against — so the sibling cohort queries inherit the fixes instead of reimplementing them.

Say the verdict correctly. “Ship-with-changes” is the outcome of an internal design review — a sign-off on the design, not a deployment status. The service shipped: it runs in production on the client's self-hosted, in-VPC stack, serving governed operations dashboards, after executive sign-off on the design. If an interviewer reads “ship-with-changes” as “not shipped”, correct it in one sentence and move on.

What I would do differently — have this ready, it is asked often

Push the ungated-observation-projection and event-time-schema contracts up to the platform before building the query, rather than absorbing them inline and routing them to shared components afterwards. Discovering during review that the standard projection drops unanswered labs — the very denominator this query lives on — meant the fix had to be retrofitted as a platform item mid-design. Sequencing those two contracts first would have let this query and every sibling inherit them from day one.

Why this query was built first

It is the simplest cohort-aggregation question the platform needed, which makes it the right place to work out the patterns the whole family shares. Its building blocks — the pair-method split, the canonical cache key, the seeded statistics, the actor-governance pattern — were then reused by five sibling cohort questions rather than reimplemented per query. Solving the hard general problems once, under review, before five other queries depend on them, is the point.

04 Architecture

Four diagrams, each redrawable on a whiteboard

These are the diagrams from the case study, unchanged. If there is a whiteboard in the room, draw the first one; if the interviewer pushes on honesty, draw the second; on the clock, the third; on serving and governance, the fourth.

Walkthrough

Everything starts at the event spine — an append-only log in DynamoDB, the source of truth, never edited. One projection reads it and maintains two things that look similar and are not. The first is the decision graph in Neptune, which holds clinical decisions and, critically, the evidence edges linking a decision to the lab it answered. The second is the ungated observation snapshot: every high-risk lab, paired or not.

Once a night, an exporter freezes the graph — vertices, edges, and a manifest carrying the as-of watermark and a content hash. The pair builder then consumes both arrows: the frozen snapshot for the decisions, and the ungated feed for the full population of labs. It runs tenant-scoped columnar joins over files — there is no per-lab call back into the graph. Its output lands in metric tables as Parquet files, pre-computed and partitioned by tenant and snapshot hash. The latency API reads only those, applying audit, role gates, and tenant assertion, and serves the governed dashboard.

The shape to notice on a whiteboard: the arrow from the graph goes up into the nightly export, never sideways into the API. The request path terminates at the metric tables.

Without the OBS → BUILD arrow the whole thing collapses into the flattering version. Delete that one edge and the builder only ever sees labs that already reached a decision, the denominator becomes “answered labs”, and the dashboard reports its best numbers for the clinics that are answering nothing. Without the SNAP freeze, two identical requests can disagree, the output cannot be partitioned by a stable hash, and the replay guarantee that everything else rests on is gone.

Walkthrough

This is the diagram to draw when someone asks how you know the number is honest. The whole population enters at the top — every high-risk lab in the snapshot window, which is only possible because of the ungated feed in Fig 1. One diamond, three exits, and every lab takes exactly one of them.

Left branch: an evidence edge exists, so the decision explicitly cited this lab. Only these rows reach the headline median, P90 and confidence intervals. Middle branch: no edge, but a clinically relevant decision — defined by a versioned allowlist of decision types, not “any next decision” — followed within fourteen days. These are computed and shown, but in a separate diagnostic slice, held back until a clinical validation study sanctions them. Right branch: nothing followed at all. These are right-censored at fourteen days and feed the survival estimate, plus an explicit unpaired rate that is reported on every surface.

The gate that polices the split sits one level up and is not drawn here: if any segment's evidence-paired share falls under 0.80, that segment's headline is suppressed and only this three-way breakdown is served.

Without the split — one arrow from the diamond straight to “headline” — you get a larger N, a smoother median, and a number no reader can distinguish from the honest one. Without the right branch you get textbook survivorship bias. Without the middle branch's quarantine, an unvalidated heuristic silently sets clinical policy.

Walkthrough

Eight boxes, one dotted line, and it is the fastest diagram to draw. Each side of the pair keeps four timestamps in the same order: event_time is when the thing happened in the world — the lab was resulted, the decision was made. observed_at is when it was observed. received_at is when it reached the platform. written_at is when it landed in the store.

The dotted line is the entire latency definition: lab received_at to decision event_time. Received-at on the lab side, because the clock starts when the platform could have acted; event-time on the decision side, because that is when the clinician actually decided. Every other timestamp is kept for provenance and for the guards, and none of them enter the subtraction.

The related invariant, from the hydration side of the system: written_at is the only field pinned to the bulk-load fence, and a per-row guard asserts every historical event's real-world time falls strictly before its ingestion time — so a value that accidentally defaulted to the load date fails the load rather than poisoning a distribution.

Pick the wrong two boxes and you have a plausible dashboard measuring pipeline throughput. Subtract written_at from written_at and nine years of history compresses toward zero, because every hydrated record shares one load date. As the design puts it: the invariant is cheap; the failure it prevents is unrecoverable.

Walkthrough

Read it top to bottom as a fixed order, because the order is the guarantee. A request arrives asking for latency split by country and AI-assist. Step one, before anything is read: write the audit entry — viewer, target, purpose, time. Not after the response; before the fetch, so a read that errors halfway still leaves a record.

Step two: build the canonical cache key from every input that could change the answer — the three-field tenant scope, the split and filters, the pairing-rule version, the metric schema hash, the snapshot hash, the event-sequence watermark, and the correction epoch. Step three: the cache diamond. Notice where the hit arrow goes — it skips the scan and the recompute, and rejoins at “assert tenant on every cell”. The warm path never skips governance.

On a miss: scan the metric table filtered to the caller's tenant partition, then branch on role. Leadership gets all splits. A clinician gets the per-doctor split for their own pseudonym only. Anyone else gets it stripped from the response entirely. All three role branches then converge on the same two gates, in this order: pairing quality ≥ 0.80 per segment, then ≥ 30 supported pairs per cell. Note that the “below” arrow from the pairing gate does not exit — it flows into the size gate too, so a low-pairing segment is also size-checked before anything is emitted.

Whatever the outcome — a full emission with median, P90, intervals, survival median and business-hours latency, or an honest “insufficient data” — every path converges on the tenant assertion, and only then returns with its provenance pins and populates the cache.

Move the audit node below the cache and you stop logging exactly the accesses that failed. Let the cache-hit arrow go straight to “return” and a warm response bypasses the tenant assertion and the pseudonymisation — the single most dangerous edit you could make to this diagram. Swap the two gates and you can emit a large-sample median built on evidence you have already judged untrustworthy. Drop the correction epoch from the key and a surgical rebuild lands in the store while the cache keeps serving the pre-correction number under the same name.

05 Likely questions

Headline first — the first sentence has to land alone

Answer with the bold line, then stop. Expand only if they ask, and expand in the order below.

Q1Why isn't this just “join the lab to the next decision, subtract two timestamps, take the median”?

Headline

Because every clause of that sentence hides a way to ship a confident, wrong number.

Four specific ways, each with a named control. “Next decision” pairs a lab with an unrelated action, so only decisions that explicitly cite the lab as evidence may headline. “The median” silently deletes the labs nobody answered, so the denominator comes from an ungated feed and unanswered labs are right-censored. “Two timestamps” can measure the database instead of the clinic, so four timestamps are kept and exactly two are pinned. “Group by clinician” renders as a scorecard the instant it draws, so the per-clinician view is pseudonymous, role-gated, audit-logged and deliberately unrankable.

Q2Your headline depends on the product attaching evidence links. Isn't that one UI bug away from collapse?

Headline

It is a genuine dependency, and the design treats it as one rather than assuming it away — a pairing-drift monitor watches it, and a segment that falls below the floor degrades to “we can't say”, never to a wrong number.

Evidence-link completeness is tracked per refresh, broken down by clinician, geography, lab type and UI version. The UI-version cut is the important one: a product change that quietly stops citing the lab shows up as drift on that version before it skews a headline, which turns a subtle front-end regression into a pageable analytics signal.

The second half matters for fairness. A missing edge is treated as a data-completeness signal, not a clinician judgment — if a doctor made the decision but didn't attach the lab, the drift monitor diagnoses it as a product problem rather than pinning it on a person, and the segment's headline is suppressed rather than under-reported.

Q3Isn't a per-clinician latency number inherently a scorecard, no matter how you dress it up?

Headline

Yes — it reads as one the moment it renders, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest; that is exactly why governance is a first-class part of the design rather than a footnote.

Concretely: clinicians are pseudonymous by default; the per-clinician split exists in a response only for leadership or the clinician's own view; an audit entry with viewer, target, purpose and time is written before any actor-level read, and another for any drill-in; the ordering is alphabetic and deliberately not rank-sortable; and every surface carries an observational tag.

If they push — “couldn't leadership export it and re-rank it themselves?” — the honest answer is yes, and that the controls raise friction and visibility rather than making it physically impossible. A determined misuse is possible, but it is deliberate, effortful, and provable after the fact. Claiming it can never become a scorecard would overstate what any access control can do. Conceding this cleanly is stronger than defending it.

Q4What do you do with the labs nobody ever answered?

Headline

They are right-censored at the fourteen-day window and carried into a Kaplan–Meier-style time-to-decision estimate, plus an explicit unpaired rate on every surface — nothing is deleted, everything is labelled.

The two alternatives are both wrong in interesting ways. Dropping them is survivorship bias: the slow and missing cases vanish and the number flatters itself, worst exactly where performance is worst. Capping them at fourteen days invents a latency they never had and inflates the tail with a fiction. Censoring is the honest middle — it uses “unanswered as of day fourteen” to shape the survival curve, biasing neither toward optimism nor pessimism, which is precisely what survival analysis exists for.

If a sceptic calls censoring a sophisticated way to avoid admitting labs go unanswered: the unpaired rate is a reported headline fact on every surface, so the unanswered set is not buried. Censoring is the opposite of avoidance — it forces those cases into the estimate.

Q5Snapshot-only means the dashboard is stale by construction. How is that acceptable for an operational tool?

Headline

Decision latency is a trend-and-distribution metric, not a real-time alert — what would be unacceptable is silent staleness, so freshness is made visible and bounded rather than eliminated.

Visible: freshness tiers — under 24 hours serves normally, 24 to 48 hours serves flagged as stale, past 48 hours the tier turns red. Separately, projection lag over thirty minutes flags before the snapshot looks stale, because a lagging projector produces a fresh-looking but incomplete snapshot, which is the most dangerous kind. Two separate signals, deliberately, so incomplete-but-recent cannot pass as healthy.

Bounded: corrections short-circuit the nightly cadence. A correction listener maps each spine correction through a dependency index to the exact pair rows whose identity depends on it and rebuilds only those, against a P95 five-minute target. That targeted rebuild is only possible because pair identities are deterministic hashes.

When would you choose live instead? For real-time alerting — “this urgent lab has gone unanswered for six hours, page someone”. That is a different product with a different requirement: immediacy, and no need to be replayable. This one has to be auditable, reproducible and tenant-partitioned, so snapshot-only is the right trade.

Q6How do you stop a cross-tenant cell from ever being served?

Headline

Three deliberately redundant layers — a tenant-scoped build, tenant-partitioned files, and a fail-closed assertion on every cell before it leaves the API — because each catches a different class of bug.

Tenancy is a three-field scope — account, workspace, control unit — resolved from the caller's authenticated context, never passed in as a parameter. A single-field check would be insufficient because one account can span workspaces that must not see each other's data.

  • In the build: join predicates require all three fields to match on both sides, so a cross-tenant pair is not even a candidate, and a write-time assertion re-checks before the pair table persists.
  • In storage: Parquet files are partitioned by the three-field scope.
  • At serve: every cell is re-checked against the caller. A mismatch fails closed and pages the on-call.
  • In the cache: all three fields are in the canonical key, so a cached cell for one tenant cannot be returned to another — the keys simply do not collide.

The acceptance bar is zero, as a page-out — deliberately not a rate you drive down over time, because a single leaked cell is already a breach. The cost of a false alarm is a page; the cost of failing open is a disclosure.

Q7You had a self-hosted language model available on the platform and used none. Why?

Headline

Because numbers a regulator may one day audit should not have a language model anywhere in their provenance — and there is no step in this pipeline a model would improve, though several it could quietly degrade.

Latency is timestamp arithmetic. The quantiles, intervals and censoring-aware estimates are seeded, replayable statistics. The gates, suppression rules and access controls are deterministic code. There is no retrieval-augmented surface either, and that absence is a decision rather than a gap: a median is read from a pre-computed cell and served with its confidence interval and pairing-quality caveat already attached, so there is nothing left to fetch and nothing a generated sentence could add.

Guarding it: “zero language-model calls” is an evaluation criterion, so accidental use would surface as a violated invariant rather than a production surprise. The platform's model seam — a self-hosted Llama 3.1 inside the client's VPC — serves the sibling retrieval and override-analytics systems one layer up; this service is drawn to never touch it.

And on the split itself: the AI in the question is measured, not used. The pipeline reconstructs from the event spine, as of each decision's moment, whether and how AI assisted — usage type, agent event, human sign-off — and reports it as an observational split.

Q8Be concrete: what can this system genuinely not answer today?

Headline

Three things, stated on the surface rather than buried — it cannot claim AI assistance causes faster decisions, it cannot give a number for any segment below the pairing or sample-size floor, and it cannot see through a systematic evidence-attachment gap.

The AI split is association, never effect. Clinicians reach for AI assistance on different kinds of cases, so any latency difference reflects that selection as much as any effect of the assistance; matching would balance only the covariates that were measured, and unmeasured confounding is never ruled out. The comparison ships with observational caveats and is blocked outright when pairing quality differs materially between the arms — because unequal data completeness between arms would masquerade as an AI effect.

Suppressed is not estimated. Below the floors, cells say “insufficient data” with their full denominator breakdown. That is a deliberate choice of a smaller, trustworthy denominator over a larger, guessed one — the cost is coverage on segments with poor edge capture.

Two honest open edges. Many-to-one correspondence — a single review that resolves a batch of labs, or a lab genuinely addressed across several decisions — sits at the edge of the pairing definition; the rule is versioned and deterministic so its behaviour is inspectable, but the clinical definition in those cases is an open question. And the denominator depends on an upstream feed this query does not own: a silent, total outage would shrink it, and while the pairing and unpaired-rate monitors would flag an anomalous shift, that dependency is named plainly rather than hidden.

If they ask “so what did it measure in production?” — the honest answer is that every figure attached to this design is a pre-registered acceptance target: the bar the system was designed, reviewed and deployed against. The observed operating numbers are the client's, and are not mine to publish.
