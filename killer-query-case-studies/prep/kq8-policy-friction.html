Interview prep · Killer query 8

Policy friction — a count that must never become a verdict

Which platform rules do clinicians keep overriding, and do those overrides track better or worse patient outcomes? A production analytics service on a chronic-care platform covering 80+ chronic conditions, with roughly nine years of patient history hydrated behind it — and no language model anywhere in the pipeline.

The verbatim brief
“Which policy constraints most frequently trigger overrides? Which correlate with worse outcomes?”
Open with these two sentences

It looks like one GROUP BY over the rulebook — count the overrides per rule, compare outcomes when it was followed against when it was broken.

It is one bad label away from a machine that puts rules — and, by proximity, the clinicians who break them — on trial.

Standing honesty guard. Every figure on this page is a pre-registered acceptance target, a design constant, or an illustrative fixture value. There is not one measured production metric here — no achieved latency, no adoption number, no error reduction, no cost saved. If asked “what did it actually move?”, the honest answer is: I left before the outcome window closed, so I don’t have measured results — what I can give you is the acceptance gates the system was reviewed and deployed against. Say that, then keep going.

01 What is it?

A governance owner has to decide which rules to change

Start with the person, not the pipeline. There are two of them, and they are standing at opposite ends of the same fact.

The clinician

A clinician is treating a patient whose kidney function has been drifting. The platform holds a rule — a dose ceiling on a particular drug — and when she starts writing the prescription, the platform flags it. She writes it anyway, and logs that she did.

That is a policy override, and it is worth being precise about what it is, because interviewers assume it is inferred: it is not. Every decision carries a recorded alignment field against each rule that constrained it. When that field reads overridden, the clinician acted against the rule and said so. Nothing is mined out of free text, and no model reads her notes.

The governance owner

Now stand at the other end. Across 80+ chronic conditions, roughly 10,000 patients, and about nine years of history, some rules get overridden constantly and some almost never. A policy-governance committee has to decide which rules to revise — and a rule change on a chronic-care platform propagates to every clinician and every patient on it. The question they bring is exactly the brief above.

Why it is genuinely hard

The counting is trivial. It would be a day’s work. Everything else exists to stop the count from becoming a verdict — and this query is more dangerous than its decision-indexed sister, which asks the same thing about individual override decisions. A per-rule signal is exactly the shape that triggers a policy revision or a model retraining. A wrong signal here rewrites the rulebook for everyone.

1

Confounding, built in by design

Clinicians override on their sickest and most atypical patients — that is what clinical judgement is for. So the followed group and the overridden group differ in baseline risk before the rule is even evaluated.

Naive failure: a raw followed-versus-overridden outcome delta measures patient mix, not the rule. It will reliably show that overriding leads to worse outcomes, because overrides land on sicker people.

2

False confidence at scale

The map scores dozens of policy versions at once. If every rule on the platform were perfectly neutral, a handful would still look damning by chance alone.

Naive failure: a sorted league table of rules where positions 3 and 8 are statistically indistinguishable, read by a committee as a ranking.

3

Verdict misuse

The moment a cell reads “policy too strict” or “clinicians ignoring evidence”, a governance action fires off a machine’s opinion.

Naive failure: the label is the product. Everyone reads the string and nobody reads the interval underneath it.

4

Grouping by a mutable rule id

A rule’s text changes over time under a stable id — a threshold moves, an exception is added.

Naive failure: three genuinely different rules pool into one cell and smear their override rates together, and editing a rule tomorrow silently rewrites what yesterday’s version looked like.

5

Reading current state for a historical decision

A rule retexted after the decision, an alignment flipped by a later correction, or an exception added months on will all bleed backward.

Naive failure: the override is scored against a rule that did not exist when it was made, and the map blames a version of the rulebook nobody was working under.

State the ceiling before they find it. Nothing this system produces is causal. Every cell is a matched-cohort association, labelled observational. The brief says “correlate”, and that verb is honoured literally on every surface. Matching balances only what was measured; unmeasured confounding is never ruled out. Saying this first turns the interviewer’s best objection into your own framing.

Vocabulary — everything expanded, in reading order

Policy override
An explicit recorded field on a clinical decision saying the clinician acted against a platform rule. Not inferred, not modelled — logged.
Propensity matching
Pairing each override with a non-override decision whose patient looks statistically alike on measured baseline characteristics, so the comparison is like-for-like instead of sick-versus-healthy.
Caliper
How close a candidate has to be before it counts as a match. Tight caliper: fewer, better pairs. Loose caliper: more pairs, worse similarity.
Common support
A check that the two arms actually overlap in the range where they are being compared. If no overridden patient looks anything like any followed patient, there is no comparison to make.
SMD (standardized mean difference)
A unit-free measure of how far apart the two arms still are on a covariate after matching. The balance gate. Above the threshold, the arms are not comparable and the delta is suppressed.
CI (confidence interval)
The range the estimate is compatible with. If the interval crosses zero, the direction of the effect is not distinguishable from “no effect”.
Bootstrap
Building that interval by resampling the data thousands of times rather than assuming a formula. Here: 2,000 resamples, percentile method, seeded from the snapshot hash.
Clustered by enrollment
Resampling whole enrollments rather than individual rows, because several decisions inside one enrollment are correlated. Resampling rows independently would fake precision.
FDR (false discovery rate) and the BH (Benjamini–Hochberg) q-value
A way of accounting for the fact that testing many rules at once produces false positives. The q-value is the per-cell version of that accounting. Here it is reported, never used as a filter.
As-of reconstruction
Computing every historical fact using only what was true at or before the decision’s own moment — never current state.
Event spine
The append-only log of everything that happened, in order. The source of truth. Facts are replayed out of it rather than read off a mutable graph.
Snapshot manifest
A hash describing exactly which frozen inputs a build ran against. If two runs share a manifest, they saw the same world.
Correction epoch
A counter that advances every time a correction lands on the spine. It sits in the cache key so a cell computed before a correction can never be served after one.
Zone code
One of six neutral labels the data layer is allowed to emit. Never a judgement — a coordinate.
virtual private cloud (VPC) (virtual private cloud)
The client’s own isolated network. Protected health information never crosses that boundary; everything runs inside it.
LLM (large language model)
The thing this system deliberately does not contain. See section 3.
95th-percentile (P95)
The 95th percentile — the value 95% of measurements come in under. Used here as an service-level agreement (SLA) (service-level agreement) target, not a measured result.
LRU (least-recently-used) cache
An in-process cache that evicts whatever was touched longest ago. It sits in front of the pre-computed cells — and the key, not a timer, is what makes a hit safe.
TTL (time to live)
Expiring a cache entry after a fixed duration. Explicitly rejected here in favour of keying on every result-affecting input.
02 How did we solve it?

Snapshot-only analytics, indexed by immutable policy version

Two clocks. A nightly offline lane does all the work and writes pre-computed cells; the request lane is a governed lookup over those cells. Nothing a human waits on touches the live graph, runs a statistic, or calls a model.

The offline lane — what happens every night

Freeze a snapshot

State is frozen at an as_of_ms: vertices, edges, a manifest hash, the maximum source event sequence consumed, and an allowed-lateness watermark. Everything downstream is pinned to that manifest, so a reviewer months later can regenerate the identical number.

Rebuild each override as of the moment it happened

The cohort builder does tenant-scoped analytical joins over frozen columnar files plus targeted reads of the append-only spine — no live graph traversal at any point. For each decision it reconstructs, at that decision’s own event time and source-sequence position: the policy_alignment flag, the CONSTRAINED_BY edge to the rule, the policy_version_id resolved from the spine’s rule-creation and rule-revision events, and the immutable policy_text_hash.

One decision can be constrained by several rules at once. Each produces one row per rule, keyed by decision id plus policy_edge_seq, so the decision appears under multiple policy cells — and the intervals later refuse to treat those rows as independent observations, because they share an underlying decision.

Borrow the control arm, or rebuild it whole

This system does not build its own matched controls. The decision-indexed sister query already pairs each override with a propensity-matched control and ships that cohort with a versioned match manifest — one hash over seven fields: covariate_schema_hash, snapshot_manifest_hash, cohort_filter_hash, seed, caliper, replacement_policy, tie_break_policy.

All seven equal → reuse the arm verbatim, manifest_source = reused_from_sibling. Any one different → full rebuild with the same matcher binary, a new match_manifest_id, and a recorded rebuild_reason. It is strictly binary. There is no partial adoption and no fresh fallback for the rows the borrowed set missed.

Control eligibility is constrained too — same archetype, same policy family, same program and phase, same tenant, pre-treatment records only — and common support is checked, because a non-override from an unrelated program is not a plausible counterfactual even if its covariates happen to line up.

Label both arms from one shared outcome library

Not a per-team outcome definition. One implementation, emitting status, attribution_decision_id, rule_version, distance_days and censoring_reason. The 90-day outcome window is forward-looking on purpose and is only allowed to count once it has fully matured before the snapshot’s as_of. That is the leakage line: covariates must be as-of because they are inputs; the label is a gated future window, which is a different role.

Aggregate per policy version — never pooled across versions

Per policy_version_id, with four things computed: the SMD balance gate; a sensitivity sweep at threshold ±20% and ±50% crossed with strict, base and loose calipers; a seeded bootstrap confidence interval (2,000 resamples, percentile method, seed derived from the snapshot manifest hash, clustered by enrollment); and multiple-testing diagnostics — how many policies were tested, insufficient, borderline, uncertain — plus a Benjamini–Hochberg q-value at q = 0.10.

Classify into one of six neutral codes

Four zone codes — override rate high or low, crossed with outcome delta positive or negative: high_override_positive_delta, high_override_negative_delta, low_override_positive_delta, low_override_negative_delta — plus uncertain_zone when the interval crosses zero and borderline_zone when the sensitivity sweep is unstable. Conclusory strings are banned at the source: there is no policy_too_strict, no clinicians_ignoring, anywhere in the data layer.

Write the tables the request path reads

Parquet files queried by DuckDB — policy-fact, policy-zone and zone-history tables, partitioned by tenant scope, snapshot hash, policy version and threshold version. Plus a zone_history append for the quarter-over-quarter governance audit.

The gate ladder — the reasoning, as deterministic code

Before a cell may show a headline zone code it climbs four rungs, in this order:

RungTestFail behaviour
1 · PowerAt least 30 patients in each arm for this policy version, plus common support insufficient_data with full denominators — references, overrides, matched, unmatched, censored. No zone code.
2 · BalanceSMD within the gate across covariates Suppress the delta, emit a diagnostic instead of a headline.
3 · SensitivitySign and interval hold across every point of the sweep grid borderline_zone, recommendation withheld.
4 · IntervalOutcome confidence interval excludes zero uncertain_zone, neutral code only.

The ordering is cheapest-and-most-disqualifying first: a cell with twelve patients per arm never has a balance test spent on it. Every failure exits with a stated reason and its full denominators — never a silent gap, and never a confident code built on sand. Because the branch is deterministic, the same cell suppresses the same way on every rebuild, so an audit can replay exactly why a code was or was not shown.

The request lane — what a governance viewer actually triggers

Audit first, before anything is served

An access-log entry is written — viewer, target policy, purpose, time — and its access_log_id ships back with the rows. Reading a friction map is itself an event on the record.

Build a canonical cache key over every result-affecting input

The three-field tenant scope (account_id, workspace_id, controlunit_id), the query namespace and endpoint and filters, the metric schema hash, policy_version_id, threshold_version, match_manifest_id, the snapshot manifest hash, source_event_seq_max, and correction_epoch — a sorted canonical serialization. Not a TTL.

Look up a pre-computed, already-gated cell

The serving path does no statistics. It reads a cell that was gated and classified last night, out of an in-process LRU cache over the DuckDB tables.

Assert the tenant on every row on the way out

All three scope fields must match the caller, row by row. A cache hit still re-checks before anything leaves the process. One mismatched row fails the entire response — it is not filtered out and served anyway.

Return codes plus governed copy plus caveats

Rows, neutral zone codes, override rates, deltas and intervals, sensitivity and multiplicity diagnostics, the observational label, the interpretive wording (owned and versioned separately by the governance team), and the access-log id.

The contracts that hold it together

  • A gate-failing number is unrepresentable. The typed Pydantic cell contract carries a suppression validator that raises rather than emit a delta on a failed cell. The guarantee lives in the type, not in reviewer discipline.
  • Tenancy is structural. The filter is applied at the columnar scan, before load — data is partitioned by the three-field scope, so a foreign tenant’s bytes are never read into the working set. Fail-closed assertions run again in the builder and on every served row.
  • Freshness degrades visibly, then stops. Under 24 hours green; 24–48 hours yellow with a visible staleness banner; past 48 hours red, which blocks and escalates rather than serve quietly outdated statistics. Projector lag over 30 minutes also flips yellow.
  • Corrections rebuild only what they touch. Every derived row records its source-event sequence range and a dependency set indexed by policy version. A correction listener resolves through that index to exactly the affected cells, rebuilds those at the pinned snapshot, and invalidates by policy_version_id + correction_epoch — P95 under five minutes, as a pre-registered SLA.
  • Thresholds are versioned and owned by humans. Changing an override-rate or outcome-delta threshold mints a new threshold_version; previously computed zones stay queryable at their prior version, so a re-thresholding never silently rewrites past cells.
  • Cross-tenant pooling is not a default view. It requires separate authorization, emits a per-tenant heterogeneity report, and suppresses cells small enough to identify a tenant.

Nine years, hydrated first — and why that is the enabling condition

The system was not switched on against an empty world. Roughly nine years of prior decisions — every override, every rule version, and every outcome that followed — were hydrated through the same append-only spine before the first friction map was ever drawn. That depth is not a nice-to-have: a per-version cell only clears the thirty-per-arm floor if enough overrides accrued against that exact rule text over time. Rule versions rotate; a single recent quarter rarely accumulates thirty overrides against one version. Without the history, the map comes back mostly empty.

Hydration was a first-class, quarantine-first, proof-carrying pipeline, and it holds five lines it will not cross:

  1. Event time is not load time. Four timestamps: event_time_ms and observed_at_ms come from the record and are true of the world; received_at_ms and written_at_ms are pinned to a single load fence, not the load clock. The invariant is event_time_ms strictly before received_at_ms, and a nine-year gap is expected, not an anomaly to be dropped.
  2. Hydrating twice changes nothing. Content-addressed event ids written conditionally, so a re-run over the same corpus yields a byte-identical spine and an identical snapshot manifest hash. Idempotency as an executable proof, not a promise.
  3. Nothing is silently coerced. Each legacy payload generation gets an explicit up-converter; any row that cannot map to a known schema, or to a tenant, is quarantined with a reason and counted. Never defaulted.
  4. History runs the live code. Replayed history is projected through the exact same projection code that serves the live stream.
  5. The load proves itself. Zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before anything downstream is built — then a fenced cutover attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate.

Zero model calls

There is no language model in this system — not in the numbers, not in the labels, not as an optional narrator. The platform’s self-hosted model seam (a Llama 3.1 running inside the client VPC, so patient data never leaves the network) exists and is wired to the sibling queries that produce prose. On every path here it sits present and untouched. Section 3 has the argument.

03 Why did we do what we did?

Every decision, and the alternative it beat

This is the section to be fluent in. Each card is one decision, the thing that was rejected, and the reason — with the original reasoning quoted where the wording is worth keeping.

Match each override to a comparable control, rather than compare all overrides to all non-overrides

Rejected
The raw group-versus-group outcome delta.
Because
Overrides are selected onto sicker and more atypical patients, so the raw delta measures case mix.
“Comparing the whole overridden group to the whole followed group measures that case-mix gap, not the rule’s effect. Matching each override to a baseline-similar control removes the measured imbalance, so the remaining outcome difference is a cleaner (though still observational) read on the rule.”

Reuse the sister query’s matcher, not an independent one

Rejected
Building an independent matcher for this query, for decoupling.
Because
The two queries are the same overrides under different indexes. Two matchers make that claim unverifiable.
“Two independent matchers guarantee divergent cohorts and divergent intervals over time… Independence here would buy nothing and cost the one property — cross-query consistency — that the parity fixture is built to defend.”

Borrow the control arm verbatim or rebuild it whole — never blend

Rejected
The earlier design’s “fresh fallback”: borrow most matches, freshly compute matches for the rows the borrowed set missed.
Because
That produces a cohort that is neither the sister’s cohort nor a clean rebuild, so its confidence intervals are unverifiable and the two queries can silently diverge.
“There is no ‘fresh fallback’ that quietly matches a few unmatched rows a different way — that path was designed out, because two dashboards disagreeing about the same cohort is precisely the bug this removes.”

The rule is binary so that a cell’s provenance is always exactly one thing. And the coupling is explicit rather than hidden: a manifest mismatch is a normal logged event during a nightly refresh — usually the sister query re-ran under new inputs — that triggers a rebuild and continues, with the rebuild_reason telling a later reviewer why that night borrowed versus rebuilt.

Index by policy_version_id plus policy_text_hash, not the policy id

Rejected
Grouping by the stable, mutable rule id.
Because
A rule’s text changes under a stable id, so the mutable key pools distinct rules into one cell and lets an edit rewrite history.

The text hash is doing real work beyond being an id: it is the content address of the rule text. A cosmetic re-save that does not change a byte collapses to the same hash and does not fracture the history; a genuine edit mints a new hash and a new lineage. That is what makes “the same rule” provable across replays.

Snapshot-only analytics, not a live per-policy traversal

Rejected
Traversing the decision graph per policy at query time.
Because
A governance figure has to be reproducible and isolated from live latency.
“A live per-policy traversal would return a slightly different answer on every run as the graph mutates, and would couple the report’s latency to graph load. Freezing state at an as_of with a manifest hash means the same inputs always give the same friction map, and a reviewer months later can regenerate the identical number.”

Reconstruct override facts from the spine, not from current graph properties

Rejected
Reading the alignment flag and rule text off the live graph.
Because
A rule edited later would bleed backward into a decision that predates the edit.

This was one of the design review’s high-severity findings — “reading the current graph for historical policy facts” — and it is the single correctness bug most likely to survive a green test suite. See the honest answer about that in the questions below.

Six neutral zone codes; conclusory strings banned at the source

Rejected
Interpretive labels in the data layer — “policy too strict”, “clinicians ignoring evidence”.
Because
A string like that, emitted by code, reads as a machine’s verdict and can directly fire a governance action.
“The layer that computes the statistic is not allowed to also editorialize about it.”

Interpretation lives in governed user interface (UI) copy owned by the policy-governance team, labelled observational, and versioned separately from the numbers. A named human sits between the number and the decision. Note the honest limit that goes with this — nothing in the pipeline can stop a person downstream from reading a code as a verdict; the design makes that reading harder and traceable, not impossible.

Report the false-discovery q-value; do not gate on it

Rejected
Making the q-value a rung in the ladder — filtering cells out on multiplicity.
Because
A cell earns its code on power, balance, sensitivity and an interval excluding zero; multiplicity is a property of the whole map that the reader has to weigh.
“The q-value is a disclosure the reader must weigh, not a filter applied on their behalf.”

This is a precision point worth having ready, because interviewers routinely assume the opposite: the four emission rungs are unadjusted, and the exploratory label is what carries that limit.

Label the ranking exploratory; never offer a sort

Rejected
A rank-sortable league table of the worst rules.
Because
With multiplicity and wide intervals the order is genuinely noisy.
“The third and eighth cells may be statistically indistinguishable… keeps the map an evidence surface instead of a scoreboard the eye trusts more than the intervals warrant.”

A hard floor of 30 per arm — and no pretence it is more than a floor

Rejected
The original one-arm sample gate (a review finding), and any claim that thirty certifies power.
Because
Thirty rejects the obviously under-powered; it does not bless what clears it.
“A cell with thirty-one per arm and a huge interval is suppressed just the same. The floor gates entry; it does not bless what clears it.”

Bootstrap clustered by enrollment, not by row

Rejected
Independent row resampling.
Because
Multiple decisions within one enrollment are correlated; resampling rows independently would treat them as independent evidence and produce intervals that are too narrow.

And the residual honestly stated: if the true unit were coarser still — the same patient across multiple enrollments — the intervals could remain slightly optimistic. That is exactly why no zone rests on one interval; the sensitivity battery and the exploratory label backstop the resampling choice.

Sweep the thresholds and the caliper before believing anything

Rejected
Testing at ±20% only — which is what the first design did, and what the review flagged.
Because
The override-rate and outcome-delta thresholds are judgement calls, and a finding that survives only at one exact threshold is not a finding.
“Without the sweep, the most confident-looking headline would routinely be the most fragile one.”

The grid is ±20% and ±50% on the threshold, crossed with strict, base and loose calipers, on the same cohort and the same seed. A single sign flip or interval cross anywhere in the grid tags the cell borderline_zone.

Filter tenants at the scan, not after the join

Rejected
Post-filtering the joined result on a tenant predicate.
Because
An aggregate is computed over whatever rows enter the query.
“If a foreign tenant’s rows are loaded and a predicate is later forgotten on one branch, they contaminate a sum or a rate silently and then propagate into every downstream table. Scanning only the caller’s partitions means a forgotten predicate cannot leak a tenant, because those bytes were never in scope to begin with.”

Key the cache on every result-affecting input, not on a TTL

Rejected
Time-based expiry.
Because
Each key field changes the result, and expiry does not know that.
“Drop the threshold version and a re-thresholded map serves under the old key; drop the correction epoch and a cell computed before a correction serves after it; drop the tenant scope and you risk a cross-tenant hit. Time-to-live expiry is not freshness — only keying on every input that can move the number is.”

Rebuild only what a correction touches

Rejected
Both extremes: rebuild the whole map on every correction, or wait for the next nightly run.
Because
Neither meets the freshness contract without an unacceptable cost.
“A rebuild-everything policy would either miss the SLA or burn the cluster nightly; a do-nothing policy would keep serving a number the source has already retracted.”

Per-version cells, not one fitted model of override versus outcome

Rejected
A single model over overrides and outcomes.
Because
A fitted surface hides the per-rule evidence and the suppression logic, and invites a causal reading of its coefficients.
“Legibility to a human decision-maker is worth more here than predictive compression.”

Zero language-model calls — the purest case of knowing when not to use one

Rejected
A narrated friction map. Also rejected: a retrieval-augmented generation surface over the policy corpus.
Because
The output is a governance signal that can revise a rulebook or trigger a retraining, and a generated sentence editorialises a coordinate into a judgement.
“The moment a model is allowed to narrate one in prose it editorialises a policy into good or bad — the very verdict the codes exist to withhold.”

Three consequences worth naming out loud. Neutrality is code, not a prompt — the six codes are assigned by deterministic classification, so there is no prompt to jailbreak because no claim ever passes through a model. There is nothing to fail quiet — with no generated text there is no additive layer to lose. And on retrieval-augmented generation specifically: a governance signal has to be a matched-cohort statistic an auditor can trace back to its own denominators, not a passage retrieved from somewhere and paraphrased into a sentence that merely sounds authoritative.

The design review — seventeen findings, and what changed

Before anything shipped, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens. It drew blood: seventeen findings across correctness, statistics and governance, with a verdict of ship-with-changes — an unambiguous “do not implement as written”. The high-severity core, and what each one turned into:

Finding (round 1)What changed in v2
Cross-query sign-flip — opposite conclusions about the same cohort A cross-query parity fixture, wired in as a deliberately failing test, that must be green before either system releases
A “fresh fallback” silently building a different control group The path was designed out. Reuse is binary: verbatim on seven-field manifest equality, or a full rebuild with the same matcher binary
Reading the current graph for historical policy facts As-of reconstruction from the append-only spine at the decision’s own event time and sequence position

Fifteen findings were absorbed directly into the design body. The remaining two — the cross-query sign-flip and its manifest contract — were promoted into shared platform components: a tenant wrapper, the outcome library, the matcher binary, a deterministic exporter, a cache-key envelope, and the clustered bootstrap.

Say this precisely, because it is the one thing easy to garble. The hardened v2 is the design that shipped to production and it runs there. What remains open is the design-review approval, which the design deliberately held against itself: it does not get to call itself approved until the sibling system emits its match manifest and the cross-query parity fixture passes for every policy version. The nine platform components have landed; those two conditions have not. Landing components does not by itself convert a review verdict. Deployed and live; design-review verdict held at ship-with-changes, on the record, on purpose.

The fixture that started life red

Worth telling as a story, because it is the most convincing thirty seconds available. The parity check began as a failing test. On one policy version with 418 references and 71 overrides, this pipeline reported an outcome delta of −0.05 while its sibling reported +0.04 — opposite signs, same cohort, a contradiction that would have shipped to a governance dashboard. The two views are supposed to be the same overrides reindexed, so opposite signs mean one of them mis-joined outcomes or matched differently, and no downstream reading of either would be trustworthy. It had to go green before either system was allowed to release.

“A disagreement about human decisions should fail a test, not a career.”
04 Architecture

Four diagrams, and how to redraw each on a whiteboard

These are the diagrams from the case study, unmodified. If you can talk through the four walkthroughs below without the page, you can draw this system live.

Fig 1 · The full pipeline

Walkthrough

Read it as a single spine with one side-branch and one listener hanging off it.

  • Snapshot → cohort builder. A frozen snapshot enters; the builder emits one row per (decision, policy edge), each carrying the alignment flag, the constraining edge, and the policy version and text hash in force at that decision’s own event time. Nothing here reads the live graph.
  • Cohort → control arm. The seven-field manifest check runs. Reuse or full rebuild — no third path. Whichever fires is stamped on the rows.
  • Control arm → outcome label. Both arms are labelled by one shared library, so the treated and control outcomes cannot drift apart through two implementations.
  • Outcome → aggregate. Per policy version, never pooled. This is where the balance gate, the sensitivity grid, the seeded clustered bootstrap and the multiplicity diagnostics all run.
  • Aggregate → classifier → serve → eval. Six neutral codes, then an application programming interface (API) that audits, keys, and asserts tenancy; then the golden and parity fixtures that must be green.
  • The side branch: the aggregate also writes zone_history, so a rule’s friction can be followed quarter over quarter.
  • The listener: the correction-event listener dots into four stages — builder, control arm, aggregate and serve — because a correction can invalidate any of them. Dotted, because it is an out-of-band trigger, not a stage in the flow.
What breaks without it

Drop the snapshot freeze and the map returns a different answer every run. Drop the as-of reconstruction and a rule edited last month rewrites a decision made two years ago. Drop the per-version grouping and distinct rule texts smear into one cell. Drop the correction listener and the map keeps serving a figure the source has already retracted — or you rebuild everything nightly and burn the cluster to do it.

Fig 4 · The manifest contract

Walkthrough

A producer box, one predicate, two mutually exclusive outcomes, and a test that converges on both. That is the whole shape.

  • The producer is the sister query. It pairs each override with a propensity-matched control and hashes seven inputs into a single match_manifest_id.
  • The predicate is exact equality on all seven: covariate schema, snapshot manifest, cohort filters, seed, caliper, replacement policy, tie-break policy. Not “mostly the same”. All seven.
  • Yes → reuse verbatim, stamped reused_from_sibling. This is provably identical to a rebuild, because under identical inputs the matcher is deterministic — the borrowed pairs are the same pairs a rebuild would produce, cell for cell.
  • No → full rebuild with the same matcher binary, a new manifest id and a recorded rebuild_reason, stamped rebuilt_here.
  • Both arrows converge on the same claim: both systems report identical policy cells per policy version. And the parity fixture is drawn as a node because it is a release gate, not documentation.

Two details worth having ready. The manifest is tenant-bound — the cohort filter hash and the snapshot manifest hash are both tenant-scoped, so a foreign-tenant cohort cannot be silently borrowed; a scope difference changes the manifest and forces an in-tenant rebuild. And the reuse path is tested against a rebuild under an identical manifest: if they differ, the conclusion is not “reuse is fine anyway”, it is “the manifest is incomplete” — some result-affecting input is missing from the seven fields.

What breaks without it

Without exact-equality gating you get the fresh fallback: a cohort that is neither the sister’s nor a clean rebuild, with unverifiable intervals. Without the same matcher binary, two implementations drift and the “same overrides, two indexings” claim becomes unfalsifiable. Without the parity fixture, the sign-flip ships — two dashboards telling a governance board opposite stories about the same rule.

Fig 5 · The gate ladder as code

Walkthrough

This is the diagram to draw if you only get to draw one — it is the query’s reasoning made literal, and it is the answer to “where is the chain of thought?” The answer is: it is code, it is seeded, and it replays.

  • Audit before anything. The very first node after the read is the access log — viewer, target policy, purpose. The log entry exists before a result does.
  • Then the key, spanning every result-affecting input. Then a cache-hit branch.
  • Four gates in a fixed order, each with exactly one escape: power → insufficient_data; balance → suppress the delta, keep the diagnostic; sensitivity → borderline_zone; interval → uncertain_zone. Only a cell that clears all four reaches EMIT.
  • Every path reconverges on the tenant assertion. This is the structurally important bit: the suppression exits and the emit path and the cache-hit path all flow into TEN. There is no route out of this diagram that skips the per-row tenant check — including a cache hit.
  • Then return and cache.

Note what the escapes carry. A failed cell is not a blank; it leaves with its full denominators — references, overrides, matched, unmatched, censored — and a stated reason. And uncertain_zone versus borderline_zone are not synonyms: uncertain means the interval crosses zero, so there is no distinguishable delta; borderline means a delta exists at the base setting but does not survive perturbation. Different failures, different messages to a reviewer.

What breaks without it

Skip the power rung and a twelve-patient cell gets a confident code. Skip balance and you publish a case-mix artifact. Skip sensitivity and the most confident-looking headline is routinely the most fragile. Skip the interval rung and you are back at the review finding — zone labels assigned from point estimates while the interval crossed zero. And if the cache-hit path bypassed the tenant assertion, one poisoned key is a cross-tenant leak on a governance surface.

Fig 11 · The request path, and where the model seam is not

Walkthrough

Two boxes inside one box, plus three dotted stubs. The nesting is the argument.

  • The outer box is the client VPC. Everything is inside it. The only thing outside is the clinician’s browser, reaching in over TLS. Protected health information never crosses that boundary — which is why the de-identification-versus-network-residency debate that governs an external model call simply does not arise for this query.
  • The inner box is the request path — the only lane anyone waits on, and it has exactly three components: the FastAPI analytics service (which audits, asserts tenancy, and suppresses), an in-process LRU cache, and DuckDB over Parquet cells, zones and history. That is it. No statistics, no traversal, no model.
  • Three dotted stubs, deliberately outside the inner box. Neptune is reached for audit and drill-down and by the nightly build — never a live per-policy traversal. LangSmith, self-hosted in the VPC, takes traces and hashes only. And the model seam — a Llama 3.1 the platform can run in-VPC — is drawn connected to nothing, labelled no model on any path.

The seam is on the diagram because it is unused. It is the point: the capability exists, it is wired to the sibling queries that produce prose, and it was declined here. That is a decision, not an omission.

Off-diagram, two offline lanes feed the DuckDB tables: a one-time historical lane that hydrated about nine years of decisions into the DynamoDB event spine, and a nightly lane that replays the spine into a snapshot, rebuilds the cohort and its borrowed control arm, runs the seeded statistics, and writes the zone tables. No human ever waits on either.

What breaks without it

Put the graph on the request path and the report’s latency couples to graph load, and the answer moves between runs. Move the cache out of process without keeping the per-row assertion and you inherit a shared-cache leak surface. And wire the seam in — even as an optional narrator — and every figure on the dashboard stops being deterministic, on the one surface where a sentence that reads as a judgement must never appear.

05 Likely questions

Headline first, detail only if they ask

The first sentence has to land alone — assume you get interrupted. Say the bold line, stop, and let them pull.

Q1Why isn’t this just a GROUP BY over the rulebook?

Because the count is a day’s work and everything else exists to stop the count from becoming a verdict.

Three failure modes are built into the naive version. Confounding: clinicians override on their sickest patients, so a raw followed-versus-overridden delta measures patient mix, not the rule. False confidence at scale: dozens of policy versions are scored at once, so a handful look damning by chance alone. Verdict misuse: a label like “policy too strict” reads as a machine’s ruling and can directly fire a governance action.

And this one is more dangerous than its decision-indexed sister, because a per-rule signal is exactly the shape that triggers a policy revision or a model retraining. A wrong signal here rewrites the rulebook for everyone. The naive one-query version isn’t simpler in the ways that matter — it is the dangerous version.

Q2A rule has a 17% override rate. Doesn’t that alone prove it’s a bad rule?

No — override rate on its own is a popularity count of dissent; it tells you clinicians disagree, not whether they were right.

A high override rate paired with a positive outcome delta and one paired with a negative delta are different zones, and neither is a verdict. Only the matched outcome comparison distinguishes a rule worth revisiting from a healthy guardrail that clinicians happen to chafe against.

There is a live example in the fixtures: a rule at 17.0% override with a positive point estimate of +0.04 — an easy headline — whose interval runs from −0.03 to +0.11. It crosses zero, so the cell is uncertain_zone and renders diagnostics only. The system had a provocative story available and declined to tell it.

Q3Reusing the sister query’s matched cohort is a hidden coupling. Won’t that rot?

It’s an explicit versioned contract that fails loudly and self-heals, not a hidden one — reuse happens only on exact equality across seven manifest fields, and any mismatch triggers a full rebuild with the same matcher binary.

The seven fields are the covariate schema hash, the snapshot manifest hash, the cohort filter hash, the seed, the caliper, the replacement policy, and the tie-break policy. Under identical inputs the matcher is deterministic, so the borrowed pairs are the same pairs a rebuild would produce — which is the only condition under which borrowing is provably the same as rebuilding.

The alternative is worse, not better. Two independent matchers guarantee divergent cohorts and divergent intervals over time, and they make the “same overrides, two indexings” claim unverifiable. And the coupling is tested, not assumed: the reuse path is exercised against a rebuild under an identical manifest and required to produce identical cells. If they differ, the conclusion is that the manifest is incomplete — some result-affecting input is missing from the seven fields.

Q4Your golden replay is green. How do you know the reconstruction is genuinely as-of and not accidentally reading current state?

The golden replay doesn’t prove that — it proves determinism, and a future-leaking reconstruction leaks deterministically, so replay reproduces the bug as faithfully as it reproduces a correct result.

This is the distinction I’d want a team to be precise about, because conflating the two is how future leakage survives a green suite. Golden replay shuffles the spine order, injects late arrivals, corrections and supersede chains, exports twice at the same as_of, and requires byte-identical output. That is determinism: same inputs, same answer, regardless of load order. It says nothing about whether the cutoff is honoured.

As-of correctness needs tests whose expected result changes only if the cutoff is being honoured. Two do that here. The first is metamorphic: take a fixture, append events measured after the as_of, re-export at the same as_of, and assert the output does not move — any read of post-cutoff state changes the answer and fails. The second is known-good historical spot checks: hand-reconstructed states at chosen dates, asserted field by field, which catches an as-of that is self-consistent but wrong.

Determinism is the floor. Those two are what turn “we intend it to be as-of” into “it is”.

Q5Why is there no language model here — and if you pinned it at temperature zero, wouldn’t it be deterministic?

Temperature zero doesn’t make a model’s output deterministic — it gives you drift detection, not generation determinism — and on a surface that can revise a rulebook, a narrated verdict that drifts while the audit reports “same inputs” is worse than no narration.

Pinning the model, the prompt and the temperature, and hashing inputs and outputs, buys you the ability to notice when the text changed. It does not buy you a guarantee that it won’t.

There is also no natural-language task on this path. The output is a coordinate — an override rate and an outcome delta with an interval — and the moment a model is allowed to narrate one in prose it editorialises a policy into good or bad, which is the exact verdict the neutral codes exist to withhold. So neutrality is code, not a prompt: the six codes are assigned by deterministic classification, there is no prompt to jailbreak because no claim ever passes through a model, and there is no additive layer that can fail quiet.

Same logic kills retrieval-augmented generation here. A governance signal has to be a matched-cohort statistic an auditor can trace back to its own denominators, not a retrieved passage paraphrased into a sentence that merely sounds authoritative. The platform can run a Llama 3.1 inside the VPC and does, for the queries that genuinely produce sentences. This one was the right place to decline it.

Q6With that gate ladder, most cells get suppressed. Is the map even useful?

Often most cells are suppressed, and that is the honest state of the evidence, not a defect — a map that lit up every cell would be the untrustworthy one.

First, a correction to the usual premise: the false-discovery figure doesn’t suppress anything. The q-value is computed at q = 0.10 and returned with every cell; it is not a rung in the gate. The suppression comes from the power floor, the balance gate, the sensitivity battery and intervals that cross zero — all unadjusted for multiplicity, which is precisely why the ranking is labelled exploratory and the q-value is put in front of the reader rather than applied on their behalf.

The value isn’t a full grid of colours. It is the small number of well-powered, sensitivity-stable cells whose signal you can stand behind in front of a governance board. And the map’s own sparsity is a monitored signal in its own right — a sudden collapse in the number of cells clearing the gates points at an upstream data problem, not at policies suddenly becoming uninteresting.

Q7You keep saying “deterministic”. Where does that actually break?

It holds per manifest and per pinned library version — not forever; upgrade the matcher, the outcome library or the bootstrap, or land a correction, and the output legitimately changes with it.

Be precise about the two mechanisms, because they are different. A correction advances the correction epoch, which is a cache-key field, so a stale cell can’t be served past it. A library upgrade does not move the manifest hash — the seven manifest fields hash the matcher’s inputs, not the matcher’s code — so it is traced through the library versions stamped on every result and its audit record, not through the key.

That is an honest weakness and I’d name it as one: the sibling query’s cache key, which pins the matcher binary and the statistical-plan version explicitly, is the stronger form. Here, if two library versions disagree on the same cohort, neither is declared right by fiat — each result is stamped with its libraries and its manifest so the two are distinguishable, and the parity fixture and the golden replay decide whether a version change is a fix or a regression.

Also worth saying: hashes detect drift, they don’t create determinism. Determinism comes from the seeded, structured computation — a seeded bootstrap, a deterministic exporter, canonical tie-breaks in the matcher, manifest-addressed inputs. The hashes are how you audit that it held.

Q8What’s the worst silent failure this design still permits — and what would you change first?

A covariate that matters but was never measured: an unmeasured confounder can move an outcome delta with every gate showing green — balanced, stable, well-powered, interval excluding zero — and nothing in the pipeline will flag it.

That is precisely why every cell stays observational and no zone is ever a causal claim. The limit is stated rather than engineered away, because it cannot be engineered away: matching balances only what was measured. The strongest argument against shipping this query at all is that no observational design can rule that out, so any delta it reports is contestable and could be acted on wrongly. The design’s answer isn’t that it solved that — it can’t — but that it refuses to emit a verdict, gates hard, labels everything observational and exploratory, and keeps a named human owner. Mitigation of a real limitation, not a resolution of it.

What I’d change first: make the unmeasured-confounding limit quantitative instead of only textual. A formal robustness figure per cell — how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain away the observed delta — would let a governance reader weigh a signal’s fragility numerically, rather than reading a blanket “observational” caveat that is easy to discount. It turns the honest limit from a disclaimer into a reported metric.

And on accountability, if governance ignores the caveats and acts on a cell anyway: partly the design’s fault, if it made the caveat easy to ignore. Which is why the mitigations are structural rather than cosmetic — verdict strings forbidden at the source, an observational label on every cell, an exploratory rather than ranked ordering, every read logged. Acting against the caveats becomes a visible, attributable choice by a person, not a default the surface quietly encouraged.

Two more they may reach for. “Does this put clinicians on a scoreboard?” — No; the unit of analysis is the policy version, not the doctor, and this query produces no per-clinician breakdown at all. “What happens when the snapshot goes stale?” — Degradation is tiered and visible: green under 24 hours, yellow with a banner at 24–48, and past 48 hours it blocks and escalates rather than hand over quietly outdated statistics. Isolation and provenance have no graceful tier at all: a tenant-scope mismatch or a manifest-equality violation fails closed immediately, because serving cross-tenant or blended-cohort data is strictly worse than serving nothing.

Where it transfers. Any system that grades a rulebook by how often it is broken and which breaks track better or worse outcomes — underwriting exception rates, site-reliability runbook bypasses, fraud-rule overrides, content-policy appeals. When the finding can change the rule, it must never read as a machine’s verdict; the statistics must be gated, the control group must be exactly the one you claim, and the access must be governed.
