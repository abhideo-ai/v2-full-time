Interview prep · Killer query 6 · AI vs clinician outcomes

AI versus clinician — what actually happened at 90 days?

A chronic-care platform covering 80+ chronic conditions. Artificial-intelligence (AI) decision support suggests a next action; sometimes the clinician says no and does something else. This is the system that grades those disagreements — propensity-matched, deterministically gated, governed so it cannot become a doctor scorecard, and held dark until the audit trail that makes it honest actually existed. It is live in production. No language model touches a single number.

Open with these two sentences

"We had AI decision support recommending a next action for chronic-care patients, and clinicians overruled it sometimes. The product wanted to know whether those overrules led to better or worse outcomes ninety days later — which is the most dangerous GROUP BY in the product, because the careless version of that query is a performance ranking of your doctors on their hardest cases."

Two things to get right in the first minute

Status. The system shipped and runs in production. The design review's remaining "approval pending" is an internal design-review sign-off, not deployment status — the upstream contract was signed and the query is live. Say live, never "designed" or "proposed".

Numbers. Every figure on this page is a design threshold or a scale figure — a bar the system was built to clear. There are no measured outcomes: latency achieved, adoption, error reduction, accuracy, cost saved. If asked, the honest line is that the observed results are not his to publish, and he left before capturing them. Never estimate one out loud.

01 What is it?

The most dangerous GROUP BY in the product

The situation, in plain language. A clinician is managing a patient with one or more chronic conditions. The platform's AI proposes a next action — a change of programme, a medication adjustment, an escalation — and shows it with a confidence bucket attached and a panel of comparable past cases beside it. Most of the time the clinician takes the suggestion. Sometimes they don't: they look at this patient, decide the model has misread the case, and choose something else. Ninety days later that patient has an outcome.

Two people want the same number from that. A model-quality reviewer or clinical leader asks: is our AI's high-confidence advice actually worth following, or are the clinicians who overrule it right to? A regulator or a clinical-governance committee asks a harder version: you have deployed an algorithm that influences patient care — show me the evidence about what happens when humans disagree with it.

Both are asking for a comparison of two arms. The naive way to produce it is one line of structured query language (SQL): group the decisions by clinician, split by whether the AI was accepted or rejected, and compare 90-day outcome rates. That query is wrong before it is even computed.

Why it is genuinely hard

  • Endogenous selection — the comparison is confounded by design. Clinicians do not reject AI suggestions at random. They reject on their most unusual, highest-stakes, hardest-to-read cases. So the reject arm and the accept arm differ precisely where the decision was hardest, and a raw delta measures the case mix, not the judgment. A naive implementation reports that overruling the AI correlates with worse outcomes, and what it has actually discovered is that hard cases go worse.
  • Scorecard misuse — the artefact is dangerous even when the statistics are perfect. The instant a per-clinician AI-agreement table exists, it reads as a league table. A statistically immaculate number still ends careers if it is presented as a ranking rather than an observation. The naive implementation ships a sortable column and has built a doctor ranking on the most sensitive cases in the product.
  • The data to define the comparison did not exist. This is what made this query different from every other cohort analysis in the programme. There was no trustworthy record of what the AI suggested, whether the human accepted or rejected it, and what they chose instead. The naive implementation infers the arm by comparing payloads — call it a rejection whenever the chosen option differs from the suggested one. That heuristic misfires in five distinct ways: partial edits to the payload, schema drift between versions, option up-conversion to a newer canonical form, multiple suggestions attached to one decision, and rejections that were never logged at all. Each one makes an accept look like a reject or a reject look like an accept, and no downstream statistic can repair a wrong label.

There are three more failure modes a naive build walks into behind those. It reads the current graph for a historical decision, so features and even the arm inherit edits made after the fact — hindsight leaking into the comparison. It recomputes the confidence bucket with today's model, silently re-bucketing a suggestion the clinician saw under an older one. And it pools everything into one headline delta, which hides the confidence interaction that is the only interesting part and invites a Simpson's-paradox reversal, where the pooled number points the opposite way to every bucket inside it.

The one-line framing

The honest version of this query is not "who was right?" It is: within a single confidence bucket, is the 90-day outcome rate different between rejected-suggestion decisions and their propensity-matched accepted-suggestion twins? — computed only when the cohort survives balance, sensitivity, concentration, power and calibration gates, and labelled observational, never causal.

02 How did we solve it?

A two-party contract, snapshot-only analytics, and an application programming interface (API) built to refuse

Step one: get the inputs that make the comparison honest

Rather than infer the arm, the design specified a formal producer/consumer event contract and handed it to the upstream team that owns the next-decision user interface. Five deliverables, drawn as an ownership boundary — one side emits, the other stayed dark until all five were signed and verified:

  1. AI suggestion emitted — carries the canonical suggested option, the option-schema version, the raw confidence and the emitted confidence bucket, the model version, the four-field event time, and the source sequence.
  2. Human accepted — references the suggestion, the decision, the actor, and the source sequence.
  3. Human rejected — the same, and additionally carries the canonical human-chosen option, so you know not just that the AI was overruled but what was chosen instead.
  4. Schema-parity contract — the suggested option and the decision's chosen option must normalise to the same canonical representation across schema versions, which is what makes the equality validation meaningful at all.
  5. Correction and replay contract — the cohort, matched and metric tables can be targeted for rebuild when an audit event is later corrected.

A prerequisite gate sat above the entire pipeline and blocked every step below it: no snapshot, no cohort, no match, no metric was built for a tenant whose upstream audit trail was absent or unverified. The hardened design existed, fully specified and armed, behind that gate. When the events were verified to attach at decision time, the gate opened and the comparison activated.

Step two: the pipeline, and what the data does as it moves

Nothing on the request path ever touches the live graph. The whole thing is snapshot-only, precomputed overnight, and read cheaply.

  1. Event spine (Amazon DynamoDB, append-only). The source of truth: decisions, the three audit events, actor edges, policy versions, outcomes. Nothing is overwritten — a correction is a new event that supersedes, never an in-place edit.
  2. Decision graph (Amazon Neptune). Projected off the spine. It feeds the nightly snapshot and is reserved for governed audit and drill-in. It is never queried per row at analytics time and never read live for a historical decision.
  3. Snapshot exporter. Freezes a point-in-time snapshot into Parquet — a columnar file format — with a manifest hash, the maximum source event sequence, and an allowed-lateness bound. Partitioned by the tenant triple and by snapshot hash together, so isolation and reproducibility come from the same physical layout.
  4. Cohort builder (DuckDB, an embedded analytical engine). Tenant-scoped columnar joins, no per-decision graph traversal. The arm is read from the immutable accept-or-reject audit event. Payload equality is still computed — but only as a logged validation flag whose disagreements are monitored, never as the label. Where several suggestions attach to one decision, pairing is ordered deterministically by suggestion event sequence, and the chosen sequence is persisted on the row.
  5. As-of reconstruction. Every pre-treatment covariate, the actor, the outcome edge and the AI's confidence are replayed from the spine at the decision's own event sequence. Each field persists its feature_as_of_ts and source_event_seq, so the reconstruction is auditable on replay rather than asserted. The confidence bucket used is the one that was emitted at suggestion time — never recomputed.
  6. Outcome label (shared 90-day library). Returns a status — mature, not-mature, missing or censored — with an attribution decision id, a rule version, a distance in days, and a censoring reason. Survival is handled by Kaplan–Meier, not a naive 0/1 rate. The window is anchored to the decision's event time plus 90 days, because the decision is the intervention, and a cell only counts a decision once decision time + 90 days ≤ snapshot as-of.
  7. Confidence-calibration gate. A hard cross-query dependency: the high-confidence cell refuses to render unless a sibling calibration query holds a passing artifact for the same model version and the same snapshot.
  8. Propensity matcher (shared, seeded binary). Caliper matching on pre-treatment covariates plus the AI confidence bucket, under a versioned match_manifest_id — a content hash over the covariate schema, snapshot manifest, caliper, replacement policy, tie-break policy, seed and cohort filter. The sibling override-analytics query's matched pairs are reused verbatim only when every manifest field agrees; otherwise the pairs are rebuilt with the same binary.
  9. Aggregate. Per confidence bucket only — there is no pooled headline number at all. Balance gate, then the five-test sensitivity battery, then a seeded cluster-aware bootstrap for the interval: 2000 resamples, seeded from the snapshot manifest hash, resampled by enrollment cluster rather than by row.
  10. Serve (FastAPI). Tenant filter pushed into the scan before load, canonical cache key, RBAC, an audit entry written before anything is read, pseudonymous clinician ids, alphabetic and never-rankable ordering, no "who was right" framing anywhere in the contract.
  11. Drift and self-reinforcement monitor. Accept rate, reject rate, case mix, confidence distribution, calibration status and the deltas themselves — all segmented before versus after the dashboard was exposed.
  12. Evaluation and golden regression. Per-bucket power floor, audit-event correction fixtures, byte-for-byte reproducibility across rebuilds.

A correction-event listener sits alongside: every derived artefact records the range of source event sequences it depends on, a dependency index maps sequences to the cohort, matched and metric slices they touch, and a correction — including an audit-event correction — invalidates and rebuilds only the overlapping slices. Target: 95th-percentile latency (P95) under five minutes from correction to fresh metric.

Step three: the serve path, in order

The ordering here is the design. Each step exists because doing it later would open a hole.

  1. Audit first. Any request touching a by-clinician split writes viewer, target, purpose and time before the response is served, and returns the resulting access_log_id in the response body. There is no window in which data is served without a record of who saw whose data.
  2. Canonical cache key. A sorted encoding of every result-affecting input: the tenant triple (account, workspace, control unit), the query namespace, the split and filters, the confidence bucket and any by-clinician target, the metric schema hash, the matcher, bootstrap and outcome-library versions, the snapshot manifest hash, the maximum source event sequence, and the correction epoch.
  3. Role branch. Leadership scans the metric tables; a clinician viewing themselves scans only their own clinician id; anyone else is denied the by-clinician split and served the confidence-bucket view. In every branch the tenant-partition filter is applied at the scan, so out-of-scope rows are never materialised.
  4. The statistical gate ladder (below).
  5. Tenant assertion on every returned cell. A row outside the caller's exact triple fails the request closed and pages — it is an incident, not a metric.
  6. Pseudonymise unless leadership drill-in, attach the observational tag, return.

The gate ladder — five gates in series, every exit stated

GateBarWhat happens on failure
Calibration (high-confidence cell only)Passing sibling artifact for the same model version + snapshotSuppress the high-confidence delta, emit a calibration diagnostic
BalanceSMD ≤ 0.1 per bucketSuppress the causal-language delta — diagnostics only, never a raw rate
Sensitivity batteryAll five tests pass; Rosenbaum robust to γ ≥ 1.5Suppress — diagnostics only
ConcentrationTop clinician ≤ 0.30 of the reject cohortTag concentrated and exclude from generalized conclusions — the one gate that tags rather than exits
Power floor≥ 30 treated and ≥ 30 control, both maturedEmit insufficient-data status with the full mature / missing / censored denominator breakdown

Only a cell that clears all of them emits a delta with its confidence interval, its denominators, a pseudonymous clinician id and an observational tag. The guarantee is enforced in the type system, not by convention: the output contract's validator (Pydantic v2) raises an exception if an outcome delta is attached to a cell whose gates failed. Within the bounds the schema enforces, a gate-failing number is not discouraged — it is unrepresentable.

What the nine years of history actually did

A private production system had been recording these accept-and-reject decisions for roughly nine years, and that corpus was replayed into the event spine by a first-class, proof-carrying hydration pipeline — not a throwaway backfill. Legacy records were normalised and up-converted with a stamped payload-schema version; anything that could not be mapped to a known schema or to a tenant was quarantined with a reason, counted and reported, never defaulted. Event identifiers are content-addressed and written conditionally, so hydrating twice is a no-op that yields a byte-identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. History replays through the same projection code that serves the live stream. Proof gates — zero-event-loss reconciliation, the hydrate-twice hash equality, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice — must all pass before anything downstream is built, and then a fenced cutover attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate.

Each event carries four timestamps: event_time and observed_at come from the source record and are real-world truth; received_at and written_at are pinned to the load fence. Only the real-world pair ever orders the timeline, and a guard asserts event_time < received_at on every row. Collapse those four into one and nine years of decisions all appear to have happened on load day, and every as-of reconstruction behind the comparison is void.

The honest limit — say this before they find it

Nine years of history does not mean nine years of AI-versus-clinician comparisons. The comparison depends on the three audit events, and those only began flowing at contract activation — so the usable cohort starts there, not nine years back. What the deep history buys is mature outcomes and faithful as-of reconstruction for the decisions it can cover. It cannot invent events that were never recorded.

03 Why did we do what we did?

Every decision, and the alternative it beat

This is the section to know cold. Each card is one decision, the alternative that was rejected, and the reason — in his own words where the source states it.

Decision 01

Refuse to serve any delta until the audit trail existed

Alternative rejected
Ship a best-effort version now: infer the arm by comparing the chosen option against the suggested option, and iterate once real audit events arrive.
Reason
The inference is a heuristic, not a fact, and it misfires five ways. A wrong arm label is unrecoverable downstream — no gate, no sensitivity test and no amount of matching can repair a cohort whose membership is wrong.
"Because a comparison is only as honest as its inputs, and inferring 'rejected' from an option mismatch misfires in five distinct ways — partial edits, schema drift, option up-conversion, multiple suggestions, unlogged rejects. A best-effort arm would have produced deltas measuring a logging gap rather than a difference in outcomes, and no amount of downstream statistics can repair a wrong label."his own reasoning, verbatim
Decision 02

Turn the blocker into a deliverable the other team could sign

Alternative rejected
File the dependency as a blocker on the upstream team and wait.
Reason
A vague "someday" dependency never closes. A named ownership boundary with five concrete deliverables does — and it made this design carry its own half of the fix rather than assign blame.
"No — the alternative was fully in this query's control: it could have shipped a plausible delta off inferred arms, and it chose not to. Rather than assign blame, the design produced a concrete deliverable for the upstream team — three audit events, a schema-parity proof, and a correction/replay contract — so the dependency was defined and actionable rather than a complaint."on whether block-on-prereq was just blaming a slow team
Decision 03

Assign the arm from the immutable audit event; keep payload equality only as a flag

Alternative rejected
Payload equality as the label — chosen option ≠ suggested option means rejection.
Reason
The audit event was written at decision time and is never mutated, so the label cannot drift with later edits: the arm is a fact rather than an inference. Equality is still computed, because a disagreement between the flag and the event is exactly the early-warning signal that an upstream schema has drifted — it surfaces as a rising validation-mismatch metric instead of as silently corrupted deltas.
Decision 04

Snapshot-only analytics; never query the live graph at request time

Alternative rejected
Traverse the Neptune decision graph per decision at analytics time.
Reason
Three problems at once. A current read of a historical decision returns today's edges and properties — rationale text, confidence, policy alignment, evidence edges and the suggestion attachment can all be edited or re-linked afterwards — so hindsight leaks into both the features and the arm. Per-decision graph queries across a large cohort are an availability and cost liability. And a frozen snapshot with a manifest hash is what makes the same as-of reproduce the same cells on every rebuild. The cost is staleness bounded by the snapshot cadence, surfaced explicitly rather than hidden.
"Live per-row graph traversal at analytics time would be slow, would couple the dashboard's availability to Neptune's, and — most importantly — would read current graph state for a historical decision, leaking edits made after the fact."on why snapshot-only
Decision 05

Trust the confidence bucket as emitted; never recompute it

Alternative rejected
Recompute the bucket at analysis time with the current model and thresholds.
Reason
Recomputing applies a newer model to a historical suggestion and silently re-buckets it, leaking model evolution into a historical cohort. The emitted bucket is the confidence the clinician actually saw — the only bucket that makes the comparison meaningful.
Decision 06

Anchor the 90-day window on the decision, not the suggestion; exclude long lags from the headline

Alternative rejected
Date the outcome from when the suggestion was emitted, and keep every decision.
Reason
The decision is the intervention whose consequence is being measured, so suggestion-time anchoring misdates outcomes wherever there was a lag. And a large gap means the decision was probably not a response to that suggestion at all: beyond 72 hours a long-lag flag is set and the row is excluded from the headline and surfaced only as a sensitivity slice, with the lag persisted so the exclusion is transparent and revisable.
Decision 07

Propensity matching — not an instrument, not difference-in-differences, not a randomised trial

Alternative rejected
Instrumental variables; difference-in-differences; a randomised comparison that withholds the suggestion from a random subset.
Reason
Each fails on its own terms, and the randomised one fails on ethics.
"There is no clean instrument for 'the clinician rejected the suggestion' — nothing plausibly shifts rejection without also touching the outcome — so instrumental variables are out. Difference-in-differences needs a parallel-trends assumption that reject and accept cohorts do not credibly satisfy on these cases. Given the data, caliper matching on pre-treatment covariates plus a sensitivity battery is the defensible observational choice, and its limits are stated rather than assumed away."on method choice
"A randomised design — withholding the suggestion for a random subset — is the gold standard for causal identification and would dissolve the endogenous-selection problem. It was not an option here because you cannot ethically or operationally randomise whether a clinician sees a decision-support recommendation on real patients."on why not a trial
Decision 08

Match on pre-treatment covariates plus AI confidence only — nothing endogenous

Alternative rejected
Throw every available variable into the match, on the theory that more balance is better.
Reason
More variables is explicitly the wrong instinct. Matching on something that lies on the causal path between the decision and the outcome, or is itself a consequence of the decision, induces collider or post-treatment bias — it can manufacture a delta that isn't there or erase one that is.
"Balance is only protective when it is balance on pre-treatment confounders."on covariate selection
Decision 09

Per confidence bucket only — refuse to compute a pooled headline

Alternative rejected
One pooled top-line number, with per-bucket detail available underneath.
Reason
Pooling hides the structure that matters and invites a reversal.
"Because pooling across confidence buckets hides the most important structure and invites a Simpson's-paradox reversal. Low-confidence rejects followed by bad outcomes are the expected default and say little about judgment; high-confidence rejects are the interesting case. A single pooled delta blends those into a number that means nothing actionable, so the design stratifies by the emitted confidence bucket and refuses to compute a pooled headline at all."on stratification
Decision 10

Suppress a failed cell — never downgrade it to a caveated raw rate

Alternative rejected
Return the raw rate with a warning tag attached, and let the reader judge.
Reason
This is the single most quotable piece of reasoning on the page, because it is about human behaviour rather than statistics.
"Because a tagged raw rate still renders as a number, and a number gets screenshotted, pasted into a slide, and read without its tag. Suppression removes the artifact that can be misquoted and replaces it with a diagnostic that explains the gate failure, so there is nothing to lift out of context. The design treats 'return a caveated number' as a social control that fails under normal human behaviour, and suppression as the structural control that holds."on suppress versus downgrade
Decision 11

Tune the whole system around false emit, not false suppress

Alternative rejected
Optimise for coverage — show as many cells as defensibly possible.
Reason
The costs are asymmetric. A withheld borderline signal is cheap; an unsupportable one can be read as a clinician verdict. So evaluation is organised around catching failures to suppress, on seeded synthetic cohorts with a planted imbalance, a planted confounder or a single-clinician-dominated reject set, where the correct gate decision is known by construction. The suppression path is tested as a first-class behaviour with its own ground truth — grading only emitted deltas would miss the failure mode the system cares about most.
Decision 12

Depend on a sibling calibration query rather than computing calibration inline

Alternative rejected
Compute calibration inside this service.
Reason
"High confidence" needs one authoritative definition across the programme; an inline computation creates a second one that can disagree. The cost is a hard cross-query dependency that can veto a cell; the benefit is that a bucket is never called high-confidence in one place and untrustworthy in another.
Decision 13

Re-check calibration at read time, even on a cache hit

Alternative rejected
Check calibration once, during the nightly build, and bake the result into the cached cell.
Reason
Calibration status is a separate artifact with its own lifecycle — it can flip to not-passing after the cell was cached. Build-time-only checking leaves a window in which a model whose calibration drifted keeps serving a confident-looking delta until the next rebuild. The cache accelerates the statistics; it does not get to answer the governance question.
Decision 14

Event-driven freshness in the cache key — not a time-to-live (TTL)

Alternative rejected
A TTL on cached cells.
Reason
A correction can land at any point inside a TTL window, and until the entry expires the cache keeps serving a value the spine has already contradicted. The key carries the maximum source event sequence and a correction epoch; a correction bumps them, so the old entry is no longer addressable. TTL is at best a backstop. The key also pins the matcher, bootstrap and outcome-library versions, because a library bump changes the meaning of a cell even when the data is identical — a library change should be a cache miss.
Decision 15

Bootstrap by enrollment cluster, not by row

Alternative rejected
Row-level resampling.
Reason
One patient enrollment contributes many decisions and those decisions are not independent. Treating each as an independent draw understates the variance and produces intervals that are too narrow — row-level bootstrap here would systematically make weak evidence look strong. Resampling whole clusters preserves the within-patient correlation. The seed is derived from the snapshot manifest hash and the resample count is fixed at 2000, so the interval is a function of the data and the pinned configuration alone.
Decision 16

Isolation as a property of the pipeline — three layers, fail closed

Alternative rejected
A single account_id predicate on each hand-written query.
Reason
Per-query discipline fails silently, and once is enough: a forgotten predicate puts cross-tenant data into a snapshot and from there into every downstream artefact. Instead: a Parquet partition on the three-field tenant scope (account, workspace, control unit), a scan-time predicate so out-of-scope partitions are never read, and a runtime assertion that every returned row carries the caller's exact scope. All three would have to fail simultaneously. It is the difference between "we didn't return it" and "we never read it."
Decision 17

Make the scorecard reading hard and visible — and refuse to claim it is impossible

Alternative rejected
A policy statement that the dashboard must not be used for performance management.
Reason
The danger is structural, so the mitigation cannot live in the query alone or in a policy: it lives in pseudonymous clinician ids by default, leadership-or-self RBAC on the by-clinician split, an audit entry written before serving, alphabetic and never-rankable ordering — a sortable column is a league table by construction — per-bucket-only defaults, and a mandatory observational label. Ordering is treated as a governance control, not a user-interface preference.
"No — 'never' would be an overclaim, and the honest statement is narrower: the controls make the scorecard reading hard and visible rather than impossible… A determined viewer with leadership access and intent could still attempt to read it as a ranking; what the design guarantees is that doing so is constrained, logged, and against the grain — not that it is unachievable."on whether the scorecard reading can be ruled out
Decision 18

Keep the language model out entirely — and no retrieval layer either

Alternative rejected
Use the platform's self-hosted model seam to narrate or explain the cells, or add a retrieval-augmented-generation surface over the supporting documents.
Reason
Three things follow from keeping it shut. There is no prompt to jailbreak — the neutrality rules, suppression logic and access controls are code and configuration, so they cannot drift with a model update. There is nothing to hallucinate — every delta is computed and version-pinned, and no generated sentence carries a claim. And the retrieval absence is a judgment, not a gap: the deliverable is a number the code has already computed and gated, so there is nothing further to retrieve that would make it more true.
"…a document-fetching narrator could only tempt the reader back toward the who-was-right verdict the whole design refuses to produce."on the model boundary

The platform does run a self-hosted Llama 3.1 seam inside the VPC for sibling surfaces that genuinely narrate. The discipline is choosing not to open a door that is one import away.

Decision 19

Monitor the feedback loop this dashboard creates, rather than deny it

Alternative rejected
Treat the metric as an independent observation of clinician behaviour.
Reason
Publishing "high-confidence rejects did worse" can nudge clinicians to accept more, which changes both the accept/reject mix and the model's future training distribution. The design does not pretend otherwise — the drift monitor segments every signal before versus after dashboard exposure, so the shift is measured rather than mistaken for a real trend. The honest position is that the metric is a moving target under its own influence.

The design review, and what it changed

Before anything shipped, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens. It returned the hardest verdict in the set: block-on-prereq — not "ship with changes", not "redesign", but "you do not yet have the data to answer this honestly, so do not build it." The blocking finding was that the AI-suggestion audit trail did not exist: no producer, no schema, no implementation. Alongside it came 13 local findings across correctness, statistics and governance.

All thirteen were absorbed into a hardened v2 and armed — specified and waiting, activating the moment the contract landed. What changed as a result:

Finding areaWhat the design became
Arm assignmentMoved off payload equality onto the immutable accept/reject audit event; equality demoted to a logged validation flag
DeterminismDeterministic pairing by suggestion event sequence, with the chosen sequence persisted on the row
LeakageAs-of reconstruction of every covariate, the actor and the outcome edge, each carrying its own source sequence and as-of timestamp
Endogenous selectionThe five-test sensitivity battery, downstream of the match
Confidence semanticsThe hard cross-query calibration check, keyed to model version and snapshot
GovernanceActor-level RBAC with pseudonymity by default and audit-before-serve
PresentationPer-bucket-only aggregation, with no pooled headline computed at all

The prerequisite has since been met: the upstream contract shipped and was verified, and the query went live behind its gates. Where sibling reviews minted new shared standards, this one confirmed and extended the programme's governance, statistics and cache-key conventions without adding a new cross-query finding. Final design-review sign-off is still open — that is an internal approval waiting on the final review and on the sibling calibration artifacts being in range, not a deployment status.

04 Architecture

Four diagrams, and how to redraw each one

Fig 1 · The event-contract handoff

Walkthrough

What flows where. Two boxes. On the left, the upstream product team that owns the next-decision screen; on the right, this analytics service. Five nodes on the left — three audit events plus two contracts — and every one of them points at a single node on the right: the prerequisite gate. One dotted arrow leaves the gate for the hardened v2 design. That dotted line is the whole story: the design was finished, reviewed and armed, and it was not running.

What each deliverable does. Suggestion emitted establishes what the AI said and, critically, the confidence bucket as emitted — without it there is nothing to stratify on. Human accepted and human rejected are the arm labels themselves, written at decision time; the rejected event additionally carries the human-chosen option, which is the difference between knowing the AI was overruled and knowing what happened instead. Schema parity guarantees the suggested option and the chosen option normalise to the same canonical form across schema versions — without it the equality validation flag compares apples to oranges and its mismatch signal is noise. Correction and replay is what lets a later-corrected audit event invalidate exactly the cohort, matched and metric slices that depend on it.

What breaks without the gate. The comparison launches on a half-built feed. Rejections that were never emitted read as acceptances, and the delta measures a gap in the logs rather than a difference in outcomes — a plausible-looking, entirely wrong number that no downstream gate can catch, because the cohort it balances and tests is already wrong.

Fig 3 · The full pipeline, gated at the top

Walkthrough

What flows where. A single vertical chain of ten stages with one gate sitting above all of them, and one listener feeding in sideways. Data enters as a frozen snapshot and leaves as precomputed metric cells that the request path only has to read.

Why the order is what it is. The snapshot must be frozen before the cohort is built, or two rebuilds at the same as-of could disagree. The arm must be assigned before covariates are reconstructed, and both before any outcome is looked at — that is the ordering that keeps hindsight out. The outcome label comes before the matcher because matching pairs decisions that will actually contribute a matured outcome. The calibration gate sits before aggregation so a bucket whose "high confidence" is not earned never becomes a headline. Aggregation is where the balance gate and the five-test battery run, and only then does anything reach the serve stage. The drift monitor and the golden regression sit at the end because they watch the whole chain.

The sideways arrow. The correction listener dots into exactly four stages — cohort, matcher, aggregate, serve — the four stages whose outputs a correction can move. A correction rebuilds only the overlapping slices, targeted through the dependency index, at a P95 target of five minutes. A full nightly-style rebuild could never hit that target.

What breaks without each piece. Without the top gate, deltas are computed off a half-built feed. Without the snapshot freeze, reproducibility is gone and a cached cell cannot be tied to the exact manifest it was computed from. Without the correction listener, a corrected audit event leaves stale cells addressable and the cache keeps serving a value the spine has contradicted. Without the drift monitor, the dashboard quietly changes the population it measures and nobody sees it.

Fig 6 · The nightly arm-assignment exchange

Walkthrough

What flows where. Four participants, one nightly conversation, tenant pinned and anchored at a fixed as-of. The builder scans the snapshot for decisions that have a suggestion attached and gets back candidates. It then asks the spine to reconstruct the emit / accept / reject events as of each decision, and the spine returns the immutable accept-or-reject event together with the suggestion-to-decision lag. The builder assigns the arm from that event — and computes payload equality only as a flag. Then, and only then, it asks the spine for the pre-treatment covariates as of the same instant, each returned with its own source event sequence. The 90-day labeller returns a status with a rule version and a censoring reason. Finally the cohort row is written with arm, lag and as-of features.

The load-bearing detail is the ordering. Arm from the event first, covariates as of the same instant second, outcome last. Reverse any two and the comparison is contaminated: covariates read after the outcome is known would balance on the future, and an arm derived after the fact would be an inference rather than a fact.

Why the lag is carried. It is what powers the 72-hour long-lag exclusion. A decision made three days after the suggestion is a different decision, and mixing it into the headline blurs the exact contrast being measured.

What breaks without it. If arm assignment trusted payload equality, this exchange would collapse the five failure modes into a single silent mislabel — and no downstream gate could recover the truth, because the cohort being balanced and tested would already be wrong.

Fig 8 · The statistical gate ladder

Walkthrough

What flows where. A candidate cell enters from the query path and walks down a spine of five diamonds. Four of the five diamonds route their "no" branch to a single exit node where the tenant assertion runs before anything returns; the concentration gate is the exception and rejoins the flow at the power check. There is no path off this diagram that produces a bare number.

What each gate defends. Calibration is checked first because it is a cross-query veto: if the sibling calibration query does not hold a passing artifact for this model version and snapshot, "high confidence" is a label the model has not earned, and the cell fails toward silence rather than toward a number. Balance (SMD ≤ 0.1) asks whether the matched arms are close enough for the delta to mean anything causal-adjacent. Sensitivity is the five-test battery — the endogenous-selection defence. Concentration asks whether one clinician is more than 30% of the reject cohort. Power requires 30 matured cases in each arm.

The one gate that behaves differently. Concentration tags and continues — note the arrow from the tag node back into the power check rather than out to the exit. A concentrated cohort is not a broken cell; it is a real observation about one clinician's practice pattern that must not be generalised. Everything else suppresses.

Why the exits point one way. Because a false emit costs far more here than a false suppress. A suppressed cell returns the gate that failed and its supporting numbers, so the reader learns something true about the cohort. An insufficient-data cell returns the full mature / missing / censored denominator breakdown, which tells the reader why the cell is quiet — small cohort, immature outcomes, or censoring.

What breaks without each rung. Without Rosenbaum bounds inside the battery, an unmeasured confounder passes unremarked. Without leave-one-clinician-out, one prolific clinician's case mix becomes a population claim. Without the power floor, a handful of cases renders as a confident number. Without the calibration veto, the most-quoted cell on the dashboard rests on a confidence label nobody has checked. No single test covers them all — that is why it is a ladder.

05 Likely questions

Headline first, detail only if they ask for it

The first sentence has to survive being interrupted. Say it, stop, and let them pull the rest out of you.

How do you actually know a clinician rejected the AI, rather than just doing something different? Because the arm comes from an immutable accept-or-reject audit event written at decision time — not from comparing what the AI suggested against what was chosen.

Inferring the arm from a payload diff misfires five ways: partial edits, schema drift, option up-conversion across versions, multiple suggestions on one decision, and rejections that were never logged. Each one turns an accept into a reject or the reverse, and a wrong arm label is unrecoverable — every gate downstream would be balancing and testing a cohort whose membership is already wrong.

Payload equality is still computed, but only as a logged validation flag. When the flag and the event disagree, that is an early-warning signal that an upstream schema has drifted — it surfaces as a rising mismatch metric rather than as silently corrupted deltas.

You are comparing rejects against accepts. Isn't that hopelessly confounded? It is confounded by design, which is exactly why the delta is matched, gated five ways, and labelled observational rather than causal.

Clinicians reject on their hardest cases, so the arms differ by case mix before anything is measured. Each rejected-suggestion decision is matched to the most similar accepted-suggestion decision on pre-treatment covariates plus the AI's own confidence — never on anything endogenous. Then the delta has to survive a balance gate at a standardised mean difference of 0.1 and a five-test sensitivity battery before it renders.

Matching only balances what was measured. The clinician's read of a case can encode signal no recorded covariate captures, so unmeasured confounding is never ruled out — the sensitivity battery bounds how strong such a factor would have to be to overturn the result, it does not prove one is absent. Every emitted cell carries the label "matched-cohort observational; not causal; not a clinician performance score."

Why didn't you ship a version 1 off inferred arms and improve it later? Because a wrong arm label is not something a later version fixes — it produces confident deltas that measure a logging gap, and the design chose to stay dark and formalise the missing inputs instead.

The design review returned block-on-prereq: "you do not yet have the data to answer this honestly, so do not build it." Rather than treat that as a complaint about a slow upstream team, the design produced a concrete deliverable for them — three audit events, a schema-parity proof, and a correction/replay contract — and held a prerequisite gate above the entire pipeline. Thirteen local review findings were absorbed into a hardened v2 that was armed and waiting.

The contract shipped, the events were verified to attach at decision time, and the gate opened. That verification step is not a formality: without it the comparison could have launched on a half-built feed where unemitted rejections read as acceptances.

How do you stop this becoming a performance ranking of doctors? You cannot make it impossible, so the controls make it hard, logged and against the grain — and I would not claim more than that.

Pseudonymous clinician ids by default, with re-identification only on an explicit leadership drill-in that is separately audit-logged. RBAC gating on whether the by-clinician split is served at all — leadership with a stated purpose, or a clinician viewing themselves. An audit entry written before the response is served, with the access-log id returned in the body. Alphabetic, never-rankable ordering, because a sortable column is a league table by construction. Per-bucket-only defaults. A mandatory observational tag. No "who was right" label anywhere in the data layer.

A determined viewer with leadership access and intent could still try to read it as a ranking. What the design guarantees is that doing so is constrained, logged, and against the grain.

Why is there no language model anywhere in this? You built the rest of the platform with them. This is a deliberate case of knowing when not to use one — the comparison makes zero model calls, and the seam that a sibling query uses is one import away and stays shut.

Arm assignment, matching, the gates, suppression, ordering and access control are all deterministic, seeded, replayable code. That buys two properties: there is no prompt to jailbreak, because the neutrality rules and suppression logic are code and configuration that cannot drift with a model update; and there is nothing to hallucinate, because every delta is computed and version-pinned and no generated sentence carries a claim.

There is no retrieval-augmented-generation surface either, and that absence is a judgment rather than a gap. The deliverable is a matched, per-bucket delta the code has already computed and gated — nothing further to retrieve would make it more true, and a document-fetching narrator could only tempt the reader back toward the who-was-right verdict the design exists to refuse.

Two rebuilds at the same as-of — could they ever produce different numbers? They must not, and a golden replay test forbids it: the same spine replayed in shuffled order, with corrections and supersede chains injected, has to export a byte-identical hash.

The classic source of disagreement is nondeterministic scan order feeding into arm pairing or matching. That is closed by ordering suggestions on their event sequence and tie-breaking on canonical ids, with the chosen sequence persisted on the row. Downstream, the matcher seed, the bootstrap seed (derived from the snapshot manifest hash, 2000 resamples) and deterministic tie handling make every derived cell reproducible.

If two rebuilds disagreed, that is a determinism defect the test is designed to fail on — not an accepted tolerance.

You hydrated nine years of history. Does that mean nine years of comparisons? No — and that is the honest limit. The usable cohort starts at contract activation, because the audit events only began flowing then.

What the deep history genuinely buys is two things. Mature outcomes: the outcome is measured at 90 days, so a decision only contributes once it is at least 90 days old relative to the snapshot, and a long history means most decisions have had time to mature and the cells can clear their power floor rather than suppressing. And faithful as-of reconstruction: every covariate, edge and fact is recoverable from immutable events at the decision's own sequence, so the matcher balances on what was truly known at decision time.

That reconstruction rests on the four-field event-time model. Two stamps are real-world truth from the source record and two are pinned to the load fence, and only the real-world pair ever orders the timeline. Collapse them into one — the mistake a throwaway backfill makes — and nine years of decisions all inherit the load date.

What is the biggest risk you could not engineer away? Unmeasured confounding — matching cannot balance what was never measured, and no test proves a hidden factor is absent.

The clinician's decision to reject encodes a read of the case that no recorded covariate captures. The sensitivity battery bounds how strong such a factor would have to be to overturn a delta — Rosenbaum bounds robust to a hidden-bias factor of at least 1.5, plus an E-value on the risk-ratio scale — but bounding is not eliminating. So every emitted cell stays observational and says so on the artefact itself.

There is a second one worth volunteering: the human arm is not what the word "human" suggests. The clinician decides with the AI's suggestion and a panel of ranked comparable past cases side by side, so this weighs a machine against a human reading evidence, not against unaided judgment. And that panel is itself gated — where a case has no comparable history the panel declares insufficient precedent and the clinician decides on the suggestion alone. The audit events carry no field recording which condition applied, so the cohort cannot split on it. That caveat belongs beside endogenous selection, not behind it.
