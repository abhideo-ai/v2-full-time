Precedent search

Leakage-safe, multi-tenant precedent retrieval over a chronic-care decision graph. Built at VoltusWave for Amura — a platform covering 80+ chronic conditions with roughly ten thousand patients and nine years of history. Sole owner, design through production. Live in production.

full case study·summary·all case studies

Read this before you quote a number

Every figure on this page is either a design or scale fact (80+ conditions, nine years of history, seven required fields, four review rounds) or a pre-registered acceptance target the system was designed and reviewed against (under 200 ms warm-path P99, normalised discounted cumulative gain at 10 of at least 0.75). There are no measured production outcomes here. You left before the observed results were captured, and they are not yours to publish. If asked "what did it actually hit?", the answer is: "Those are the floors we designed and reviewed against; I left before the post-launch numbers were mine to quote, so I won't invent one." That answer is stronger than a guess, and an interviewer who is worth working for will read it as discipline.

01What is it?

The situation, in plain language

A doctor is on a call with a patient. Fasting insulin above 15, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c — the marker that averages blood sugar over roughly three months) sitting between 6.5 and 7.5, body mass index (BMI) above 28, patient in India. The doctor is about to put them on nutrition protocol A. The question actually in the doctor's head is not "show me similar patients." It is:

"Show prior cases where fasting insulin >15, HbA1c between 6.5–7.5, BMI >28, country = India, and nutrition protocol A was chosen. Return decision rationale summaries, evidence used, and outcomes at 3 and 6 months."

Who else faced this exact fork, what did they choose and why, and what happened at three and six months. This system answers that in the clinical flow, on a right-rail panel, while the decision is still a draft. Three roles open it: a guidance counsellor sitting with a prospective patient before enrolment, a doctor with an existing patient, and a health coach on a minor ailment.

There is a second reader, and he arrives months later. A compliance officer — or a regulator — asks why the panel showed those ten cases on that date. Every response carries an as-of timestamp plus the version pins that produced it: the index-manifest hash, the feature-schema hash and the embedding-model version. The exact answer can be re-derived, not reconstructed from memory. That requirement shapes the architecture as much as the doctor's does.

One boundary worth stating early, because interviewers reach for it: the panel puts forward nothing. A separate service — an artificial-intelligence recommender — proposes an option and carries a confidence. This panel emits no option and no confidence. It shows what happened to comparable patients who faced the decision before. It is the evidence a human weighs when deciding whether to accept the recommender's suggestion — the thing that makes an override informed rather than a gut call.

Open with these two sentences

"A clinician drafting a treatment decision wants to know which prior decisions looked like this one, and how they turned out. The hard part isn't finding them — it's guaranteeing that nothing which happened after those decisions is allowed to influence which ones come back, because a precedent that looks wiser than it actually was is worse than no precedent at all."

Why it is genuinely hard

The spec reads like a database filter with a fuzzy tail. The design's first job is to notice that the fuzzy tail is the whole product.

The structured half — the HbA1c band, the BMI threshold, country, chosen protocol — is a cohort filter any database can run with structured query language (SQL). It returns three hundred patients. It cannot rank the most analogous decisions inside that cohort, because analogy lives in symptom phrasing, in the decision rationale, and in the pattern of evidence sitting in front of the clinician at the time. That half needs dense-vector similarity. So the system is deliberately hybrid: structured filters define the cohort, vector similarity orders it by resemblance.

Then there are three silent failure modes. None of them throws an error. All three return plausible, wrong answers — which is precisely why they destroy trust quietly instead of loudly.

  1. Future leakage — describing a past case using information that only arrived after the decision was made. It makes every precedent look wiser than it was. The obvious version: grabbing the patient's latest recorded BMI for a decision made two years ago.
  2. Tenant leakage — one clinic's patients surfacing in another clinic's results. That is a reportable privacy incident, not a bug.
  3. Immature or misattributed outcomes — showing a six-month outcome for a decision made six weeks ago, or crediting an improvement to one of two overlapping decisions when a later intervention may have caused it.

On top of those: the panel sits inside a live clinical workflow, so the warm path carries a sub-200-millisecond 99th-percentile (P99) latency budget; the corpus is protected health information (PHI), so no patient data may leave the client's virtual private cloud (VPC); and the platform is multi-tenant, so isolation is a first-class control rather than a query predicate.

The one-line framing of the risk

"A precedent panel is an advisory surface a clinician reads at decision time, so a wrong-but-plausible result is worse than an empty one — it can nudge a real clinical choice on a false analogy. The whole design is biased toward refusing to answer over answering badly."

As-of
Every field describing a past decision is the most recent valid observation at or before that decision's timestamp — never the latest value.
Censoring
Deliberately refusing to credit an outcome to a decision when a later decision could have caused it. Here: censored_by_later_decision.
kNN
k-nearest-neighbour search — return the k closest vectors to a query vector. "Filtered kNN" means the structured filters run inside that same search.
Recall@10
Is the known-relevant precedent anywhere in the top ten at all.
NDCG@10
Normalised discounted cumulative gain at 10 — is the top of that list well ordered, since the top is what a clinician actually reads.
Denormalised
Pre-joined and copied into the serving document so reading it costs one hop, not two.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation — retrieve evidence, then let a model speak over it. Here the retrieval is governed and the model's leash is very short.

02How did we solve it?

The pivotal choice: the unit of retrieval is the Decision, not the Patient

One Elasticsearch document per qualifying decision, keyed by tenant · patient · enrollment · decision · decision_ts. The query asks about an action taken at a point in time ("protocol A was chosen"), so patient-level retrieval answers the wrong question: you would get a similar patient without necessarily getting the matching decision, and a single patient carries several decisions with different rationales and different outcomes. Making the decision the addressable, embeddable, filterable object is what aligns the returned rationale, evidence and outcomes with the action the clinician is about to take. Almost every other choice in the design follows from this one.

Five stores, each doing exactly one job

  1. DynamoDB — the append-only event spine, the source of truth. Nothing is ever updated in place, so any past instant is reconstructible.
  2. Amazon Neptune — the decision graph, projected off the spine. Audit, regulator export and deep drill-down only. Never on the serving path.
  3. SageMaker — the self-hosted embedding model, in-VPC. Vectors cached by content_hash + model_version, so a document is embedded once per model version.
  4. Elasticsearch dense_vector — the authoritative serving index: filtered kNN plus the denormalised precedent-card payload in the same document.
  5. Redis — a two-tier cache (final results, and query embeddings) on canonical keys, with single-flight owner-token locks.
  6. FastAPI — the retrieval service. One authenticated endpoint, tenancy resolved from the token.

The as-of rule, and the nightly indexer that enforces it

Every night at 02:00 the indexer picks up the decisions whose follow-up has newly matured past min_followup_days, and for each one replays the append-only spine up to that decision's source_event_seq — it never reads current graph properties. It computes the clinical state, the rationale and the evidence available at the time, builds a context-only document plus a separate denormalised precedent card, embeds the document (cache hit on content_hash + model_version, otherwise a batched call to the in-VPC embedder), and upserts into a fresh versioned index carrying every version pin. Nothing is edited in place.

Outcomes are computed in a separate later pass, only once follow-up has matured, and stamped onto the card — never into the embedding. The attribution rule is "nearest eligible same-domain decision before the outcome observation wins", and every outcome record persists the attribution decision id, the rule version and the distance in days. If a later same-domain decision falls inside an earlier decision's outcome window, that earlier window is marked censored_by_later_decision rather than credited a result a subsequent intervention may have caused.

The new index is built behind an alias, shadow-evaluated against the golden set and the operational metrics, and only then does the alias flip atomically. If the acceptance floors do not hold, the system keeps serving yesterday's index and pages the on-call. The previous index is retained for rollback through a full follow-up refresh cycle.

The query path — the gates, in order

This is the sequence to be able to recite. Each step is a gate, and the ordering is load-bearing.

  1. Auth resolves the tenant scope — a three-field identity of account, workspace and control-unit, taken from the authenticated token. Never from client-supplied parameters. A caller cannot widen its own scope by editing the request body.
  2. Persona 1 parses the request. A large language model (LLM) turns free text into a typed, schema-validated QueryCase (Pydantic v2 structured output). A malformed parse is a validation retry, never a corrupted query.
  3. Cold-start quality gate. All seven required at-decision clinical fields present; at least three pieces of at-decision evidence; a minimum two-hop graph neighbourhood; and a large-enough filtered candidate pool inside the caller's own tenant. Fail any one and the panel returns an honest "insufficient comparable precedents" state with a plain cohort count — explicitly labelled a filter count and explicitly not vector similarity.
  4. Canonical cache key. A canonical sorted hash over everything that could change the result: tenant scope, query case, draft decision, K, every filter, the dedup policy, the feature-schema version, the embedding-model version, the index-manifest hash, the patient's latest event sequence and the correction epoch.
  5. Redis final-result tier. A hit skips straight to the tenant assertion — that is the warm path, and it costs milliseconds.
  6. Redis query-embedding tier. A miss on the final tier can still skip the model call. A true cold miss goes to the self-hosted embedder behind a circuit breaker at roughly 1.5 seconds; if it trips, the panel stays in its deferred state and never blocks the warm path.
  7. Filtered kNN in Elasticsearch. The tenant filter lives inside the vector query, alongside the hard structured filters on the *_at_decision fields — never applied after the fact.
  8. Oversample, then dedup. Retrieve K × an oversample factor, then exclude the query's own patient and enrollment and collapse to at most one result per patient.
  9. Read the precedent card straight from the search hit. No graph call on the serving path.
  10. Annotate outcome maturity — mature, not_mature, missing, censored, censored_by_later_decision — plus the attribution rule that linked each outcome to this decision.
  11. Assert tenancy on every returned row and verify the manifest pins match the request. Fail closed.
  12. Persona 2, optionally. One neutral summary sentence per card, generated from as-of-decision fields only, then scanned by a leakage guard and decomposed by a claim grammar. If validation fails, the sentence is suppressed with failure metadata and the cards serve anyway.
  13. Respond with the top-K cards, the as-of timestamp and the version pins. Tracing goes asynchronously to self-hosted LangSmith — metadata and payload hashes only, never raw clinical text.

The contracts that hold across every response

  • Hard filters define membership, not ranking. Similarity only orders decisions that already passed them.
  • Outcomes never touch the embedding and never touch the ranking. They ride the response as labelled payload the clinician reads after the analogous decisions have already been selected by at-decision similarity.
  • No number in any response is written by a language model. Code computes and ranks; the model only narrates.
  • Every generated sentence is backed by cited as-of-decision fields, or it is not served.
  • Hashes pin the inputs and the output — content hash, model version, validation version — so any card summary can be re-derived and audited.

Nine years of history, replayed in before launch

This was not built against an empty world. A private production system had already answered this question for roughly nine years, and that corpus is the raw material a precedent panel runs on. The platform ships a first-class hydration pipeline — a quarantine-first, proof-carrying load, not a throwaway backfill. Five properties make it trustworthy rather than merely large:

  • Event time is not ingestion time. Each legacy record keeps the real-world moment the decision was made; separate fields record when the system learned of it. Collapse the two and nine years of decisions look as though they all happened on load day — voiding every as-of reconstruction the index depends on. The watermark runs in a distinct hydration mode where a nine-year spread of "late" data is expected, then flips to a small live value at cutover.
  • Hydrating twice is a no-op. Content-addressed event ids and conditional writes make the load idempotent and resumable; re-running over the same corpus yields an identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. That is an executable proof, not an assurance.
  • Nothing is silently coerced. Each legacy payload-schema version gets an upconverter; any row that cannot be mapped to a schema — or to a tenant — is quarantined with a reason, never defaulted. A defaulted tenant would be a cross-tenant leak, the exact failure this system exists to prevent.
  • The same projection code serves history and live. Otherwise the hydrated graph would be a different product from the streaming one and every determinism guarantee would be theatre.
  • The load is proof-carrying. Zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before anything downstream is built. A fenced cutover then attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate.

What that buys this query specifically: enough matured, as-of-embedded decisions that the cold-start gate fires far less often, because the comparable cases usually already exist. A retrieval service launched against an empty index would suppress most queries on day one.

Operations: corrections, stampedes, drift

  • A correction event hits a dependency index — a reverse map from each affected entity, at the relevant event sequence, to the cache keys that depend on it — and invalidates every dependent Redis key. It atomically marks the affected search documents stale and bumps the index-manifest hash; because every serving query carries a mandatory "not stale" filter, a stale document is invisible to serving until a priority reindex clears the flag. Target: 95th-percentile (P95) under five minutes from correction ingestion to fresh document plus cleared flag plus invalidated keys.
  • Out-of-order corrections are dropped idempotently — each entity tracks the sequence of the last applied correction, and anything at or below it is discarded with a metric, so a delayed replay cannot clobber a newer fix.
  • Cache stampede on a cold key is handled by a single-flight lock with a random owner token and a compare-and-delete unlock. The lock's time-to-live (TTL) is derived from the cold-path breaker plus the search timeout plus a margin, with jitter — never a fixed guess. Waiters poll the final-result key and fall back to stale-while-revalidate or a deferred response.
  • Drift is caught daily: the index's document count, the eligible-decisions count and the graph's embedded-flag count must agree under the current manifest. Divergence means a build dropped or double-counted documents.
  • Cardinality blow-up is an explicit alert — a dependency-set lookup returning more than about a thousand keys pages, as does any document staying stale beyond about thirty minutes.

03Why did we do what we did?

Each block is a decision, the alternative that was rejected, and the reason. The quoted lines are the reasoning as written at the time — those are the sentences to recall under pressure, because they are short enough to survive an interruption.

The shape of the problem

The Decision is the retrieval unit, not the Patient

Rejected
Patient-level embeddings — one document per patient.
Because
The query asks about an action taken at a point in time. Patient-level retrieval returns a similar patient without necessarily returning the matching decision, and one patient carries several decisions with different rationales and outcomes.

"Almost every other choice in the design follows from this one." Getting it wrong — patient-level — was the original defect that returned the wrong retrieval grain.

Hybrid retrieval: structured filters plus dense-vector similarity

Rejected
A pure SQL cohort query; or a pure vector search with no hard filters.
Because
SQL returns a cohort and stops. It cannot rank the most analogous decisions inside it, because analogy lives in symptom phrasing, rationale and evidence pattern. A pure vector search, conversely, cannot guarantee the stated clinical criteria are met.

"The spec looks like a database filter with a fuzzy tail. The design's first job is to notice that the fuzzy tail is the whole product."

The structured filters stay hard, not soft-weighted into the vector

Rejected
Soft-weighting protocol, HbA1c band and country into the embedding for more flexible matching.
Because
A soft weight would let a very similar protocol-B decision leak into a protocol-A request. It also keeps the two halves separable for evaluation: you can measure whether the filter is too restrictive (empty-result rate) independently of whether the ranking is good (NDCG@10).

"Clinical filters encode contracts, not preferences." A clinician who asks for protocol A never sees protocol B, no matter how similar the embedding.

One index shaped for this question, not a general-purpose embedding

Rejected
A single reusable patient-embedding index serving many questions.
Because
The index-time and query-time documents must be in the same distribution to be comparable. A general-purpose patient embedding blurs the decision grain and pulls in post-decision or outcome signal — leaking the future. Scoping the index to the decision-level, context-only shape is what lets the acceptance gates mean anything.

Time, and not describing the past with the future

Reconstruct from the append-only spine; never read current graph properties

Rejected
Reading the decision's current value map from the graph — cheaper, one hop, obvious.
Because
The graph is a projection. If someone edits a rationale or removes an evidence edge after the fact, the current value reflects that later edit, and reading it folds a future change into a historical document. The spine is immutable, so reading it at the decision's event sequence is the only way to recover at-decision truth.

"The latest recorded BMI is the obvious field to grab" — and it is banned, because for a decision made two years ago that BMI was observed long after the clinician acted. This was a priority-zero (P0) review finding, now enforced by feature-freshness audit fields carried on every row.

Outcomes ride on the card, never inside the embedding

Rejected
Embedding the full decision record including its three- and six-month outcomes.
Because
Outcomes are post-decision facts by definition. Embedding them would make similar-looking decisions cluster by how they turned out rather than by how they looked at the time — which is exactly the wrong axis for precedent.

Graph-relationship freshness is tracked separately from clinical-feature freshness

Rejected
One collapsed as-of timestamp covering everything.
Because
A single number would hide that the evidence edges were, say, fresher than the clinical labs — and that distinction is exactly what you need when auditing whether a document leaked. Clinical freshness gets one as-of timestamp; rationale, supporting-evidence and constraining-policy edges each carry their own sequence field.

Folding them into one number was itself a reviewer correction in a later round.

A nightly batch rebuild with a blue-green swap, never an in-place edit

Rejected
Indexing decisions as they stream in, updating documents in place.
Because
A precedent only becomes eligible once its follow-up window matures — a decision made six weeks ago has no six-month outcome — so indexing is a function of time passing, not just of the decision arriving. And in-place edits make a bad partial write indistinguishable from a good one, and defeat rollback: you cannot atomically revert a document you overwrote.

Corrections do get a near-real-time targeted reindex, so the nightly cadence is the floor for routine maturation, not the ceiling for correctness.

Tenancy — where the design is deliberately redundant

Five layers of isolation, including a response-time assertion that duplicates the query filter

Rejected
Trusting the tenant predicate in the vector query, since it is already there.
Because
The filter and the assertion fail differently. A filter can be silently dropped by a refactor, a bad alias, or a cache key that lost its prefix. The layers: a traversal wrapper injecting tenant scope on every graph read and write; a mandatory tenant filter inside the vector query; tenant-prefixed cache keys; a response-time assertion on every returned row, failing closed; and per-tenant graph endpoints for tenants in the PHI/regulatory risk tier.

"It is the difference between 'we intended to filter' and 'we verified every row before it left the process.'"

A thin candidate pool is never backfilled from another tenant

Rejected
Borrowing comparable decisions across tenants to keep the panel useful for small clinics.
Because
Pooling across tenants would be a cross-tenant leak — the exact failure the whole isolation stack exists to prevent. Thin-pool tenants get the honest empty state, not a diluted cross-tenant answer.

"Privacy is an incident class, not a metric to optimise." Cross-tenant assertion failures carry a pre-registered target of zero and a page-out.

Latency, caching and determinism

The graph database is off the serving path entirely

Rejected
Reading the precedent's rationale, evidence and outcomes live from the graph at serve time.
Because
The warm path has a sub-200-millisecond P99 budget and must not depend on a graph traversal's tail. Every field the panel needs — summary, short rationale, evidence one-liners, both outcome objects with status — is denormalised into the search document at index time. The consequence is operational, not just fast: if the graph is degraded, the clinician's panel is unaffected.

"The budget is a design constraint that shaped the architecture, not a target bolted on afterward."

Canonical cache keys pin everything result-affecting; TTL is a backstop, not the correctness mechanism

Rejected
A short TTL as the freshness strategy.
Because
A TTL only bounds how long a stale entry can live; it does nothing to prevent serving a wrong answer inside that window, and in clinical use even a short window of stale rationale or outcomes is unacceptable. Freshness is enforced structurally: the key includes the version pins and the patient's latest event sequence, so a change invalidates by making the key miss.

"A stale cache entry is unreachable rather than merely unlikely."

Dedup to one decision per patient — tracked, not thresholded

Rejected
Returning the raw top-K; or setting a fixed acceptable suppression rate.
Because
A clinician wants K analogous situations, not K near-duplicate decisions from one prolific patient's timeline. But there is no single correct suppression rate — it depends on how many multi-decision patients sit in the cohort — so a fixed threshold would be arbitrary. The rate is tracked and alerts on a spike, which is the real signal that something changed.

Refusal as a feature

Suppress on thin data, and show a plain cohort count instead

Rejected
Serving a short, low-confidence ranked list and letting the clinician judge.
Because
A ranked similarity list built on thin data is misleadingly confident, whereas a structured-filter cohort count is an honest, non-ranked fact the clinician can still use. The suppressed response is explicitly labelled as a filter count and explicitly not as vector similarity, so the two can never be confused.

"The panel does not manufacture a thin ranked list to fill the silence. The human decides without precedent evidence, and is told that is what is happening."

The empty-result rate is itself a first-class metric

Rejected
Treating refusal as automatically virtuous.
Because
"An honest gate that fires constantly is still a broken product." The tension is made measurable: a pre-registered target under 5% per program, so a gate that suppresses too often trips an alert rather than silently frustrating clinicians. If a program's pool is chronically small, that surfaces as a data-coverage problem to fix upstream, not a gate to loosen.

The honest caveat, worth volunteering: for the guidance counsellor sitting with a prospective patient, refusal is the common case, not the edge case — there is no decision history and no graph neighbourhood for the gate to pass. A floor that is comfortable across a doctor's caseload is not automatically comfortable there.

The model boundary

The language model appears at exactly two edges and nowhere in between

Rejected
LLM re-ranking of candidates; LLM-computed outcome deltas; an agent that runs the search.
Because
Similarity search, filtering, dedup and outcome computation must be reproducible, auditable and correct. A model at any of those points introduces non-determinism and un-auditability into clinical results. So it parses natural language in, and narrates one sentence out, and everything between is code.

"Know when not to use a language model." The summary sentence is optional — the card reads fully from its structured fields, and the sentence can be rendered from a deterministic template instead. "The model is an enhancement on a short leash, never a dependency."

Self-hosted inference in-VPC — not an external provider under a no-retention contract

Rejected
A managed model application programming interface (API) with a contractual no-training, no-retention agreement.
Because
Conflating the two is a real error. A no-retention agreement is a legal promise about what a provider does after receiving the data; it is not a network-residency control and it does not keep clinical text on your network. Production runs self-hosted Llama 3.1 (an 8- billion-parameter workhorse with a 70-billion-parameter escalation) and a self-hosted embedder, both in-VPC. An external provider is permitted only with de-identified payloads under a signed data-processing agreement.

Evaluation

Clinician-graded golden set, not model-graded relevance

Rejected
Using a language model as the relevance grader — far cheaper and faster.
Because
Relevance here is a clinical judgement about whether a precedent is genuinely analogous in context, evidence and decision intent. "A model grader would encode the same distributional priors as the retriever, flattering it." Model grading is permitted only to pre-screen candidates. Acceptance labels are two clinicians per query on a 0–3 rubric, with adjudication when they disagree by two or more grades or weighted kappa falls below 0.60.

Per-program Recall@10 floored separately, and lower

Rejected
A single global recall number.
Because
Small programs have thin candidate pools and get averaged into invisibility, yet a clinician in that program experiences its recall, not the platform's. A separate lower floor catches segment collapse before launch rather than after. It is a deliberate guard against "good on average, broken for you."

Say this when asked about the review process

"Designs earn approval; they don't declare it. Approved-with-nits is not 'done' — it means the design is sound and the remaining items are named tasks rather than unknowns. I kept the findings that hurt in the trail, including the one where my own runnable prototype checked two of seven gate fields when the design said seven."

What is still honestly open

  • The production vector store. Elasticsearch dense_vector is the default behind an index abstraction; the pgvector / Qdrant / Pinecone bake-off has explicit acceptance criteria and is not yet closed.
  • Which tenants sit in the PHI/regulatory risk tier that warrants per-tenant graph endpoints — a legal and compliance classification, not an engineering one.
  • Whether "country" means the patient's country at decision time — the design commits to that reading, but confirming it against product intent is open. The wrong reading would filter on the tenant's registered jurisdiction and silently change the cohort.
  • The actual current, 10× and 100× post-filter candidate-pool sizes that the gate thresholds ultimately depend on.
  • Which clinicians label the golden set, and which tenants (if any) have signed external-embedding agreements.

    API --> UI[Clinician panel<br/>warm + deferred cold states]
    IDX -.-> EVAL[Offline eval harness<br/>NDCG@10 · Recall@10 · P99]

Fig 1 · The graph database is never on the serving path

What flows where

Left to right in time, top to bottom on the page. Events land on the DynamoDB append-only spine — the source of truth, never updated in place. A projection keeps the Neptune decision graph current. A nightly snapshot exports vertices, edges and a manifest, so the indexer works from a pinned, hashed artefact rather than a moving live graph — that pinning is what makes a build reproducible and auditable.

The featurise step is where the leakage control lives: it computes each decision's context as-of decision_ts, with no outcomes and no post-decision evidence. That context-only document goes to the self-hosted embedder inside the VPC, cached by content_hash + model_version so a document is embedded once per model version. The vector plus the denormalised precedent card land in Elasticsearch, which is the authoritative serving index. The retrieval API is the only thing clinicians touch; it fronts Redis and the panel. The dashed edge to the offline eval harness is dashed on purpose: evaluation reads the same index but is never in the request path.

The gate that is not drawn as a gate

Notice what is missing: there is no arrow from Neptune to the retrieval API. That absence is the design. The graph is upstream of the index and nowhere near serving.

Without it: the warm path would inherit the graph's tail latency, and the sub-200-millisecond P99 budget would be unmeetable in practice. Worse operationally, a degraded graph would take the clinician's panel down with it. As drawn, a graph outage costs you audit export and deep drill-down — and nothing the clinician sees.
Without the caching key on the embedder: every nightly rebuild would re-embed the entire nine-year corpus rather than only what changed, and a model-version change would be indistinguishable from a content change.

Fig 3 · Replay, featurise, embed, blue-green swap — nothing edited in place

What flows where

A cron at 02:00 selects only decisions whose follow-up has newly matured past min_followup_days — eligibility is a function of time passing, not of the decision arriving. For each, the indexer replays the spine up to that decision's source_event_seq, explicitly not reading current graph state. It computes at-decision context, builds two artefacts from it — a context-only document that will be embedded, and a denormalised precedent card that will not — and checks the embedding cache.

The upsert writes into a fresh versioned index, keyed by tenant · patient · enrollment · decision · decision_ts and carrying every version pin. From that upsert two things fan out: a separate outcome pass that stamps the three- and six-month results with censoring and attribution rules applied, and the blue-green swap.

The three gates

  • The embedding-cache diamond is a cost and determinism gate: the same content under the same model version must never produce two different vectors, and re-embedding nine years nightly would be indefensible.
  • The blue-green swap diamond is the safety gate. Shadow evaluation runs the golden set and the operational metrics against the new index before any traffic sees it. Pass and the alias flips atomically; fail and yesterday's index keeps serving while the on-call is paged.
  • The final invalidation node is not a step so much as a consequence: because every cache key pins index_manifest_hash, the alias flip makes every entry keyed to the old manifest unreachable. Nothing has to be deleted. Morning traffic re-warms.
Without the replay step (reading current graph properties instead): every document would be described using edits and observations that arrived after the decision. Precedents would look systematically wiser than they were, and no error would ever be raised.
Without the separate outcome pass: outcomes would end up inside the embedding, and decisions would cluster by how they turned out rather than by how they looked at the time — matching on the one axis that is unknowable at draft time.
Without blue-green: a bad build would reach clinicians before anyone noticed, and there would be no atomic way back — you cannot revert a document you overwrote.

Fig 5 · Refuse early, pin every version, fail closed on tenancy

What flows where

Read it as four bands. Band one, identity: auth resolves the three-field tenant scope; the client cannot assert its own. Band two, refusal: the cold-start gate runs before any expensive work — before the cache lookup, before the embedder, before the search. Band three, retrieval: canonical key, then two cache tiers, then the filtered kNN, then dedup, then the card read, then maturity annotation. Band four, the choke point: every path — warm cache hit and full cold path alike — converges on the same tenant assertion before anything is returned.

What each gate does

  • The cold-start gate checks four different ways the answer could be confidently wrong: a missing one of seven required at-decision fields means similarity is computed on a partial vector; fewer than three evidence items means the case is under-described; a sparse two-hop neighbourhood means an unreliable case; and a small candidate pool means similarity is noise. Fail any one and the clinician gets a plain cohort count, labelled a count and never a similarity.
  • The canonical cache key is a gate against time: it pins the tenant scope, the query, every filter, the schema and model versions, the index manifest, the patient's event-sequence watermark, the correction epoch, and the dedup and maturity rule versions. Anything upstream changes, the key stops matching.
  • The two cache diamonds are a cost ladder. A final-result hit is the warm path and costs milliseconds. A miss can still hit the query-embedding tier and skip the model. Only a true cold query pays for an embedding.
  • The circuit breaker at 1.5 seconds on the embedder is a blast wall: if the model is slow or throttling, the panel returns in its deferred state with skeleton rows rather than holding the request open. A model miss never blocks the warm code path, because the warm path does not call the model at all.
  • Oversample-then-dedup retrieves more than K precisely because the next step removes the query's own patient and enrollment and collapses to one result per patient. Without oversampling, dedup could leave fewer than K.
  • The tenant assertion is the last line. It re-checks what the query filter already enforced, and fails closed.
Without the gate running first: a thin case would still burn an embedding call and a search, and — much worse — would return a confident-looking ranked list built on a partial vector. The whole product's trust budget is spent on results like that.
Without the tenant assertion: the isolation story would rest on a single query predicate that a refactor, a bad alias or a cache key missing its prefix could silently drop. The assertion is what turns a mis-keyed cache entry into a paged incident instead of a silent leak.
Without the maturity annotation: a six-week-old decision would show a blank or partial six-month outcome that reads as "no improvement", and an outcome that a later intervention may have caused would be credited to this one.

Fig 8 — The language-model boundary

flowchart TD
    FT[Clinician free text] --> P1[LLM · persona 1<br/>precedent-librarian<br/>parses text into a typed QueryCase<br/>structured output · no retrieval]
    P1 --> DET[Deterministic core<br/>cold-start gate · canonical cache keys ·<br/>filtered kNN · dedup · maturity status ·<br/>every number computed by code]
    DET --> P2[LLM · persona 2<br/>card-summarizer<br/>one neutral sentence from<br/>as-of-decision fields only]
    P2 --> GUARD[Leakage guard<br/>rejects any summary containing<br/>post-decision values]
    GUARD --> OUT[Precedent cards<br/>to the clinician]

Fig 8 · Two personas, one guard — the summary sentence is the only field a model writes

What flows where

This is the whole governance story in five boxes, and it is the one to draw first on a whiteboard if the interviewer asks about the artificial intelligence (AI) architecture. Free text enters. Persona 1, the precedent-librarian, converts it into a typed QueryCase — structured output, schema-validated, with no retrieval powers of its own. Everything in the middle is the deterministic core: the gate, the cache keys, the filtered kNN, dedup, maturity status. Every number in the response is computed there. Persona 2, the card-summarizer, writes one neutral sentence per card from as-of-decision fields only, at temperature 0 against a pinned model and a pinned system prompt that forbids computing, re-ranking or concluding — it may only restate. The leakage guard then scans that sentence for any post-decision value and a claim grammar decomposes it into templates backed by cited as-of fields, rejecting anything it cannot decompose.

The shape of the failure

On a validation failure the sentence is dropped with failure metadata and the ranked cards, their evidence and their outcomes serve regardless. That is why the boundary holds: the answer never depends on the model. The worst case is a missing sentence, not a wrong clinical fact.

Without the typed contract on persona 1: a malformed parse becomes a corrupted query that runs silently and returns the wrong cohort. With it, a bad parse is a retry or a safe fallback — "no answer or safe default", never "wrong answer".
Without the guard on persona 2: a summary could quietly narrate a post-decision value — "the patient's HbA1c later fell to 6.1" — and reintroduce exactly the future leakage the entire indexing pipeline exists to prevent, at the very last step.
If the model were moved into the middle box (re-ranking, or computing an outcome delta): the ranking would stop being reproducible and the numbers would stop being auditable. The honest limit is worth saying out loud: pinning the version, prompt and temperature gives drift detection and reproducible auditing, but it does not make generation truly deterministic — which is exactly why nothing load-bearing sits there.

05Likely questions

Headline sentence first, every time. Detail only if they ask for it — and in a round with seven interruptions and three time warnings, they often will not.

Why is the retrieval unit a decision and not a patient?the one they always ask

Headline: Because the question is about an action taken at a point in time, and a single patient carries several decisions with different rationales and different outcomes.

Patient-level retrieval returns a similar patient without necessarily returning the matching decision — you would surface someone whose profile rhymes but whose clinical fork was different. Making the decision the addressable object means the document key is tenant · patient · enrollment · decision · decision_ts, and the returned rationale, evidence and outcomes all line up with the action the clinician is about to take. It is the most consequential decision in the design; the context-only embedding, the denormalised card and the outcome-attribution rule all follow from it.

You say there is no future leakage. Prove it, don't assert it.

Headline: Two mechanisms — every row carries auditable freshness fields so a replay can check that no feature's freshness postdates its decision, and a leakage guard scans every generated sentence for post-decision values.

The structural control is that features are reconstructed by replaying the immutable spine up to the decision's event sequence, never by reading current graph properties — because the graph is a projection and a later edit would fold the future into a historical document. Each row then carries the as-of timestamp plus per-relationship sequence fields, kept deliberately separate so you can see if the evidence edges were fresher than the clinical labs. On top of that, the platform's deterministic exporter and its golden replay now prove that re-exporting the same point-in-time under shuffled spine order and late arrivals yields identical hashes. The honest part: those are audit mechanisms, not a proof of absence — which is why leakage is also a named acceptance concern rather than a box that was ticked once.

One shared index across tenants. Convince me clinic A can never see clinic B's patients.

Headline: Five independent layers, any one of which can fail without leaking — and a cross-tenant result is treated as a page-out incident with a pre-registered target of zero, not as a bug.

Tenant identity is three fields — account, workspace, control-unit — resolved from the authenticated token, never from the request body, so a client cannot widen its own scope. Then: a traversal wrapper injects the scope on every graph read and write; the tenant filter lives inside the vector query rather than being applied afterwards; cache keys are tenant-prefixed and the tenant scope is a field in the canonical hash; a response-time assertion re-checks every returned row and fails closed; and tenants in the protected-health-information risk tier get physically separate graph endpoints. The assertion looks redundant, and that is the point — a filter can be silently dropped by a refactor, a bad alias, or a key that lost its prefix. It is the difference between "we intended to filter" and "we verified every row before it left the process." And when a tenant's candidate pool is too thin, it is never backfilled from another tenant — the gate refuses instead.

A time-to-live is the normal way to keep a cache fresh. Why isn't it enough?

Headline: A TTL only bounds how long a stale entry can live; it does nothing to stop you serving a wrong answer inside that window, and in clinical use even a short window of stale rationale is unacceptable.

Freshness is enforced structurally instead. The cache key is a canonical sorted hash over everything that could change the result: tenant scope, query case, draft decision, K, every filter, the dedup policy, the feature-schema version, the embedding-model version, the index-manifest hash, the patient's latest event sequence, and the correction epoch. Anything upstream changes and the key simply stops matching — a stale entry becomes unreachable rather than merely unlikely. Upstream corrections additionally push: a dependency index maps affected entities to dependent keys, invalidates them, marks the search documents stale and bumps the manifest hash, with a P95 target of five minutes. Canonical sorted JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) is what stops field ordering from causing accidental misses. The TTL survives as memory hygiene, not as the correctness mechanism.

Cosine similarity over an embedding is a black box. Why would a clinician trust the ranking?

Headline: The clinician isn't asked to trust the similarity number — every returned card is fully readable from its structured fields, so they evaluate the precedent, not the score.

The card carries the at-decision clinical values, the rationale, the evidence and both outcome windows with explicit maturity status. The score is a retrieval mechanism, and its quality is acceptance-gated by clinician-graded floors — NDCG@10 at least 0.75, Recall@10 at least 0.80, and a separate per-program floor of 0.60 so a small program cannot be averaged into invisibility. Two clinicians label each golden query on a 0–3 rubric with adjudication on disagreement; a model grader was explicitly rejected because it would encode the same distributional priors as the retriever and flatter it. Trust comes from the gate, not from the score being self-explanatory.

Your gate refuses to answer. Isn't a product that frequently says nothing a broken product?

Headline: Yes — which is why the empty-result rate is itself a first-class metric with a pre-registered target under 5% per program, so an over-firing gate trips an alert instead of silently frustrating clinicians.

The gate exists because a wrong-but-plausible precedent can nudge a real clinical choice on a false analogy, so suppression beats emission on thin data — and the suppressed state is still useful: a plain structured-filter cohort count, explicitly labelled a count and never as vector similarity. Nine years of hydrated history is the other half of the answer: with a deep back-catalogue the candidate-pool condition is satisfiable for most real queries, so the gate can be tuned to protect genuinely thin cases rather than ordinary ones. And there is a caveat I would volunteer rather than wait to be caught on: for a guidance counsellor sitting with a prospective patient who has no history at all, refusal is the common case, not the edge case. A floor that is comfortable across a doctor's caseload is not automatically comfortable there, and where the two meet is a question for measured behaviour.

Where is the language model, and why isn't it doing the ranking?

Headline: In exactly two thin gated roles — parsing a request in and writing one optional sentence out — because ranking and numbers have to be reproducible and auditable, and a model at either point would make them neither.

Persona 1 turns free text into a typed, schema-validated QueryCase, so a malformed parse is a validation retry rather than a corrupted query. Persona 2 writes one neutral sentence per card from as-of-decision fields only, at temperature 0 against a pinned model and prompt that forbid computing, re-ranking or concluding. A leakage guard and a claim grammar then reject any sentence carrying a post-decision value or citing a field not in the card — and on rejection the cards serve without it. The sentence is optional by design and can be rendered from a deterministic template instead. Everything in between is code: the gate, the cache keys, the filtered kNN, the dedup, the maturity status. No number in any response is written by a model. The principle is knowing when not to use one.

What is the worst realistic failure this design still allows?

Headline: A subtle featurisation or attribution bug that passes the golden set but systematically mis-ranks one thin segment.

The gates catch gross regressions, and per-program Recall@10 catches a small program collapsing — but a narrow, plausible error can survive if the golden set under-samples that stratum. The honest mitigation is stratified sampling, per-program floors and human clinician grading, not a claim of completeness. That is also why the review verdict is approved-with-nits rather than a declaration that the system cannot be wrong. The related honest limit on the product itself: it is an observational precedent surface. It shows which prior decisions most resembled this one and what observably happened next. It cannot tell a clinician what to do, cannot claim a protocol caused an outcome, and refuses outright when the case is too thin. Its value is disciplined analogy, and its integrity comes from being explicit about that boundary.

"It answers, at the moment a clinician drafts a decision, which prior decisions were most analogous — with their rationale, the evidence in front of that clinician at the time, and outcomes at three and six months. The retrieval unit is the decision, not the patient. Everything embedded is reconstructed as-of the moment the decision was made by replaying an append-only spine, so outcomes never contaminate the matching.
