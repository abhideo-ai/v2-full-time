Case study · ML system design · Healthcare

A high-risk lab result just landed — how long until someone decides?

For a chronic-care platform covering 80+ conditions, I designed the decision-latency analytics system and took it to production. It measures the time from a high-risk lab arriving to the treatment decision that answers it — split by clinician, geography, and AI usage. The denominator counts the labs nobody answered, every statistic is seeded and replayable, and the per-clinician view is governed to resist scorecard use — pseudonymous ids, leadership-or-self access, an audit entry written before every read, and deliberately unrankable ordering. The same discipline applies to any turnaround-time question where dropping the unanswered cases would flatter the number: support SLAs, incident response, underwriting queues.

01 The problem

The dangerous version of this dashboard is one join away

On paper this is a subtraction: join each high-risk lab to the next treatment decision, subtract two timestamps, take the median, GROUP BY clinician. Every clause of that sentence hides a way to ship a confident, wrong number — so the design treats each one as a first-class problem with a named control:

  • Which decision "corresponds"? Nearest-in-time joins happily pair a lab with an unrelated decision. Here, only pairs where the decision explicitly cites the lab as evidence in the decision graph may make the headline, and a versioned allowlist defines which decision types count — the definition can evolve, but never by silently rewriting history.
  • Survivorship bias. The labs that never got a decision are precisely the ones leadership most needs to see — and an inner join deletes them. The denominator is built from an ungated lab feed, and unanswered labs are carried as right-censored observations, never dropped.
  • The wrong clock. Subtract an ingestion timestamp from a write timestamp and you are measuring the database, not the clinic. Every row preserves four distinct timestamps, and the latency definition pins exactly which two are subtracted.
  • Performance data. Per-clinician latency reads as a scorecard the moment it renders. Clinicians are pseudonymous by default, the split is need-to-know by role, every actor-level view is audit-logged, and the ordering is alphabetic — deliberately never rank-sortable.
02 The architecture

Snapshot-only analytics with an honest denominator

The pipeline never touches the live graph at query time. A nightly export freezes the decision graph at a single as-of watermark; a pair builder runs analytical joins over the snapshot — no per-row graph calls — to pair each high-risk lab with its treatment decision; segmentation covariates and AI attribution are reconstructed as of the moment they were true by replaying the append-only event spine; and everything downstream is pre-computed into columnar metric tables served by an embedded analytical engine.

The ungated denominator

Most of the platform's analytics read a projection that materialises an observation only once it attaches to a decision. For a latency question, that gate is a quiet catastrophe: labs that never got a decision would never enter the data, and the platform would look fastest exactly where it is failing worst. This system therefore reads a second, ungated lab feed, so every high-risk lab enters the denominator whether or not anyone ever acted on it. It is the single most important line in the design.

The nightly build, read as a conversation between components, shows where the honesty lives in the handoffs. Two feeds are consulted, not one: an ungated lab feed returns every high-risk lab — paired or not — each already carrying its four timestamps, while the snapshot returns only the decisions that cite a lab as evidence. Segmentation covariates and AI attribution are fetched by replaying the append-only spine to the decision's own moment, never read from current state. And the two numbers that matter most — the latency and the row's deterministic identity — are computed inside the builder, off the snapshot, so nothing statistical ever touches the live graph.

Read covariates from current state instead of replaying them to the decision moment and a patient who later changed country or programme would silently rewrite their own history — the as-of replay is the one thing keeping a two-year-old pair labelled the way it actually was.

03 Hydration

Nine years of labs and decisions, replayed into the spine

This system was never pointed at an empty graph. Roughly nine years of prior labs and treatment decisions already existed in a private production system, and that history is hydrated through the same append-only spine the live stream writes to — a first-class, quarantine-first, proof-carrying pipeline, not a throwaway backfill. A latency question is especially unforgiving here, because it is built entirely on timestamps and on pairing depth, and both are exactly what a careless load destroys.

  • Event time is not ingestion time. Each legacy record carries the real-world moment it happened — the lab was resulted, the decision was made — while separate fields record when the system learned of it. Collapse the two and nine years of labs and decisions would all bear the load date; every as-of reconstruction, and every latency subtraction, would be void. Hydration therefore runs under a watermark sized for a nine-year spread of "late" data, not the minutes-to-days window the live stream uses.
  • Hydrating twice is a no-op. Every event gets a content-addressed identifier and a conditional write, so the load is idempotent and resumable: re-running the pipeline over the same corpus yields an identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. That equality is an executable proof, not an assurance.
  • Nothing is silently coerced. Nine years means many legacy payload-schema versions; each gets its own upconverter, and any row that cannot be mapped to a known schema — or to a tenant — is quarantined with a reason rather than defaulted. A defaulted tenant would be a cross-tenant leak, and a coerced timestamp would poison exactly the latency numbers this system exists to protect.
  • The same projection code runs for history and for live. Historical events are replayed through the identical projection and pairing logic that serves the live stream. Route history through different code and the hydrated latency distributions would belong to a different product than the one validated every day.
  • The load is proof-carrying. A zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before a single latency table is built on top of it. A fenced cutover then attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate at the boundary.

For a latency question, the most dangerous thing a nine-year load can do is quietly stamp every historical record with the date it was loaded. Hydration forbids that structurally. The two clocks the subtraction reads — when a decision was made, when a lab result reached the platform — are recovered from each legacy record's own domain fields; only the write-into-the-new-spine timestamp is pinned to a single load-fence constant. A per-row guard then asserts that every event's real-world time falls strictly before its ingestion time, so any value that accidentally defaulted to the load date fails the load rather than poisoning a distribution. And because as-of order sorts by event time — never by the order rows happened to be written during the load — a latency computed from a lab resulted years ago is a same-era subtraction, indistinguishable in kind from one computed today.

Collapse event time into ingestion time and the guard trips on the first row; skip the guard and nine years of latencies quietly compress toward zero as every record inherits the same load date. The invariant is cheap; the failure it prevents is unrecoverable.

What nine years buys this latency question

A latency median is only honest when the cell behind it has real support. Nine years of resulted labs and their treatment decisions give each segment — clinician, geography, AI-assisted or not — enough supported pairs to clear the thirty-per-cell floor and report a median and a P90, instead of collapsing to "insufficient data". Depth of history is what keeps the segmentation from suppressing itself.

04 The pairing spine

Which rows are allowed to make the number

Every high-risk lab in the window gets exactly one of three pair methods, and the method decides what the row may do. Only pairs where the decision explicitly cites the lab as evidence can make the headline median and P90. Labs paired by heuristic — the next clinically relevant decision within fourteen days — are a separate diagnostic slice until the heuristic is clinically validated. Labs with no decision at all are right-censored at the fourteen-day window and flow into a Kaplan–Meier-style time-to-decision estimate — the same survival math clinical trials use for patients who have not had the event yet — and an explicit unpaired rate. Nothing is deleted; everything is labelled.

The per-segment gate makes the split self-policing: if the evidence-paired share of any segment falls below 0.80, that segment's headline is suppressed and only the pair-method breakdown is served — the dashboard says "pairing quality too low", never a smooth median built on guesswork. Each pair also carries a deterministic identity — a hash over tenant, lab, decision, pairing-rule version, and snapshot hash — so a rebuilt night reproduces identical rows, and a changed pairing rule is a new version that can be diffed against the old one, never a silent redefinition of history.

That identity is what makes the whole pipeline replayable and cheaply correctable. The pair identity is a stable hash of five deterministic inputs, so a rebuilt night reproduces byte-identical rows and the golden regression can demand equality down to the row id; and when a single upstream correction lands, a dependency index rebuilds only the pairs whose identity depends on it rather than the whole table.

Without a stable identity every correction would force a full-table rebuild, and the five-minute freshness target would be impossible to keep; deterministic ids are what make targeted rebuilds cheap enough to promise.

The other half of honesty is the clock. Every event carries four timestamps — when the thing clinically happened, when the result was observed, when it reached the platform, and when it landed in the store — and the latency definition pins exactly two of them. Because clinics live in time zones, a tenant-local, versioned business-hours view is computed alongside wall clock: a lab arriving at midnight is not evidence that the clinic slept on it. Both views ship, and the dashboard can toggle between them.

05 The query path

Audit first, gate before headline

Serving is deliberately boring. An audit entry is written before the response; a canonical cache key pins the tenant scope — account, workspace, and control-unit — plus the query shape, pairing-rule version, metric schema, snapshot manifest, and correction epoch — if any of those move, the key moves, so a stale number can never be served under a fresh name. On a miss, the API reads pre-computed metric tables; nothing statistical is recomputed at query time. The viewer's role decides whether the per-clinician split exists in the response at all.

Seen as a timeline rather than a branch tree, the ordering is itself the guarantee. The audit entry is written before any data is fetched; the tenant assertion and the pseudonymisation run on a cache hit exactly as they do on a miss; and the pairing and sample-size floors decide the shape of the response last — either a headline or an honest suppression.

A cache hit still pays for audit, tenant assertion, and pseudonymisation on every request — the warm path skips the recompute, never the governance.

07 The LLM boundary

Where the language model is: nowhere

The through-line of this portfolio is knowing when not to use an LLM, and this system is the purest case: the number of language-model calls in the pipeline is zero. Latency is timestamp arithmetic. The quantiles, confidence intervals, and censoring-aware estimates are seeded, replayable statistics. The gates, suppression rules, and access controls are deterministic code. There is no step here a language model would improve — and every step is one it could quietly degrade.

There is likewise no retrieval-augmented generation surface, and that absence is a decision rather than a gap: a latency median is read from a pre-computed cell and served with its confidence interval and its pairing-quality caveat already attached, so there is nothing left to fetch and nothing a generated sentence could add that the cell does not already state.

The "AI" in the question is measured, not used. "Segmented by AI usage" means the pipeline reconstructs, from the event spine as of each decision's moment, whether and how AI assisted that decision — usage type, the agent event, the human sign-off — and reports it as an observational split. That split describes association, not effect: clinicians may reach for AI assistance on different kinds of cases, so the comparison ships with observational caveats, and is blocked entirely when pairing quality differs materially between the arms.

The platform's gated LLM edge — self-hosted Llama 3.1 inside the VPC, used by sibling systems for parsing and narration — exists one layer up, and this service deliberately takes no dependency on it. Numbers a regulator may one day audit should not have a language model anywhere in their provenance.

08 The stack

Boring infrastructure, deliberate boundaries

The analytical core is deliberately unexciting — columnar files and an embedded query engine, each doing one job, plus a shared statistics package so every cohort question in the programme computes its intervals the same way. The second table is where an AI layer would normally sit; for this system the honest entry is what it deliberately does not use.

Read as a picture, the stack falls into three lanes and one boundary. The request lane is the short list of things a clinician actually waits on: the API, a canonical-key result cache, the pre-computed metric tables, and the governance gates. The nightly lane runs on a schedule — snapshot export, the pair and metric builders, the correction listener — none of it in a request's critical path. The one-time lane is the historical hydration that seeded the spine. Every one of those boxes lives inside the client VPC; the only thing outside it is the clinician's browser, which receives aggregated, pseudonymised cells over TLS and never any PHI. The two boxes drawn with dotted edges — the decision graph and the model seam — are precisely the ones a clinician's request never touches. The two tables below are the legend: they say what each box is; the picture says when it is touched and where the trust boundary sits.

Fifteen ways for a build to fail

10 The review gauntlet

How adversarial review hardened this into a reusable pattern

Before anything shipped, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens — and the first pass hit hard: eighteen findings, five of them high-severity in correctness alone.

  1. Design review · round 1 Ship-with-changes, 18 findings — unstable pairing rules, an undefined notion of the "corresponding" decision, wrong time semantics, missing tenant isolation, the gated-denominator exposure, and the governance finding that per-clinician latency is performance data.
  2. Design revision · v2 Eleven findings absorbed directly into the design — the pair-method split with its headline lock, versioned pairing rules, the four-timestamp contract, tenant-scoped build and partitioning, censoring-aware statistics, the canonical cache key. Seven were platform-level and routed to shared components already tracked by id — tenant wrapper, correction backbone, ungated projection, deterministic exporter, event-time schema, the shared bootstrap library, the actor-access policy — all seven have since landed at the platform layer, so sibling queries inherit the fix instead of reimplementing it.
  3. Programme impact The hardened v2's building blocks — the pair-method split, canonical cache key, seeded statistics, and actor-governance pattern — were reused by the platform's later cohort-analytics questions rather than reimplemented per query. The same adversarial review discipline is documented end-to-end in the precedent-search case study.

11 Delivered

What was delivered — and where it transfers

  • A production deployment — the latency-analytics service live on the platform's self-hosted, in-VPC stack, serving governed operations dashboards. Shipped after executive sign-off on the design.
  • A complete design packet — the pairing spec with its versioned decision-type allowlist and deterministic pair identity, the four-timestamp time-semantics contract, a pre-registered statistical plan (seeded cluster-aware bootstrap for median and P90, censoring-aware estimation, versioned business-hours policy), API envelopes with full provenance pins, the RBAC/audit governance spec, freshness SLAs, and the fifteen-criteria evaluation contract.
  • The template for a family — this system defined the reusable pattern set — pair-method split, canonical cache keys, shared statistics, actor governance — that five sibling cohort questions on the platform were then built from.
  • A review audit trail — an eighteen-finding adversarial design review and its absorption into the v2, with every platform-level item tracked by id to a shared component.

The pattern generalises well beyond healthcare: any turnaround-time question where the event that starts the clock and the event that stops it must be paired honestly — support-ticket response SLAs, incident detection-to-mitigation, underwriting queue latency, order-to-fulfilment. If dropping the unanswered cases would flatter your number, you need a design where the denominator is ungated, the pairing is versioned, and the weak matches can inform but never headline — that is the discipline I bring.
