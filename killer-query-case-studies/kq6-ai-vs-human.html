Case study · ML system design · Healthcare

When the AI recommended one thing and a clinician chose another — what actually happened?

For a chronic-care platform covering 80+ conditions, I designed the analytics that grades AI recommendations against the decisions clinicians actually made — the highest-risk question in the product, because a careless version of it becomes a doctor scorecard. Every number is computed by deterministic, seeded, replayable code behind statistical gates, and no language model touches the result at all. And the design did something rarer: rather than fake the comparison from decision records, it stayed dark until an upstream team shipped the audit trail that makes it honest — the immutable record of what the AI suggested and whether a human took it. That contract shipped; the comparison now runs behind its balance and sensitivity gates. The same discipline transfers anywhere a machine's recommendation is graded against human judgment — underwriting, credit adjudication, triage, content moderation.

01 The problem

The most dangerous GROUP BY in the product

Ask the question naively — "when the AI was overruled, who was right?" — and you have built a performance ranking of your clinicians on your most sensitive cases. It is wrong before it is even computed, for two structural reasons.

  • Endogenous selection. Clinicians do not reject AI suggestions at random. They reject on their most unusual, highest-stakes, hardest-to-read cases — which means a raw rejected-versus-accepted outcome comparison is confounded by design. The two arms differ precisely in the cases where the decision was hardest, so any naive delta measures the case mix, not the judgment.
  • Scorecard misuse. The instant a per-clinician AI-agreement table exists, it reads as a league table. Even a statistically immaculate number ends careers if it is presented as a ranking rather than an observation.

And this query carried a third problem the others did not: the data needed to define the comparison did not exist yet. There was no trustworthy record of what the AI suggested, whether a human accepted or rejected it, and what they chose instead. So the design treated all three as first-class requirements with named controls — and, uniquely in this programme, refused to serve a single delta until that record existed. It does now, and the comparison runs.

02 The prerequisite it refused to fake

The design that would not manufacture the comparison

This is the part I am proudest of, and it was the opposite of shipping fast. A comparison is only as honest as its inputs, and the inputs were missing. You cannot recover "AI suggested X, the human chose Y" from decision records alone — you would be inferring a rejection from the fact that the chosen option differs from some suggested option — a heuristic that misfires in at least five distinct ways, catalogued below where the arm is assigned.

So instead of manufacturing a number, the design specified a formal producer/consumer event contract and handed it to the upstream team that owns the next-decision UI. That diagram draws the contract as a static ownership boundary — who owns which event. What it cannot show is the order that turned the boundary from a document into a live feed. The handshake below is that sequence over time: the consumer holds dark while the producer implements and emits, two proofs make the two arms genuinely comparable, and a single verification step flips the gate open.

The verification step is not a formality. Without it the comparison could have quietly launched on a half-built feed — rejections that were never emitted would have read as acceptances, and the delta would have measured a gap in the logs rather than a difference in outcomes.

Until that contract was signed, the query was unbuildable — and the design said so out loud rather than emit a delta it could not stand behind. That is why the structured design review returned a verdict of block-on-prereq: not a gap in the analytics, but a two-party contract that had to close first. It did, and everything downstream in this case study — the hardened design that armed on signing — is now live.

03 The architecture

Snapshot-only analytics, gated at the top

The pipeline never touches the live graph at query time. A nightly snapshot feeds a cohort builder that reconstructs each decision as of the moment it happened by replaying the append-only event spine; a shared propensity matcher pairs each rejected-suggestion decision with the most similar accepted-suggestion decision; everything downstream is pre-computed into columnar metric tables served by an embedded analytical engine. A single node sits above the whole pipeline as a standing gate — no snapshot, cohort, match, or metric is built for a tenant whose upstream audit trail is absent or unverified.

04 Hydration

Nine years of history, replayed into the spine

This comparison was not built against an empty world. A private production system had been recording these accept-and-reject decisions for roughly nine years, and that history is the actual source of one entire side of the comparison. Before any arm was graded, the full nine-year corpus was replayed through the append-only event spine by a first-class, proof-carrying hydration pipeline — not a throwaway backfill — so the analytics inherit real matured history instead of starting from load day.

Five properties are what make a nine-year replay trustworthy enough to grade one arm against the other:

  • Event time is not ingestion time. Each legacy record keeps the real-world moment it happened, with a separate observed stamp for when the source first resulted it; only the received and written stamps are pinned to the load, marking when the system ingested and stored the row. As-of reconstruction always orders events by that real-world time and its source sequence — never by the order they happened to arrive. Collapse event time into load time and nine years of decisions would look as though they all occurred on load day, and every as-of reconstruction behind the comparison would be void. The watermark runs in a distinct hydration mode that expects a nine-year spread of arrivals, where live mode expects minutes.
  • Hydrating twice is a no-op. Content-addressed event IDs and conditional writes make the load idempotent and resumable; re-running it over the same corpus yields a byte-identical spine and an identical snapshot-manifest hash. That equality is an executable proof, not an assurance.
  • Nothing is silently coerced. Nine years means many legacy payload-schema versions; each gets an upconverter, and any row that cannot be mapped to a schema or to a tenant is quarantined with a reason rather than defaulted — a defaulted tenant would be a cross-tenant leak, and a guessed arm would be a fabricated comparison.
  • The same projection code runs for history and for live. History is replayed through the exact projection logic that serves the live stream; otherwise the hydrated arm would be a different product from the streaming one and every determinism guarantee would be theatre.
  • The load is proof-carrying. Zero-event-loss reconciliation against the source, per-tenant and per-class counts, as-of spot checks against known-good historical reports, and a golden replay over a historical slice all gate the load before anything downstream is built. A fenced cutover then attaches the live stream with a proof of neither gap nor duplicate at the boundary.

The first of those five properties is worth drawing on its own, because every as-of reconstruction in this comparison rests on it. Each event carries four timestamps, and they do not mean the same thing: two are the real-world truth carried by the legacy record, and two are bookkeeping pinned to the moment of the load. Only the real-world pair is ever allowed to order the timeline.

Collapse those four fields into one — the mistake a throwaway backfill makes — and every historical decision inherits the load date. The human arm of this comparison would then be graded on covariates and a confidence bucket that did not exist when the clinician actually decided. The event_time < received_at guard on every row is the cheap assertion that catches exactly that class of mistake.

What nine years buys this query is the historical arm of the comparison itself. Grading AI suggestions against the decisions clinicians actually made is only meaningful if there are years of those clinician decisions to grade against — hydration lands them already matured and already carrying their 90-day outcome edges, so each confidence bucket holds enough accepted-and-rejected pairs to clear its power floor rather than being suppressed. The audited AI-suggestion events, which could only begin flowing once the upstream event contract shipped, are then compared against that deep replayed history instead of against a thin slice of recent traffic.

05 The core mechanism

Which arm you are in comes from the audit event, not a guess

The naive way to label the two arms is payload equality: call it a rejection whenever the chosen option differs from the suggested option. The review flagged that this misfires five ways — partial edits, schema drift, option up-conversions across schema versions, multiple competing suggestions, and rejections that were never logged all masquerade as one another. So the design assigns the arm from the immutable accept/reject audit event itself — the event that recorded, at decision time, that a human accepted or rejected the suggestion. Payload equality is kept only as a logged validation flag; it never becomes the label.

Two more leakage traps are closed here. When several suggestions attach to one decision, pairing is sorted deterministically by suggestion-then-audit sequence, so the same inputs always yield the same pair. And every pre-treatment covariate — plus the AI's own confidence — is reconstructed as of the decision's event time from the spine; the confidence bucket is the bucket that was emitted at suggestion time, never recomputed after the fact. Live reads against the current graph for a historical decision are forbidden.

What produces the arm label is not a table lookup but an exchange — one that happens nightly, in the cohort builder's conversation with the spine. The load-bearing move is that the arm is read from the immutable accept-or-reject event, then the covariates are reconstructed as of the same instant, before any outcome is looked at.

If arm assignment instead trusted payload equality, this exchange would collapse the five failure modes above into a single silent mislabel — and no downstream gate could recover the truth, because the cohort it balances and tests would already be wrong.

06 The gate ladder

An API built to refuse

Before any cell may show a headline delta, it climbs a ladder of deterministic gates — and the reasoning is not a prompt, it is seeded, replayable code. Every failure exit is explicit: a suppressed cell with a stated reason and full denominators, never a confident number built on sand and never a silent gap.

A control inherited from the sibling override-analytics service and shared across the programme does the load-bearing work here: the output contract's validator raises an exception if an outcome delta is attached to a cell whose gates failed. Within the bounds the schema enforces, a gate-failing number is not merely discouraged — it is unrepresentable. Two rules do the heavy lifting alongside it. Deltas are computed per confidence bucket only, never pooled into a single headline — pooling would hide exactly the confidence interaction that matters. And an imbalanced or fragile cell is never quietly downgraded to a raw rate; it renders diagnostics only.

That branch tree is the decision logic; the read that a reviewer actually waits on is short, because the cell was pre-computed overnight. The sequence below is a warm request for the high-confidence delta — the one cell that carries a hard cross-query dependency. Note the order: the audit entry is written before any actor-level split is served, and the calibration artifact from the sibling query is fetched and checked at read time, so a lapsed calibration suppresses the number even if the cell is cached.

Were the calibration check done only at build time, a model whose calibration drifted after the nightly build could keep serving a confident-looking delta from cache until the next rebuild. Re-checking on the read closes that window.

07 Guardrails

Every way this dashboard could mislead has a named control

What could go wrongThe control
Endogenous selection Clinicians reject on their hardest cases, so each rejected-suggestion decision is compared only against a propensity-matched control on pre-treatment and AI-confidence covariates — never endogenous ones. Matching can only balance what is measured; unmeasured confounding is never ruled out, which is exactly why every surface stays observational rather than causal.
A finding that is just selection bias Every reject-cohort delta must survive a five-test sensitivity battery — Rosenbaum bounds at gamma ≥ 1.5, an E-value, a negative-control outcome, leave-one-clinician-out, and a reject-concentration check. Any flag, or a standardised-mean-difference above 0.1, suppresses the causal-language delta and renders diagnostics only.
Miscalibrated confidence The high-confidence cell will not render unless a sibling calibration query has a passing artifact for the same model version and snapshot — a hard cross-query gate, not a nice-to-have. No passing artifact, no high-confidence delta.
False confidence from small or lopsided cells Minimum 30 treated and 30 control per cell, or it reports insufficient data with full denominators; if a single clinician makes up more than 30% of a reject cohort the cell is tagged concentrated and excluded from generalized conclusions.
Scorecard misuse The design states it plainly: this is not a clinician scorecard. Neutral codes in the data layer — no "who was right" label; pseudonymous clinician ids; alphabetic, never-rankable ordering; role-based views; an audit entry for every actor-level look. A negative reject-delta does not mean the clinician was wrong, and the copy is written and governed against that reading. These controls make the scorecard reading hard, logged and against the grain — not impossible.
A self-reinforcing feedback loop A drift monitor watches accept-rate, reject-rate, case-mix, the confidence distribution, the calibration, and the deltas themselves — segmented before versus after the dashboard was exposed, so the system can see if it is quietly training clinicians to defer.
Hindsight leaking in As-of reconstruction from the spine; the emitted confidence bucket, never recomputed; and a long-lag exclusion — decisions made more than 72 hours after the suggestion are kept out of the headline and shown only in a sensitivity slice, since a three-day gap is a different decision.
Stale snapshots and ignored corrections Freshness tiers keyed to snapshot age — 24h, 24–48h, and past 48h — degrade the surface progressively, from a staleness warning to withholding the numbers rather than serving quietly outdated statistics. A correction listener maps each spine correction, including audit-event corrections, to the slices that depend on it and rebuilds just those: P95 under five minutes.

The second row above — "a finding that is just selection bias" — is the load-bearing one, and the query path compresses it into a single sensitivity node. This is the inside of that node: an ordered ladder — the balance gate, then the five sensitivity tests — where a candidate delta must survive every rung. Almost every failure routes to the same exit, drawn dotted: suppress the causal-language number and show diagnostics only. A concentrated cohort is the one exception — it is tagged and held back from any general claim rather than suppressed outright. The exits point one way on purpose, because a false emit costs far more here than a false suppress.

Drop any one rung and a different failure mode walks through: without Rosenbaum bounds an unmeasured confounder passes unremarked; without leave-one-clinician-out a single prolific clinician's case-mix becomes a population claim. The ladder exists because no single test covers them all.

Scoping caveat · how to read the human arm

A reader will assume the human arm is a clinician deciding from memory. It is not. The decision is taken with two things side by side, by design — and at the same moment the accept-or-reject event above is written: the AI's suggested option, carrying the confidence bucket that was emitted with it, and a panel of ranked comparable past decisions and what became of them. That panel is evidence, not a second suggestion. So this comparison does not weigh a machine against unaided human judgment; it weighs it against a human reading evidence — which is what makes a rejection an informed one rather than a gut call, and also a narrower claim than the word human carries on its own.

Nor is the arm homogeneous. That panel comes from a sibling query, and it is itself gated: where a case is too thin to answer honestly — no comparable decision history to rank — it declares insufficient comparable precedent rather than manufacture an evidence panel, and the human decides on the suggestion alone. The arm can therefore hold decisions taken with that evidence beside decisions taken without it, and the three audit events this comparison depends on carry no field recording which.

That bounds what the delta may be read to mean: it compares a suggestion against a decision taken under a mixture of evidence conditions the cohort cannot split on, because the audit trail does not record them. The caveat belongs beside endogenous selection rather than behind it — one more reason every surface here stays observational, and one more field the contract would have to carry before the comparison could be cut any finer.

08 The LLM boundary

The language model never touches a number

This is a deliberate case of knowing when not to use an LLM. Arm assignment, matching, balance and sensitivity gates, suppression, ordering, and access control are all deterministic, seeded, replayable code, and the comparison makes zero model calls: every delta a reader sees was computed, gated, and — where support runs thin — suppressed by that code, never written or reworded by a model. The platform does run a self-hosted Llama 3.1 seam inside the VPC for the surfaces that genuinely narrate, the graph-traversal and orchestration queries, and this comparison deliberately opens none of it. There is no retrieval-augmented-generation surface here either, and that absence is a judgment rather than a gap: the deliverable is a propensity-matched, per-confidence-bucket delta the code has already computed and gated, so there is nothing further to retrieve that would make the comparison more true — and a document-fetching narrator could only tempt the reader back toward the who-was-right verdict the whole design refuses to produce.

  • No prompt to jailbreak: the neutrality rules, suppression logic, and access controls are code and configuration, not instructions to a model — they cannot drift with a model update, because there is no model in the loop.
  • Nothing to hallucinate: every delta on the surface is computed and version-pinned, and no generated sentence ever carries a claim.
  • The seam stays shut: the platform's model door is one import away for a sibling narrative query. The discipline is choosing, deliberately, not to open it on a comparison that must never read as a verdict.
09 The stack

Boring infrastructure, deliberate framework choices

The analytical core is deliberately unexciting — columnar files and an embedded query engine, each doing one job. The AI layer reuses the platform's LangChain seam and LangGraph runtime, with observability designed for PHI from the start.

The two tables below say what each component is; the picture first says when each one is touched and where the trust boundary sits. Every component falls into one of three lanes: the request path a reviewer waits on, the nightly build that runs on a schedule, and the one-time historical hydration that seeded the spine. All of it runs inside the client VPC, and the two components most people assume are in the hot path — the decision graph and the model seam — are drawn where they actually sit: off it.

11 The review gauntlet

The review that said: do not build this yet

Before anything shipped, the design went through structured adversarial self-review under a Principal-AI-Engineer lens — and this one returned the hardest verdict of the set: block-on-prereq. Not "ship with changes," not "redesign" — "you do not yet have the data to answer this honestly, so do not build it." That prerequisite has since been met: the upstream contract shipped, and the query went live behind its gates.

  1. Design review · round 1 Verdict block-on-prereq. The blocking finding: the AI-suggestion audit trail did not exist — no producer, schema, or implementation — so the comparison had nothing trustworthy to stand on. Alongside it, 13 local findings across correctness, statistics, and governance.
  2. Design revision · v2 All 13 local findings absorbed and armed: arm assignment moved off payload equality onto the immutable audit event; deterministic pairing and as-of reconstruction; the five-test sensitivity battery for endogenous selection; the hard confidence-calibration cross-check; actor-RBAC with pseudonymity; and per-bucket-only aggregation with no pooled headline. Each armed the moment the contract landed.
  3. The decisive move Rather than fake the comparison, the design formalised the missing inputs as a two-party event contract for the upstream team to sign — three audit events, a schema-parity proof, and a correction/replay contract. Where the sibling reviews minted new shared standards, this one confirmed and extended the programme's governance, statistics, and cache-key conventions without adding a new cross-query finding.

12 Delivered

What was delivered — and where it transfers

  • A production cohort-analytics service — live on the platform's self-hosted, in-VPC stack, the same service that powers the sibling override-analytics dashboards. This specific comparison stayed dark until its upstream audit-event contract shipped and could be verified — a production system built to know which question it was not yet entitled to answer, and to serve it only once it was.
  • A complete design packet — the gated pipeline, the arm-assignment spec (immutable audit event, not payload equality), as-of reconstruction, the pre-registered statistical plan (balance gate, five-test sensitivity battery, power floor, seeded cluster-aware bootstrap), the API envelope with full provenance pins, the RBAC/audit governance spec, freshness SLAs, and the per-bucket-only, no-pooled-headline rule.
  • An event-contract handoff — a formal producer/consumer contract for the upstream product team: three audit events, a schema-parity proof, and a correction/replay contract. It was the single deliverable that unblocked the query — defined rather than assumed, and the design would not proceed without it.
  • A review audit trail — the Principal-AI-Engineer design review (verdict: block-on-prereq), its 13 dispositioned local findings absorbed into the hardened v2, and the shared-backbone dependencies it confirmed.

The pattern generalises well beyond healthcare: any system that grades a machine's recommendation against a human's decision — underwriting exceptions, credit adjudication, triage, content-moderation appeals. If you need honest numbers about who deviated and whether it worked, with the statistics gated, the access governed, an LLM kept away from the arithmetic, and the integrity to refuse the comparison until the data can support it — this is the discipline I bring.
